# A Companion to Svellheim

## The turning year

The year begins with Harpa, traditionally the first month of summer. Daylight still lengthens through spring. The thaw has failed for three successive years.

A year has 360 days. Each month has thirty, and each half of the year has six months. A date followed by **A.S.** gives the year After Settlement. The count begins with the founding of the earliest settlements. The keepers of the law agreed on that count long after the event. Máni, the moon, takes about twenty-nine and a half days to turn from new to new. A pilot who sailed by a full moon last month looks for it a little earlier this time.

| Day of year | Sumar · summer | Day of year | Vetr · winter |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1–30 | Harpa | 181–210 | Gormánuður |
| 31–60 | Skerpla | 211–240 | Ylir |
| 61–90 | Sólmánuður | 241–270 | Mörsugur |
| 91–120 | Heyannir | 271–300 | Þorri |
| 121–150 | Tvímánuður | 301–330 | Góa |
| 151–180 | Haustmánuður | 331–360 | Einmánuður |

**Sumar** is the summer half of the year. The first day of summer, Sumarmál, falls on the first of Harpa. A farming family once celebrated by planting the first seed. In Skerpla they sharpened their tools. By Sólmánuður there was daylight enough for a long journey, and neighbors who needed a judgment could travel together to the summer assembly. Heyannir was the hay harvest. At Tvímánuður debts fell due while there was still time to lay in stores. The last three days of Haustmánuður are Winter Nights, or Vetrnætr. Families remember their dead and light the household fire anew.

**Vetr**, the winter half of the year, begins in Gormánuður. A farmer who put up hay in summer now knew how many animals could live on it. The rest were slaughtered and the meat salted against the months ahead. Yule, the festival of midwinter fires and gifts, falls in Ylir. Even now families contrive to keep a present hidden for that evening. By Mörsugur the stores of fat run low. In Þorri, the month named for a frost giant, families bring out preserved food for a feast called Þorrablót. They hope to appease the giant and hasten the end of winter. Góa once brought the first birds back. In Einmánuður people enter outstanding debts and prepare their oaths for the coming year.

**The week.** A traveler reaching town on Sunnudagr, the day of the sun, finds the market closed. Work resumes on Mánadagr, the moon's day. Courts sit on Týsdagr, named for Tyr, the god of law. Óðinsdagr belongs to seers and skalds, and on Þórsdagr neighbors turn out for heavy repairs. Frjádagr is favored for marriages. Laugardagr means washing day. With clean linen hung indoors to dry, the week begins again.

## The ages in living memory and song

The first three years of Fimbulwinter are counted from the Long Night. Earlier ages are preserved in sequence through assemblies, records of the halls and oral tradition. Their lengths are often uncertain.

| Reckoning | What passed from one age to the next |
| --- | --- |
| Before the Roots | Families carry their beginnings in sacred stories. |
| Age of Roots | The World Tree, Yggdrasil, takes root. Most written histories begin here. |
| Walled kingdoms | Reedbank shelters people who have returned from the grave. The other shore opposes it. Their allies join the War of the Dead. |
| Between the wars | Cities guarantee warmth to anyone at a flame spring. Vargeld exploits a dispute over who controls the roadside shrines and takes the mountain passes. |
| Second War | The allied armies defeat Vargeld at the mountain pass called Titan’s Stair. Survivors rebuild the kingdoms together. |
| Age of High Kings | At Thingvellir the rulers agree to share roads and defense. They name Harald High King. Trade flourishes in the Silver Era. |
| Long Night | On the longest night the moon vanishes. For three days people cannot kindle a fire. |
| First winter | Livestock die and food runs short. Some villages turn away hungry families. |
| Second winter | Raids for fuel burn the forest called the Greenweald. Its ruins become the Ashen Woods. |
| Third winter · today | Harald holds Hrafnborg. Fuel and food still move along the surviving roads and waterways. |

## The speech of home

Svellspraak is the common language of trade, schooling and public records. Most children also learn a regional dialect. The word mål means speech, and a person’s mål usually identifies the place they grew up. Fjordmål, for example, is the speech of the Splintered Fjords, shared by harbor families of several ancestries.

On the Weeping Coast, Gråtstrandsmål gives a pilot the words to describe a wreck to someone who must find it in fog. Across the water one hears Sangøymål, the lilting speech of the Singing Islands. Inland, the bog workers speak Myrmål. Their neighbors in the Ashen Woods give watch calls in Askeskogsmål. Up on the mountain passes, a guide speaking Skjoldfjellsmål can describe loose stone in words borrowed from the Deepforged miners below. On the eastern plateau, families who travel the Bone-Fields speak Beinmarksmål. Among the phrases a visitor learns first are the courtesies for asking a place at someone else’s fire.

Farther north, a wrong word about the direction of the wind can cost a search party a day. Hvitviddemål, the speech of the White Waste, is exact on that subject. In the volcanic ridges called the Throat of the World, Strupemål gives people words for the steam and hot ground they may encounter on an otherwise frozen path. Travelers also tell of Sommerdalsmål, the speech of Vanaheim's Joy. Few who repeat the name claim to have heard it themselves.

A home tongue may also survive in a craft or a ceremony. A Deepforged mason can put the terms of a contract into Dvergsmål. Tonttumál carries quietly between workers in woodland. The formal addresses of Ljósamál survive among Lumenkin lore-keepers. Cragbound families use Jötuntunga to call across a ravine, and smiths of several peoples have borrowed words from Askmál, the ancestral language of the Ashmarked. An Ormsbani heir reciting a lineage in Ormstunga can leave even a fluent speaker of Svellspraak waiting for an interpreter. A Barrowbound who comes home still knows the speech in which a sister once called them in to eat.

## Where worshippers go

The horn of Hallinskidi identifies the god honored by the eastern beacon keepers. A light maintained for strangers recalls the passage he kept open for the ancestors of the Lumenkin. Njoror’s principal coastal shrine stands above Strandvik. The albatross feeds undisturbed there as a bird sacred to the sea god.

At Hrafnborg and Thornwall the iron-bound temple posts tell a traveler where to seek Thunar's protection. The nail in a threshold is his familiar sign. Frigg's worship at Brimstead has its center in a working lighthouse. A family may offer the goddess a lock of hair woven into cloth. At the lighthouse, weavers work the names of drowned sailors into a tapestry. They can show a visiting child the very place where her father’s name appears.

Some signs belong to an observance. Enemies making peace break a blade for Tyr, god of oaths. Skalds pour mead into the hearth for Bragi, who protects those who carry news. On the upper Ormá the final standing sheaf marks the harvest ground dedicated to Freyr.

## The worlds of the ash

In the oldest account the earth beneath a child's feet was once flesh. The god Grimnir and his brothers slew Ymir, the first giant, where the ice of **Niflheim** met the heat of **Muspelheim**, across the gulf called **Ginnungagap**. They raised the land from his body. A child brought up in the high ranges may hear the same deed told with grief for the giant. Both tellings give the mountains a history older than any king.

The skalds call the mortal world **Midgard**. Svellheim lies within it. The ash Yggdrasil joins Midgard to eight other realms. Above it stands **Asgard**, the citadel of the gods, reached in song by the bridge Bifrost. **Vanaheim** is the green home of the Vanir. **Alfheim** is the bright weald remembered in tales of the Lumenkin. A sailor hearing of a green trading land across the southern water has good reason to ask whether the teller means a foreign coast or the country of a god.

The giants have their country in **Jotunheim**. The old songs place the forges of **Svartalfheim** beneath the earth. A Deepforged smith who has worked a lifetime underground can understand why a storyteller might imagine a whole world lit by forge fires. Farther from the light of living hearths lies **Helheim**, where Hel receives the dead. The Barrowbound who return remember the choice Hel offered them. They could remain in her house and suffer, or go home to keep a promise they themselves had named. Those who have kept the last of their oaths have left no agreed account of what follows.

Niflheim and Muspelheim complete the nine. In a winter that has outlasted two summers it is easy to understand why a hearer might blame the realm of first ice. Others remember that the first flame came out of the same meeting. Even those who agree on the making of the world disagree on what has happened to it.
