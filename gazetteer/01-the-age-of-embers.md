# The Age of Embers

Three years have passed since the winter solstice when the moon vanished and the hearths of Svellheim went cold. Fire returned after three days. Summer has yet to return. This is Fimbulwinter, the long cold that ended the Silver Era. In Harpa, the traditional planting month, the soil is still frozen beneath the surface. Sólmánuður still brings the longest daylight of the year, but many of the roads that once carried delegates to the summer assembly are impassable.

## Who shares the fire

Most towns have been home to families of several ancestries since the rebuilding after the Second War. The people who share a harbor depend on one another for the catch, the work of preserving it, and the watches that keep the boats safe. A poor catch strains relations even between close neighbors. The local public assembly, called the Thing, can hear witnesses to a promise of labor, provisions or shelter.

Three familiar terms describe what a household can offer. **Heat** includes the food and fuel that sustain life. **Weight** is the value of usable goods, such as iron fit to trade for barley. A household’s **Word** is the standing its members gain by keeping their promises. Where fuel is rationed, a respected neighbor willing to answer for a guest may secure lodging that silver alone cannot buy.

Flame springs sustain the largest settlements. At these sites a living fire called an Ember rises naturally from the earth. Ember Keepers care for the fire and maintain the public right of access to its warmth. On the old roads, Ember Tenders keep smaller Embers alight in wayside shelters. The network predates the surviving records. Some shrine roads still connect villages. Others continue beyond the last occupied house.

## The southern routes

Before the southern sea lanes froze, the inland harvest passed through Ormsund to ports abroad. Customs records and surviving pilots preserve the names of trading partners. The wrecks off the Weeping Coast supply timber and fittings to settlements that once earned their living from those voyages.

Oral traditions and secret teachings also describe a green southern land whose people traded with Svellheim. Its relationship to the ports named in the records remains uncertain.

## The gods and their people

The gods seldom intervene in the affairs of kingdoms. A few people have encountered one directly, and many more claim such a meeting. Particular devotions often arise from these experiences. Oil given for a headland beacon honors Hallinskidi, the god who guarded the passage of the Lumenkin into this world. Shelter offered during a storm honors Thunar. Families can disagree over whether a god appeared and still keep the light or open the hall.
