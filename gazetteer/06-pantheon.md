# The Gods of Svellheim

Temples, working harbors and private hearths all serve as places of worship. At Brimstead the keepers of the lighthouse weave the names of the drowned into a tapestry for Frigg. At Strandvik crews cast offerings into deep water before they sail. Such observances often concern the safety or memory of one person. Direct encounters with gods are rare and the claims numerous. A worshipper’s account may shape the practice of a whole household without persuading the neighbors.

## The Aesir

The Aesir are the divine family led by Grimnir. Their stories recount the making and defense of Midgard, the world of mortals. Their worship has a public place in the timber halls where towns hold assemblies, offer sanctuary and hear oaths. Even the gods are subject to **Wyrd**, the fate shaped by past deeds and binding promises.

### Grimnir, the All-Father

Grimnir and his brothers made the land from the body of Ymir, the first giant. Grimnir gave an eye for wisdom and hung upon a tree to learn the runes, signs through which a carver gives words lasting power. Rune readers offer a drop of blood pressed into carved wood. Kings seek his counsel in war. Ravens gathering on a hall roof are counted among the signs of the Masked One, another of his names.

### Thunar, the Thunderer

Thunar protects households in storms and forbids refusing aid to a neighbor in danger. His temple at Hrafnborg shelters families whose roofs have fallen. At Thornwall his timber temple doubles as the mustering place for coastal defense. Iron binds the great posts, and a nail driven into a threshold is a familiar offering.

### Tyr, the Hand Given

Tyr lost a hand when the Aesir bound the great beasts beneath the roots of the World Tree. He is the god of law and of the obligations people undertake in their own names. An assembly may witness an oath, but only the person assuming the duty can swear it. The Ormsbani observe this rule when appointing a keeper for a river crossing. Former enemies honor Tyr by breaking a weapon when they agree to peace.

### Hallinskidi, the Watchman

The Lumenkin credit Hallinskidi with holding open the passage by which their ancestors reached Svellheim. Beacon keepers on the eastern headlands burn lamp oil in his honor for foreign and local crews alike. His sign is the warning horn. Gate guards who serve him must challenge arrivals and may never demand payment for safe passage.

### Frigg, the Hearth-Mother

The fjord peoples honor Frigg for the fire she entrusted to the first human child. The custom of sending a child with coals to the next household accompanies the sharing of winter food and lodging. Frigg also keeps the names of the dead. Bread set beside a hearth and a lock of hair woven into cloth are customary offerings. The memorial tapestry at Brimstead is a center of her worship on the Weeping Coast.

### Bragi, the Mead-Bonded

Bragi protects skalds, the poets who preserve public memory, and messengers who carry unwelcome news. His worship gave bereaved families a witness to the lives of people who died far from home in the great wars. An original verse or mead poured into the fire honors him. A boast made over his cup is remembered as a public undertaking.

### Forseti, the Arbiter

Forseti, son of Balder, governs truces and hearings between enemies. Drawing steel in a sanctuary or breaking an accepted truce offends him. Former enemies share bread to mark an agreement made under his protection. The Cinderbound trace their insistence on hearing every party in a familiar language to Forseti’s judgment at the spring, where he required the giant Surt to heed the limits of Una’s request.

## The Vanir

Families turn to the Vanir for a harvest, a fair wind, safe hunting and the older arts of divination and enchantment called seidr. The peace that ended their war with the Aesir obliged some Vanir to live in Asgard, the citadel of their former enemies. In Svellheim their sanctuaries are often springs, groves and stones at the edge of cultivated ground.

### Mardöll, the Lady of the Field

Mardöll receives half the battle-slain and hears lovers’ vows. Her worship requires bereaved households to acknowledge the partners of the dead at the burial meal. Jewelry buried in earth is an offering to her. Cats at a threshold and gold that stays warm in the hand are among her signs. Cream may be offered in place of jewelry.

### Njoror, the Sea-King

Coastal households ask Njoror for wind, thaw and a safe harbor. His shrine stands on the cliff above Strandvik, visible to departing crews. Worshippers cast gold into water too deep for its recovery. The albatross is sacred to him, whistling aboard ship is discouraged, and gulls far inland may herald a break in the coastal ice.

### Freyr, Lord of the Stack

Freyr’s title refers to the gathered harvest. The last sheaf belongs to him, and reapers leave their weapons outside the field. The custom recalls the god who gave up his sword for love. Honey poured over a standing stone honors him, and the stag is sacred to him. Villages along the upper Ormá still gather on the day of the last sheaf, although their fields no longer yield grain.

### Skadi, the Snow-Shoe Goddess

Born among the giants, Skadi married into the gods. The Cragbound honor her as the guide of their first ancestors, and the Ashmarked as the goddess who helped their hunters bring fire and meat home. Search parties leave frozen meat on the heights and give her wolves room at a kill. As Lady of Vengeance she also receives oaths to remember deaths for which no one has answered.

### Idunn, Keeper of Apples

Idunn tends the fruit that preserves the gods’ youth. In Tonttu tradition her apple brought the first children out from under the roots at a spring in the Greenweald. Surviving communities honor her by planting seed in ash, protecting children and sparing live wood from the fire. Cuttings carried from the burned forest preserve trees once tended by villages along the Askfoss.

### Ullr, the Glory-Hunter

Ullr rules during Grimnir’s absence. Hunters honor him for skill with a bow. People called to single combat ask him to witness their agreement on weapons and ground. A ring hung from a yew branch is a customary offering.

## The Ascended

The Ascended were mortals whose deeds earned them lasting devotion. They are usually known by a craft or a title. A pilot can honor Njoror for the wind and ask the Mariner for the judgment to sail by it.

### The Shield-Maiden and the Bear-Sark

Thornwall’s ruling house claims descent from the Shield-Maiden. Her worship honors the defender who holds a position until the wounded can be removed. Soldiers facing a charge call on the Bear-Sark for courage and battle fury. Their households remember the duty of companions to bring a fighter safely home when the battle is over.

### The Law-Speaker and the Staff-Carrier

Lawspeakers preserve judgments by reciting them accurately across generations. They honor the Law-Speaker as one of their own, remembered for keeping faith with those named in a judgment. The Staff-Carrier, or Volva, receives the prayers of seers, wandering prophets and people cast out of their own halls. Giving such a traveler shelter is part of her worship.

### Volund the Smith

Volund is honored for fine workmanship and feared for his traps and vengeance. Locks in particular belong to his tradition. His stories preserve the divided obligations of makers whose skill can protect a household or serve a cruel lord.

### The Mariner and the Horse-Lord

The Mariner brought crews home by the stars. Pilots who remember the former southern routes still teach the night sky in his honor. The Horse-Lord protects travelers and their animals. His followers provide shelter for a mount and assign a watch over it even after the rider has reached safety.

## The powers people fear

The Outsiders belong to the Jötnar, the elder giant kindred. Some receive offerings from people hoping to avert their attention. Giant descent carries other religious meanings among the Cragbound, who mourn Ymir as a forebear and claim the goddess Skadi as kin.

### Hel, Queen of the Dead

The people of Cairn Ness lay a coin beneath the tongue of the dead, including strangers, so that Hel may receive them as guests. They call her the Hostess. Older temple verses assign her the dead judged unworthy of a place elsewhere. The testimony of the Barrowbound has complicated that teaching. Each describes a personal choice between suffering in her realm and returning to fulfill a promise. What follows its fulfillment remains uncertain.

### Surt, Lord of Muspelheim

Surt rules the realm of fire. The volcanic crater called Surt’s Forge bears his name. The songs associate him with the burning of the world, and some who desire that end call on him. No offering to Surt is held safe. The Cinderbound remember him at mourning tables because of his part in the deaths and deliverance at their ancestral spring.

### Ymir, the First Giant

The mountains of Midgard’s Shield are Ymir’s bones in the creation account shared by the giants and the worshippers of Grimnir. Cragbound families call the names of their dead into a gully, believing their oldest kinsman carries the voices back. A separate, sterner devotion to Ymir requires its adherents to give up lighting a fire, an observance of particular consequence during Fimbulwinter.
