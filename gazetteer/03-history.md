# A History of Svellheim

On Svellheim’s southern shore, the old trading ships lie trapped in the ice. Sailors call it the Weeping Coast. At low tide the wrecks stand close enough to walk from deck to deck. At the little port of Grátholm, the customs house once collected duties on the cargo those ships brought home. Now its people earn their living cutting sound timber and prying fittings from the wrecks. Farther inland, where the king's roads disappear under snow, the circular banks of old forts are still visible. The people who built these places expected ships to come back and roads to stay open.

Svellheim reckons years from the founding of its first settlements. The abbreviation A.S. means After Settlement. Generations later the skalds, who preserved history in verse, and the lawspeakers, who recited the law at public gatherings, agreed on a common count. None could put an exact year to the first settlement. The names of the ages are more widely known than the dates of their beginnings.

## Before the Age of Roots

Most of Svellheim's written history begins with the World Tree, Yggdrasil, taking root. For the time before it, the peoples of Svellheim turn chiefly to spoken tradition. Each has accounts of its beginnings, of the place it was given or made for itself, and of the gods to whom it owes particular obligations. The accounts survived the breaking of the old kingdoms. They are still told in the homelands and in settlements far beyond them. See [The Beginnings of the Peoples](05-ancestries.md#the-beginnings-of-the-peoples).

Those stories had consequences once kingdoms began to put boundaries around places older than their walls. When a dead boatwright came home to finish a job, her neighbors on one shore of a lake believed they had a duty to shelter her. On the other shore her own daughter believed that opening the door put every family’s dead in danger. Both had learned their duties at home, from people they trusted.

## The Age of Roots

The creation song begins with a gulf between two worlds. Out of Niflheim came ice. Heat reached it from Muspelheim, the world of fire. Where the frost began to melt, Ymir, the first giant, stood up. The god Grimnir and his brothers Vili and Vé killed him. From his body they made Midgard, the mortal world in which Svellheim lies. His bones became the mountains, his blood the sea. Yggdrasil put down roots through the realms. Even a village founded within living memory may have a spring its people believe was occupied long before they arrived. They leave gifts for the spirit who lives in its water.

Among the earliest powers the songs name are the Jötnar, Ymir's giant kin, and the Vanir, the gods to whom a grower might entrust the last seed of a failing crop. Grimnir's kindred, the Aesir, bound the great beasts beneath the roots. In the mountain homes of Ymir’s descendants, children learn that the ground beneath their feet was bought with their own ancestor’s life. Tyr, the god to whom people swear their oaths, lost a hand binding the beasts. In the peace that followed the war between the two families of gods, some of the Vanir had to leave their own country and go to live with the Aesir in their citadel, Asgard.

## The Flame Springs and Shrine Roads

In a few places in Svellheim, fire comes up out of the earth. In the fjords there is a cleft in the rock where a person can kneel, lay a wet coat on the stone, and feel the wool begin to dry. Nobody laid a hearth there. The fire was burning before the first houses went up. The people of Svellheim call such a place a **flame spring**, and the living fire that rises there an **Ember**. Families built close enough to walk to the warmth in bad weather. Hrafnborg began this way. Boats could land in the inlet below the fire. Fishers brought their catch ashore, traders came to buy it, and in time the path from the water to the spring became a city street. Looking after the cleft became someone’s daily work. The person who clears fallen rock from it and keeps people safe beside the fire is the **Ember Keeper**.

A traveler can also find a small Ember burning in a shelter beside an old road. People call these shelters shrines. The **Ember Tender** who cares for one keeps wind and rain off its fire and makes a dry place for the next person through the door. A traveler who has spent the afternoon in sleet may owe the use of both hands in the morning to the stranger who kept that Ember lit.

The surviving shrines follow roads whose original destinations and purposes are often lost. On some stretches a Tender can point out the next shrine. Elsewhere a road dwindles to a track and disappears in pasture. Ask who set the fires along it and a Tender may tell the story she learned from her grandfather. Across the mountains another family tells a different story about the same road. No record has settled who first traveled it, or where they were going.

## The Walled Kingdoms and the Great Wars

From the tops of their stone walls the early kings could look down on the fields that fed their households. Beyond them lay the roads by which their daughters went to marry, their herders took cattle to pasture, and their neighbors came when called upon to fight. Even in the years of the walled kingdoms, few families owed every duty to the ruler behind the nearest gate. At a lake below the central mountains, two promises made to people on opposite shores would bring those kingdoms to war.

### The quarrel at Urdvatn

The War of the Dead began at Lake Urdvatn, below the central mountain range called Midgard’s Shield. The fishing village of Reedbank had given a hall to the Barrowbound, people who had died and returned to fulfill a promise made to Hel, queen of the dead. Individuals had returned to their families before. At Reedbank they could settle together, including those whose families would refuse them. The villagers held that Frigg, goddess of the hearth, entitled any stranger to shelter.

Across the lake, Cairn Ness kept the burial ground for much of the district. Its people believed that a family entrusted the dead to Hel at burial. They also had a practical objection to Reedbank’s welcome. If a boatwright could return to finish work for which she had already been paid, other creditors might try to collect debts that had formerly ended at the grave. The western assembly eventually forbade the Returned passage from its landing. When Reedbank sent boats to fetch a man stranded there, both shores called on their allies.

The Deepforged miners of the mountains owed Cairn Ness help for its part in recovering their dead after an avalanche. The Ormsbani, who kept the river crossings on the upper Ormá, had promised boats to Reedbank in return for a rescue during a flood. Both answered. An arrow from an unknown archer killed the boy who tended the landing lamps. Reprisals cost the lake villages boats and lives. The fighting might still have ended at Urdvatn had the western king confined his claim to the men responsible. Instead he sent guards to occupy the Ormá ford through which the inland harvest passed.

### The War of the Dead

The guards killed the ford-keeper’s brother in the doorway of his hall. Under an old agreement, each neighboring river hall owed nine defenders to any hall attacked in its own home. The companies drove the guards away, then pursued them into the western kingdom. Once armed parties began burning farms across the border, kings on both sides summoned the households that held land from them. The passes, harbors and grain roads of Svellheim became military objectives. Even settlements with no prior allegiance were drawn in as armies took their livestock, seized ships and violated places of shelter. The giant dead among the bones of the eastern plateau date from this war.

Prolonged fighting gave Vargeld, a miller in the foothills of the Shield, control of the valley’s grain supply. With the help of goblin settlements beneath the mountains, he carried flour past the contested crossings and fed villages on both sides. He had lost two sons to the royal levy. Accounts of his rise agree that in the workings below his mill he found a means to hear their voices and make others hear them. He used that power against those who resisted him. Once his goblin and orc companies held the granaries, whole villages had to surrender children as hostages to obtain food. By the time his forces reached Urdvatn the miller was known throughout the valley as Vargeld Hollowking.

The fall of Cairn Ness brought its surviving households across the lake to Reedbank. With both populations threatened, the families that had called in the old oaths publicly acknowledged that their allies had discharged them. This released the river companies and the miners to serve alongside their former enemies. The kings exchanged prisoners and deferred claims for losses. While their combined forces held Vargeld’s army at the ford, miners and goblin guides brought the hostages out through the workings. The river flooded the chambers beneath the mill. Vargeld disappeared, his captains scattered, and the allies reopened the granaries.

Reedbank survived the peace as a settlement of the Barrowbound. Its inhabitants could hold property, earn a living and decide whether to resume an obligation from before their burial. Creditors and relatives alike had to obtain the consent of the person who had returned. The settlement established how the living at Urdvatn would treat their dead should they come home. What becomes of a Barrowbound who has fulfilled the promise to Hel remains unknown.

## The Years Between the Wars

The wars displaced families into towns built around flame springs, where fire rises naturally from the earth. At one fjord spring nearly all the warm ground came to be occupied by benches rented out for a fee. The town paid for the benches and opened them to everyone. Other towns adopted the ruling and charged their Ember Keepers with enforcing it. A ruler could levy taxes in the market, but even the ruler’s household had to give up a reserved place at the public fire.

Along the roads, villages supported Ember Tenders to care for the smaller fires in wayside shrines. Farms supplied food and trained successors to keep the shelters open. A traveler who left weapons at the door had the right to stay until morning, even in the company of an enemy. In years of poor harvest the towns at the springs sent grain to the shrines. Their carters received temporary access to the stores. In time, a coastal city could be feeding travelers several days’ journey beyond its own territory.

Vargeld had survived the flood. Over three generations he gathered an army beneath the Shield. To draw the mountain garrisons out of the passes, his agents bought up barley and paid for false records of deliveries. The apparent shortages forced the shrines to seek coastal grain. Lady Inga, ruler of the city bearing much of the expense, posted guards at the shrines her people supplied. Permission to draw grain soon became permission to enter the shelter at all. When a Tender admitted travelers without the city’s token, the guards arrested him. One of Vargeld’s agents killed the inland envoy sent to demand a hearing. The inland towns drove out the guards, Inga seized their grain at the river mouths, and the mountain defenders marched downriver to recover it. Vargeld’s army emerged behind them.

## The Second War

Forty thousand fighters came out of the mountains. At the main grain crossing of the Ormá, Vargeld attacked along the high road while a second force emerged from the workings behind the river settlements. Of the eight thousand mustered against him, fewer than three thousand returned. He destroyed the refuge at Reedbank, occupied the spring towns and made access to their fires conditional on supplying labor. By the second summer he held the roads on both sides of the Shield. Timber from the Greenweald supplied his siege works. The loss of the grain ports brought famine even to islands his armies never reached.

At Ormsund, the harbor at the mouth of the Ormá, the surviving rulers formed an alliance of all ten peoples. Inga returned the grain she had seized and put her ships at the allies’ disposal. The terms of service mattered as much as the size of the army. Allied stores would feed the families of those who served, and no king could recall a contingent merely because its own homeland had been freed. The alliance entrusted the coastal advance to Asa, a Barrowbound commander from the river trade. In two campaigning seasons her army moved up the Ormá while a second host approached from the north.

### The battle of Titan's Stair

Titan’s Stair is the pass where a flight of giant-cut steps crosses the Shield. Beneath it lay the galleries that supplied Vargeld’s army and offered it a line of retreat. Nearly thirty thousand allied troops gathered on the two approaches. On the first day Vargeld drove the western army back through Stairwatch and held the crest against the eastern advance. On the second, island crews reinforced the western climb while the eastern army fought its way up from the plateau. Deepforged and Cragbound companies went below to free the captive workers and collapse the gate behind them. Only eleven of the volunteers returned.

The two armies met at the crest and killed Vargeld among his guard. More than eight thousand of the alliance died in the battle or of their wounds that autumn. Enemy forces on the plateau surrendered or were slain, and the surviving bands withdrew underground. The alliance held together through the following winter to free the occupied towns and recover the last prisoners.

Many old kingdoms had lost their rulers, their draft animals and the records of who owned the land. The allied companies still had grain and rolls of the families entitled to it. They organized the first relief. Island fishers fed inland work parties, woodland growers supplied seed, and displaced families took up farms beside the people with whom they had served. At Reedbank, timber from Cairn Ness helped rebuild the refuge, which also housed the lists of the missing. Across Svellheim, the mixed settlements founded in those years outlasted the armies that had provisioned them.

## The Age of High Kings

The kingdoms that emerged from reconstruction followed rivers, harbors and mountain roads. Along the Ormá, river lords collected a share of the harvest to keep ferries operating and grain moving between valleys. In the Splintered Fjords, ports furnished ships to a common fleet. Between them the mountain kings maintained the passes on which both trades depended. The descendants of all the old peoples lived under these crowns. Restoring the ancestral borders would have divided families and farms established since the war.

By the reign of Harald of Hrafnborg, the cost of the mountain roads threatened that peace. The mountain rulers sought a share of the duties collected in the ports. The coastal kings refused, and the passes closed. Grain spoiled inland while the coast went hungry. By spring, river and coastal armies had converged on Titan’s Stair. Offered passage for Hrafnborg alone, Harald disclosed the offer to the river kings, withdrew his troops from the gate and pledged part of his own harbor revenue to maintain both approaches if food could pass freely. The mountain kings opened the gates and the armies dispersed.

At the summer assembly at Thingvellir the rulers made the arrangement permanent. The ports contributed duties and ships, the river kingdoms maintained the grain landings, and the mountain crowns accepted a common toll and gave up the right to close a pass over a private quarrel. Disputes between kingdoms went before witnesses from both sides. Even a claim for compensation for a killing could now cross a border without an armed pursuit. This body of common law became known as the **Danelaw**. The yearly gathering where such claims could be heard was the **Alþing**, or great assembly.

The rulers acclaimed Harald High King and gave him command of the common defense. Local rulers kept their courts, and Harald himself answered at the assembly for the revenues pledged to the roads. The settlement earned him the name **Unifier**. The circular forts called Trelleborgs, built to shelter travelers and supply escorts along the grain roads, still mark the reach of his peace.

### The Silver Era

For the first time since the great wars, an inland harvest could reach the sea under continuous protection. Southern merchants returned to the fjords, and the worked silver they brought in exchange for grain and other cargo reached far up the river valleys. This prosperity gave the Silver Era its name. In its last years, early frosts began to spoil the barley. Trade and loans carried settlements through individual crop failures while the sea lanes remained open.

## Fimbulwinter

The Silver Era ended at the winter solstice remembered as the **Long Night**. The moon turned red and vanished. For three days fires went out across Svellheim and could not be rekindled, an interval known as the **First Silence**. Flame returned, but the cold endured. Glaciers advanced, the southern trade routes froze and the dead rose in unprecedented numbers. This was the onset of Fimbulwinter. Accounts agree on the season and the sequence of events. No explanation for them commands general belief.

Livestock losses and famine marked the first year. In the second, raids for fuel set the Greenweald ablaze in the conflict called the War of Fire. The burned forest is now the Ashen Woods. Charcoal from its ruins moves through the market at Kolhavn to towns that once depended on the living forest for timber. In this third year of winter Harald still rules at Hrafnborg. His authority reaches farthest along the surviving routes for food and fuel. Beyond them, winter relief depends largely on agreements between neighboring settlements.

The longer accounts of the people who lived through these events appear in the companion volume *Stories of Svellheim*.
