# The Spare Bed

## Chapter One · Bare Feet

“Come and look at the moon,” his father said. “It'll have done whatever it's doing by the time you find your trousers.”

Finn accordingly saw the end of the world bare-legged in his nightshirt. So, as it happened, did a large part of their street. The moon had gone the color of the water in a butcher's bucket. People who had lived beside one another for years without much acquaintance stood in the road remarking on one another's toes. His father stepped on Finn's foot to make him yell and get his aunt Embla out for the joke. Embla had the forge across the street. She came in her leather apron with one whole cheek powdered in black.

Finn was nine. What he remembered afterward was that there was still an orange square of light behind her when she came into the street. As he watched, the red moon shrank to a splinter and went away. He turned to see what Aunt Embla thought of that. In the dark he almost walked into her. She had gone perfectly still. Behind her the square had gone black.

Their mother was at the hearth with one of the twins on her hip, stirring the ashes. There had been broth on the boil when Finn went to sleep. He put a finger in. It was warm enough to eat. His father set about kindling a fire and his mother gave the children the broth while they waited. She blew on a spoon before giving the youngest twin a mouthful and caught his father looking at her. For a little while they were all quite happy. The twins had never had supper in the middle of the night.

By morning every child on their street had been sent into the next street to borrow a coal. Some had got quite far up the hill to the place where an Ember rose naturally from the earth, with a great part of Hrafnborg built round it for the sake of the warmth. An old Keeper was on his knees in front of the cleft where his family had tended that fire for generations. At first Finn thought the man was praying. He had his fingers on the rock trying to find a place warm enough for a woman to lay her baby. All the town's little iron coal pots hung by their handles from people's cold hands. Every time somebody moved the pots knocked together.

He went back to the harbor. His father had taken the legs off the best bedstead. A neighbor was lifting one end of it through the back door. “Don't tell your mother,” his father grunted, as if they were stealing it, and Finn laughed. It was what he said when he had been feeding the cat fish that belonged in the family pie.

They made one room for six households. His mother put people who got up frequently on the outside, babies as far from the door as they could be got and old Livard under the window on account of his wind. The twins went down between his mother and Embla. Finn's father took the piece of floor where the boot heels went. There was nothing for the adults to do once they had got everybody fed except lie down in daylight, intimately acquainted with the small discomforts of people whom yesterday they had only known across a street.

On the second night Finn woke to a tiny regular sound coming from the kitchen. His father was crouched with his back to the room. His knuckles were bloody from striking his steel. There was good tinder on the hearth. It smelled of the summer fungus he'd helped his father peel from a birch. Across the lane his aunt had worn herself out trying to get her forge to light. Everybody's steel still struck sparks. The sparks fell and sat winking at them and went black.

“You said there wasn't any use,” Finn whispered.

“I didn't want Embla to use up her hands. She needs them.”

“You need yours too.”

His father turned them over as though he had never seen them. “Put a thumb on the tinder so it doesn't slip.”

They tried three more times. After the third his father went down on one hip with his head against the smoke hood. Finn sat beside him without speaking. He was old enough to know that a man who made a joke as often as his father did ought to have made one by now.

“Do you know what happened to the moon?”

“No, son.”

“Do you think somebody does?”

“I expect there'll be a great many by tomorrow who think so. Come put your feet between my knees. They're like two fish out of a well.”

When fire returned, three days had gone by. The first sound Finn heard was a woman saying an ugly word in a perfectly ordinary voice. She had burned herself trying a bit of wreck wood on a flame that a moment before had been only a spark. The fire took on the hearth. His mother fetched all the pots together and set them on the floor. Years before, his father told him, Frigg had entrusted fire to a child to carry to the next house. It was the custom in their part of the fjords for the children to take the first coals to a neighbor. Finn had done it at eight in a new coat while everyone watched and said what a big fellow he was.

In the third morning's gray light he went across the lane in his old shirt, in boots that pinched his feet, his shoulder to the wall because it seemed frightfully easy to slip. His aunt met him on the step with her apron over her hands.

“Did you bring the right sort?” she asked. “I'd hate to find your mother had sent cold ones.”

The small coals lit up her whole black face and the tracks left by the tears cutting through it. He didn't tell her he'd seen. She got the forge going. That night the family sat down to hot fish, singed on the outside and splendid, and thought the worst was over.

## Chapter Two · Milk for the Journey

Harpa was the month the barley went in. In the year when no one had quite settled on calling it Fimbulwinter yet, the water rained off the eaves for three days in Harpa and the bells began to ring down at the fish market. Finn's father sent him for bait, whistling around a mouthful of needles while he got the nets ready. His mother put her hand outside and let it get wet. Most mornings after that terrible night she had touched the hearthstone before letting herself go back to sleep.

They went up to a little holding beyond the south wall to help sow old Dag's field. Dag had a daughter Una's age, about grown, whose name was Oda. She had once taught Finn the great secret of drinking warm milk, which was to put the first squeeze in the cat's mouth so he would move his head and let you get at the second. Her cow's name was Poppy. Oda could lean against the cow's warm side on a milking stool and go to sleep.

The earth rolled back in black strips. Finn's father set a finger into the furrow and hit frost at the first joint. Beneath the surface it was hard enough to ring when the share struck. The sowing barley was brought back inside. Finn and Oda ran an old wheel down the slope until they had mud up to the backs of their heads. He went home with a bottle of milk, well pleased with a day's planting in which he'd been spared the job of planting anything.

They went back ten days later. Dag put the share through the same thin layer of earth. There was another rain. The grass greened on the south bank where the wall caught the sun, enough for a couple of sheaves cut with the kitchen scissors. The cow wanted more. Her hide kept the print of the rope against her breast long after Oda led her inside. They were mixing straw from the bedding into her feed. When Finn's father began to say something about waiting till it was safe to put a boat out, Dag put the share in the shed and closed the door on it with great care. He would have to buy hay with fish from a man who had hungry people of his own.

In the long daylight of the planting summer Finn and Oda carried Poppy's drinking water up from the well in a square bucket that thumped the side of Finn's knee raw. Oda fed the cow little handfuls while they worked. She had taken to carrying stems of green stuff in her apron like a person trying to tempt a sick child. At night a skin of ice formed again in that bucket. One morning there was no milk. Finn could feel the cow's tongue against his hand, patient as ever, looking for a turnip.

The day his father brought the long knives up, Finn was detailed to stand on the turn in the lane so nobody would take a cart through and frighten her. He was there a long time, looking down on a little window in the dairy where a pot of lavender had died. Oda came and leaned her back against his. They were still standing that way when the old man brought them jacket potatoes out of last year's store, slit with a knife and steamed with a piece of meat. Finn ate everything. He was sorry afterward for how good it tasted.

“I got her when my mother died,” Oda said. “People keep asking if I'm thinking about Mam. I'm well able to cry for a cow.”

“Will you come and live with us?”

“The house is still here, you fool.”

“Is there anything you'd like me to do?”

“People are going to say they're sorry and ask Da how he is and talk themselves silly. I'd give anything for somebody to take that milking stool out of there and put it where I needn't see it.”

He took it home. At the end of the week she walked down to the port carrying two jars of liver she had put up herself. She found Finn's father sitting on the stool mending floats. She made him take the bigger jar on the ground that he'd never fit on so small a stool if he had too much good food in him. After that she came down on market days to see if there was work.

By the Long Night's anniversary they had eaten the seed barley. There were thin spots in the city palisade where people had pried out a piece of dry wood at a time. One morning a watchman coming on duty saw a man who had been buried the previous week standing barefoot by the gate where he had sold mussels. That evening Finn ran all the way home. Everybody remembered accounts of people who had returned from death under a promise to Hel. Here was somebody who had stood beside an empty cart until the blood froze in his feet and had never once answered to his own name. A woman in the market said she had been warned of it all in a dream. His father took the children down another street the next time they went for fish.

There was still no southern sail when the days again began to shorten. From his house Finn could see men on the harbor boom chipping ice from the iron links. A ship from the south would have brought grain for all that neighborhood. He had seen the sacks swinging in a row, each big enough to make a chair for one of his sisters. People continued to put out an empty barrel where a ship's cook could find the old woman who came round with fresh water. By the second winter somebody had broken up the barrel for fuel.

## Chapter Three · The Apple Woman

There was a stranger at Embla's bellows wearing one green stocking and one gray one. Finn came in by the yard and caught her teaching his sisters to spit apple pips into the air, using pellets rolled from the paper the nails came in.

“What are you wasting good paper on?” he said in Embla's voice.

“I could carry you out and dunk you in the trough,” his aunt shouted from the back, “and it would do you good. Come and work the bellows or sit down and enjoy the lesson.”

The stranger's name was Virve. Her ears tapered to little points, particularly noticeable when she pinned what remained of her hair back above them. She was one of the Tonttu, the people of the forest that grew along the Askfoss far to the east. The raiders who had burned her village came for fuel. She had come away with her hands full of the wet clothes she had been hanging on the line. One of her stockings belonged to a cousin who had died before they made the coast. They were speaking of this as the War of Fire now. Finn could look clear through Virve's bundle of kindling to the light behind and see what a man might set fire to half the world to get.

“What's it taste like?” the youngest twin wanted to know.

“An apple?” Finn said. “You know what they taste like.”

“I know the picture. I want to know if it's like a potato.”

Virve made a little hollow of her hands and put them together. “The biggest ones we had for cooking were this size, and if you ate one raw it puckered your mouth till you could thread a needle with it. We had a little tree by the privy that made the best ones to eat. Yellow and brown. My father wanted it dug up on account of the roots in the pit. He never got my mother to let him.”

“Is it still there?”

“No, love.”

“Could you plant it again here?”

“I've seed enough. An apple you plant from a pip has its own ideas. If you want that same tree again you take a piece from a branch and fit it into another tree. We had to leave in a hurry.”

From her coat she produced a little packet. They were the real thing, polished dark brown with a chip on one tip like a toenail. The girls said they could smell apples on them. Virve said they had been in her brother's snuff box for a year and what they could smell was his abominable tobacco. They sat in a row passing the packet back and forth and sniffing until Embla came out to see what was holding up her bellows.

On the shortest day of that second winter the smith went downriver to the road where loaded sledges came in. Finn and Virve went with her. They could do very little by boat now. Glaciers had come down into the northern coves, and news of which stretches of road had disappeared in a fall of ice came south on the next man's sledge. The harbor had lost so much open water that a child with good aim could throw a fish head across it. South of town there was still a strip of sea between the landfast ice and the moving pack. What came from elsewhere came mostly by the back road. Worn-out draft animals stood trembling with their heads down. They were fed more regularly than some people Finn knew.

The sledge from Kolhavn stood at the back. That was the town where charcoal from the burned Greenweald was brought down to the water before the last part of the journey west. Most people now called what was left of that green country the Ashen Woods. Virve stopped with her hand on a sack. On the side of the sack somebody had drawn the ears of a hare in a circle.

“Used to buy apples in one of these,” she said. “A whole peck. There was one over every child's head at the races at our midsummer market. They put your feet inside and you had to jump up the street.”

Finn could imagine the twins doing precisely that across his mother's kitchen, breaking things. Virve undid the tie and reached in. A piece of charcoal came out with the shape of a twig still in it and a little knot where another twig had grown out of it.

“Do you know the wood?” he asked.

“How the devil would I know it?” she said. She raised her head sharply, as astonished as he was at the sound of her own voice. The carter looked round. “Everybody here asks me which bit of black wood came out of my house. It's all gone black. That's what a fire does to a place.”

Embla came over to look. She had enough saved to buy four sacks at last year's price. Today it would buy two. She stepped away to see what was to be had three sledges down. Finn stayed where he was.

“My aunt needs it,” he said at last. “She can do hooks with an ordinary fire if she has to. There's a fisherman waiting for a new rudder fitting. If we don't buy the stuff he won't sail.”

“You want me to tell you it's all right to use it.”

“Yes.”

“I'd quite like to meet the man who'd say he wasn't going to use it, and stand by him a day or two when the wind got up.” She tied the sack. “Take the end with the hare. The other end leaks.”

Together they carried it. On the way home she asked him the fisherman's name. It was his father. He told her he had scraped the old paint off most of the boat himself, and how his father had left the green strip above the waterline because nobody on their shore had such an ugly green to repaint it. By the time they'd got the load inside, Virve had learned all about the *White Gull* and the tendency of Finn's father to throw up over the side for the first ten minutes of a rough trip, although he had been at sea since he was a boy. That evening she helped to work the iron for his rudder. It took two sets of hands at the bellows. The girls were allowed to sit on Oda's milking stool and watch.

The supper that came to them from his father's next trip was flatfish fried in the last spoonful of butter his mother had been hiding from them. All seven licked their fingers. Of all the meals in Finn's life he would remember the taste of that one because they ate it so slowly. Even the girls knew how hard a thing it was, in that second year of winter, to put a browned fish in a child's two hands.

## Chapter Four · A Boat in the Next Cove

The third year he was twelve. There was a mark on the door frame to prove it. His father used the point of his knife to give the last mark a little boat of its own. Finn was tall enough to wind the harbor chain with a second person at the handle. He was old enough to sign for the fuel allowance at the gate, which meant going home with enough wood to dry the little girls' wet things and enough left to cook the supper if one thing was done immediately after the other.

On the last trip his father took, his mother said there'd be fish cheaper next week. He put his head into the room where she was rolling up the nets and said, “If there's fish at all next week it'll be because somebody goes for it this one.”

He bent to the youngest twin, who had developed a habit of putting her head under his chin to see if his beard had grown longer overnight. Finn came to him with a frayed line from the spare thole on the steering side. His father kissed him on the crown in passing and went out saying something about mending it when he came home. Just an end of line, hardly long enough to do anything with. Finn kept it in his trousers and found it there every time he went looking for something else.

During the gale they could hear the ice breaking in the harbor. They got doors slammed and children in. Afterwards Finn and his mother went down to the shore each time there was a shout. Three boats came in under oars. A fourth came in behind the others in pieces. Nobody had seen the *White Gull* since the wind veered. On the fourth night Aunt Embla fetched a sailor home from the south gate with a split lip and one foot so swollen they had to cut her boot off.

“Her name is Rua. She can have my bed if she'll have the forge,” his aunt said. “But I've the two little ones from the cobbler's in there, and they wake and scream. She's got enough to do to get a bit of sleep.”

“The nets are on Da's bed,” Finn said quickly.

His aunt looked at him. “Yes, I had noticed that.”

Rua was awake when he brought her a little dish of broth. The name on her boat had been *Little Auk*. Of the five aboard her, three had made it to the fishing hut at Split Rock. The other two survivors were still there. One had got his leg between the boat and the shore. The second man had a hurt shoulder but was able to keep him warm. Rua had set out because she was the lightest. She had fallen on the port road and crawled the last bit.

“Split Rock?” he said. “The chimney goes sideways? My father used to put the catch there till he'd a load. Did you see a boat pulled up? Green stripe on her.”

It was plain she'd been asked about boats at every house she'd passed. She put her spoon down. “I remember picking a green board out of the water. You wouldn't know a boat from so little. Will you get somebody to go for those two? I've got to lie down before I fall down.”

He went to his aunt. “I know which hut,” he said. “If Da put in there he'd have had to leave the boat. The whole beach goes out from under you when the wind changes.”

“My dear, how many times have you had him alive up and down this coast since he sailed?”

“He's shown me where he goes. I can bring you right to the door. Will you take Virve's sledge? Those other two are there. One of them might have seen him.”

His aunt put on her apron as if to go to work on an ordinary morning. She tied it extra tight. With her left hand she found the key to the shed and made a little space on her palm so he could take it. “Your mother's got to hear from your own mouth where we're going.”

The sledge was on runners that Virve's people had been using in the wet woods for a long time before Fimbulwinter. Oda came to take a turn pulling. Nobody in the house talked about what happened at the hut if there were three live men to bring home. His mother put out three pairs of wool socks. She had to borrow the last from a neighbor who had sailors of her own still out.

The sledge kept trying to twist off the path on the way up the headland. From there they could see the open water in long green rips as the pack moved against the tide. A black spot leapt up and down on the edge of it without ever getting any closer to land. He thought it was a bird. His aunt thought so, too. Then they went down the lee of the hill where it was almost warm, heaving on the runners to drag the load past big patches of bare rock. Finn got to the hut first. It stood well above the beach, with a funny elbow in its stone chimney that his father said had been built by a man who'd been turned round dancing.

Inside, a stranger was shaving another man with cold water. Of all the things he had been prepared to walk in on, that had never occurred to him.

“Got to look well when we get into town,” the man with the razor said. “Did Rua get there?”

Aunt Embla said yes. The sailor shut his eyes. He was trying to hold another man's chin with an arm that could barely get higher than his elbow. The strop had gone dark where he'd brought the razor down on his own finger.

“Is there anybody else?” Finn asked.

“Just the two of us. Arni there saw a boat making the next cove the afternoon of the blow.”

“Might have been green,” said the man laid out on the fishing table. “A long black hull and a bit of color astern. Kept the sail up till it went out of my sight.”

They had brought a sledge wide enough for the man to lie down if he drew his elbows in. His aunt took the man's hand in both of hers and spoke to him almost as soon as she saw his leg. “I've seen a smith's striker go down with one like that and dance again. It took him time. It will hurt beyond anything you've begun to guess when we lift you. I'll tell you before we do.”

Out on the headland Oda held the sledge still while Finn knocked its runners free of ice. The next cove was some way away. The path over the ridge was open. And it was bright enough still to make out the little wooden windmill on the crest, an idle thing a man had carried up there to please his children, its arms going round quite fast against the plain sky.

Aunt Embla came out. “If you're going round there, go now. Oda can go with you. I'll stay the night here if you need the light. Your father is my brother. I'll sit that watch for him gladly.”

Finn put his foot on the path that went up to the windmill. He could see the whole length of it. In an hour or so he would come into the next little bay. He had stood up there on his father's shoulders in midsummer with a piece of bread spread with butter so thick it bore the print of his thumb. The water below had been the color of a field in bloom, every jellyfish taking the long sunlight about itself as it opened and closed. His father's two hands about his ankles had been quite steady. For a small boy it was the nearest thing there could be to flying.

Behind him there came the most wretched sound he had ever heard a man make. His aunt was helping Arni into his coat. “Wait a little,” he heard the man say. “If you'll just give me a minute I'll be able to take the other sleeve.”

He came down the path.

“Fetch the sledge up close, Oda,” he said. “Let's get him home while he can still sit up.”

His aunt nodded. It did not feel good to have made the decision. When the others went in to help him get the man up, Finn took the short line out of his pocket and laid it on the windowsill, coiled the way his father liked. With a piece of old charcoal he wrote under it where they had taken the men and the time they had left. His father would see his writing and know.

## Chapter Five · The Good Blanket

It was nearly midnight when they brought the sledge in. The light above the town rose from the crack in the earth where it had burned since before there was a king in Hrafnborg. There were people underneath it drying their clothes in patches of warmth, quietly making room as one got warm enough to give another his place. When the sailors on the sledge called out that Rua had got them home, Finn's mother looked first at the two men and then across the gate to the unbroken darkness behind him. He had to take her hand before he could get out what he meant to say.

“Somebody saw a boat making the next cove. I could have gone. I brought these fellows instead.”

His mother put her cold fingers against the back of his neck. “Give me a minute,” she said. They stood together while his aunt began to wrangle the sledge round to the house where the healer lived. When his mother was ready they went after her.

At home the little ones were asleep, both in the same nightdress, top to tail, because all the shirts in the house had been torn up for the people off the boats. Virve and Oda were sitting on the floor taking turns over a bowl of what looked like yesterday's supper, with the cat hanging his head over the rim. The house smelled of damp wool, the sour yeasty smell of feet bound up a long while, the good salted smell of the broth. At the hearth Rua lay in a chair. She had got the creases of her sleeve printed into one cheek from trying to make a pillow out of so little arm.

“I have to go and see them,” she told Finn.

“Morning will do. One's shaved and the other isn't. We got there right in the middle.”

She closed her eyes. He went through into the next room. His father's spare nets were stowed clear up to the bed head. It took him three trips to carry them to the shed and come back with them off his forearms, where they caught in the skin. He took the good blanket out of the chest and put the soft side to the sheet. Rua sat down, dragging her bad foot up and over with a hand under her knee. The board beneath the mattress gave the loud, familiar creak that used to send the cat flying off the bed when his father turned over on it.

She started to get up. “Did I break something?”

“That's been doing that since I was little. You'd think somebody in a family with a smith in it could have fixed it by now.”

“I could sleep in the chair.”

He tucked the blanket behind her foot where somebody had slit open the boot. The tears had come into his eyes quite without warning. There was the sound again as she settled into the hollow where his father's shoulder had lain.

“He used to whisper to the cat,” Finn told her. “He gave him the fish from our pie, and he'd whisper so my mother couldn't hear him. Have you ever known a cat tell on anybody?”

She moved a little so he could sit on the edge of the bed if he liked. From the kitchen came his mother's voice, low, pleased to have caught them at it again after all this time.

“I could hear the pair of you perfectly well,” she said.
