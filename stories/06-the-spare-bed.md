# The Spare Bed

## Chapter One · The First Pot

Every winter since he was eight, Finn had carried a pot of live coals across the lane to his aunt’s house. The first time, he had been so frightened of dropping it that he walked sideways with his shoulder against the wall. His aunt opened the door, lifted the lid and said gravely, “I see you’ve brought the right sort. I was afraid your mother might send cold ones.”

He had adored her ever since. Her name was Embla. She was a smith. One of her hands closed only halfway after a burn, and she could pick up a hot pan with that hand while everyone else in the room yelled at her to put it down.

Finn was twelve that winter. In Hrafnborg the harbor had frozen three years running, and what his aunt burned in her forge now came by the back road on sledges. He had started helping her weigh it. This year she had a stranger asleep by her fire. The woman’s boots stood upright by themselves. A strip of sailcloth served her for a coat. Embla took the pot and set supper for three.

“Your mother still has that bed?” she asked.

Finn knew the bed. The boards creaked in the middle where his father had turned over in his sleep, every night of his life, so hard it used to tip the cat onto the floor. His father’s boat had failed to come in the previous autumn. The cat still slept on the floor.

“She keeps nets on it,” he said.

“I know what she keeps on it.”

“Then why did you ask?”

The woman by the fire opened one eye. “If it’s a question of nets, I can mend them.”

“Which boat?” he asked her.

“Mine was the Little Auk. Five of us and three of us made shore.”

“Did you see the White Gull? Green stripe above the waterline?”

His aunt reached for the soup ladle. “Finn.”

“What would it hurt to tell me if you saw her?”

“I saw a green plank with a rope hole in it the morning after the blow,” the woman said. “That’s the truth. There must be a hundred boats with a green stripe on your coast. Are you sure you wanted me to tell you that?”

Finn waited for her to take it back or make it into better news. She ate her supper, little spoonfuls at a time because there was a split in her lip. When Embla gave him the empty coal pot he took the woman’s sailcloth as well. Someone had tried to mend a hole in it with a fish bone, and he didn’t think she ought to have to put it on again tonight.

## Chapter Two · Five Names

His twin sisters came running barefoot to look at the visitor. His mother took the nets off his father’s bed herself, in a temper, and made it up with the good wool blanket, also in a temper. In the morning Finn went with her to enter their guest on the fuel roll. The little girls had spent half the previous day picking the bark off scraps at the wood yard. One extra share of fuel would mean a week they could keep their mittens dry.

At the gate stood benches full of people. One man had gone to sleep sitting bolt upright with a fish basket in his lap. Finn’s mother gave the clerk four names and then the stranger’s, which was Rua. Finn watched the man’s finger run down the list to their house. His father’s name was still there under a thin brown stroke of ink. He could read every letter.

“Where did she sleep before?” the clerk asked.

“On a boat,” said Rua. “Then under my boat. Then there wasn’t enough of her left for either purpose.”

“Who bears witness for you here?”

Finn expected his aunt. Embla gave away enough iron on credit that half the city had cause to speak well of her. His mother had come directly from hanging up washing and there was a wet patch on her skirt.

“I do,” his mother said.

The clerk had his knife out to sharpen the pen. He paused. “You knew her on the boat?”

“No. She came through my own door last night. Isn’t that what you’re asking me to answer for?”

All up the benches people had begun to listen. The clerk made room on the old line for Rua. Finn got his thumb onto the page before the pen touched it.

“That’s my father’s place.”

The woman halfway along the bench stopped bouncing her baby. His mother took him by the wrist. He was old enough that she could no longer simply move his hand. He was surprised to feel how little strength there was in hers.

“I can come in myself and tell you if I leave,” Rua said. “I’d like a line I can put my own name to. There seems to be a little room under his.”

Finn lifted his thumb. Rua wrote a good clear hand, rather better than the clerk’s. She put her name in the space below the old one. The man blew on the ink. “That will do very well,” he said.

## Chapter Three · The Creak

That evening Rua took one of the torn nets onto her knees. Her knots lay flat under the thumb. She fell asleep in the chair halfway through the second net, snoring loudly enough to make Finn’s mother smile into her sleeve. Later, in his own bed, Finn lay awake listening for Rua to turn over in the next room. He wanted the board to creak. When it did, he began to cry and was furious with her.

His mother came and sat on the edge of the bed. “It’s the damn board,” she said.

He nodded. Through the thin wall Rua said, “I can put the blanket on the floor.”

“Stay where you are,” his mother said. “We have a cat. You’ll have to fight him for it.”

In the morning Finn carried the pot back to Embla and asked if he could go through the offcuts by her bench. He found a scrap of oak just thick enough to wedge the loose board tight. His mother stood in the door while he worked it into place. He had been afraid she would object. She put her shoulder under the bed to take the weight and waited till he had it snug.

That evening Rua spread one of the mended nets on the kitchen floor and showed Finn how to make the flat knot. In the next room his mother sat on the bed to thread a needle by the window. They heard the creak.

“You missed one,” she called.

Finn looked at the way Rua was holding the net to keep the cat from getting tangled, and at his mother coming back with enough needles for three. “I think we’ll leave it,” he said.
