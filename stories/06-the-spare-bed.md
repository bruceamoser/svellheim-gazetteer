# The Spare Bed

## Chapter One · Bare Feet

“Come and look at the moon,” his father said. “It'll have done whatever it's doing by the time you find your trousers.”

Finn accordingly saw the end of the world bare-legged in his nightshirt. So, as it happened, did a large part of their street. The moon had gone the color of the water in a butcher's bucket. People who had lived beside one another for years without much acquaintance stood in the road remarking on one another's toes. His father stepped on Finn's foot to make him yell and get his aunt Embla out for the joke. Embla had the forge across the street. She came in her leather apron with one whole cheek powdered in black.

Finn was nine. What he remembered afterward was that there was still an orange square of light behind her when she came into the street. As he watched, the red moon shrank to a splinter and went away. He turned to see what Aunt Embla thought of that. In the dark he almost walked into her. She had gone perfectly still. Behind her the square had gone black.

Their mother was at the hearth with one of the twins on her hip, stirring the ashes. There had been broth on the boil when Finn went to sleep. He put a finger in. It was warm enough to eat. His father set about kindling a fire and his mother gave the children the broth while they waited. She blew on a spoon before giving the youngest twin a mouthful and caught his father looking at her. For a little while they were all quite happy. The twins had never had supper in the middle of the night.

By morning every child on their street had been sent into the next street to borrow a coal. Some had got quite far up the hill to the place where an Ember rose naturally from the earth, with a great part of Hrafnborg built round it for the sake of the warmth. An old Keeper was on his knees in front of the cleft where his family had tended that fire for generations. At first Finn thought the man was praying. He had his fingers on the rock trying to find a place warm enough for a woman to lay her baby. All the town's little iron coal pots hung by their handles from people's cold hands. Every time somebody moved the pots knocked together.

He went back to the harbor. His father had taken the legs off the best bedstead. A neighbor was lifting one end of it through the back door. “Don't tell your mother,” his father grunted, as if they were stealing it, and Finn laughed. It was what he said when he had been feeding the cat fish that belonged in the family pie.

They made one room for six households. His mother put people who got up frequently on the outside, babies as far from the door as they could be got and old Livard under the window on account of his wind. The twins went down between his mother and Embla. Finn's father took the piece of floor where the boot heels went. There was nothing for the adults to do once they had got everybody fed except lie down in daylight, intimately acquainted with the small discomforts of people whom yesterday they had only known across a street.

It was Livard who knew the lullaby his mother sang. Finn had always supposed it had come out of her head the way the shopping list did. There had been a long, unpleasant time when the younger of the cobbler's children cried herself so blind with it that her father couldn't find a moment in which to get her to swallow a mouthful of milk. Then from the bed under the window came the words about a little gray goose stepping out in her red shoes. The child's mouth stayed open just long enough. His mother sang along. When they came to the place where Finn had always had to giggle, Livard sang a word Finn had never heard out of his mother's mouth in his life.

“That's how it went at the ropewalk,” the old man said. “We had to twist three cords together while we sang it. What did they give you to do with your hands when they taught you?”

“Shucked peas,” his mother said after a moment, as if her hands had had to remember for her. “My sister would put all the little ones in her cheek. My father thought one side of her face grew quicker than the other.”

In the silence that followed Finn could hear the baby take the milk and swallow. When he woke again his father had put a coat under the door where the draft came in and was hanging their one glass lamp back up on its hook. He had stood with it in his hand for quite a long time. Finn thought with a trouble he had no way to put into words that he had fallen asleep listening for the little noise the wick made as his father turned it down. It was the sound that meant his father was the only person still up, and Finn could let the house go on without him.

On the second night Finn woke to a tiny regular sound coming from the kitchen. His father was crouched with his back to the room. His knuckles were bloody from striking his steel. There was good tinder on the hearth. It smelled of the summer fungus he'd helped his father peel from a birch. Across the lane his aunt had worn herself out trying to get her forge to light. Everybody's steel still struck sparks. The sparks fell and sat winking at them and went black.

“You said there wasn't any use,” Finn whispered.

“I didn't want Embla to use up her hands. She needs them.”

“You need yours too.”

His father turned them over as though he had never seen them. “Put a thumb on the tinder so it doesn't slip.”

They tried three more times. After the third his father went down on one hip with his head against the smoke hood. Finn sat beside him without speaking. He was old enough to know that a man who made a joke as often as his father did ought to have made one by now.

“Do you know what happened to the moon?”

“No, son.”

“Do you think somebody does?”

“I expect there'll be a great many by tomorrow who think so. Come put your feet between my knees. They're like two fish out of a well.”

By the third afternoon they had eaten everything that could be eaten cold except some wrinkled roots and his mother's best pot of salted butter. That pot was her spring money. She meant to sell it when the southern ships came in and buy leather to sole the twins' shoes. Finn's father fetched the butter and cut it with a piece of thread because the knife was in use cutting up a neighbor's last piece of smoked meat. It gave like cold wax under the thread. Every child had a yellow shaving on a brown slice of turnip. The taste opened a space in Finn's mouth he hadn't known there was, so that when he swallowed there was a little ache under both ears.

“Will the ships come if the fires stay out?” he asked.

His father made room on the bench. “You can come into this harbor in daylight without seeing a lamp. You can come in after dark if you know the wind and have somebody in the bow who can hear the sound off the cliffs. You can be a damn fool too, and I've managed all three. What makes you ask?”

“The shoes.” He was sorry he had mentioned it the instant he saw the empty pot on its side.

“Come here, then. Give me a look at yours.” They sat with the boot in his father's lap and the big thumb that had lost so much skin trying to kindle a fire finding the stitch where wet came in. “I can do something for these. Do you know what?” Finn didn't. “I can put a piece of my old belt in that heel. You'll take the skin off me next time you tread on my toes.”

His father put the empty pot under the bench. “I don't know what will happen to the ships, Finn. It's all right to ask me things I don't know. I sometimes find I get a better question that way.”

When fire returned, three days had gone by. The first sound Finn heard was a woman saying an ugly word in a perfectly ordinary voice. She had burned herself trying a bit of wreck wood on a flame that a moment before had been only a spark. The fire took on the hearth. His mother fetched all the pots together and set them on the floor. Years before, his father told him, Frigg had entrusted fire to a child to carry to the next house. It was the custom in their part of the fjords for the children to take the first coals to a neighbor. Finn had done it at eight in a new coat while everyone watched and said what a big fellow he was.

In the gray light after the third night he went across the lane in his old shirt, in boots that pinched his feet, his shoulder to the wall because it seemed frightfully easy to slip. His aunt met him on the step with her apron over her hands.

“Did you bring the right sort?” she asked. “I'd hate to find your mother had sent cold ones.”

The small coals lit up her whole black face and the tracks left by the tears cutting through it. He didn't tell her he'd seen. She got the forge going. In years to come people called the night the moon vanished the Long Night and the three days that followed it the First Silence. When Finn heard the name he thought of the scrape of his father’s steel against stone in the dark kitchen, and how long he had listened for the next stroke. That night the family sat down to hot fish, singed on the outside and splendid, and thought the worst was over.

## Chapter Two · Milk for the Journey

Harpa was the month the barley went in. That first year a few people already called the cold Fimbulwinter, the great winter that stretched from one year into the next. Most expected to see gulls and ships again when the ice broke. Sure enough, the water rained off the eaves for three days in Harpa and the bells began to ring down at the fish market. Finn's father sent him for bait, whistling around a mouthful of needles while he got the nets ready. His mother put her hand outside and let it get wet. Most mornings after that terrible night she had touched the hearthstone before letting herself go back to sleep.

They went up to a little holding beyond the south wall to help sow old Dag's field. Dag's daughter Oda was sixteen and had been milking Poppy twice a day since she was Finn's age. She had once taught Finn the great secret of drinking warm milk, which was to put the first squeeze in the cat's mouth so he would move his head and let you get at the second. Oda could lean against the cow's warm side on a milking stool and go to sleep.

The earth rolled back in black strips. Finn's father set a finger into the furrow and hit frost at the first joint. Beneath the surface it was hard enough to ring when the share struck. The sowing barley was brought back inside. Finn and Oda ran an old wheel down the slope until they had mud up to the backs of their heads. He went home with a bottle of milk, well pleased with a day's planting in which he'd been spared the job of planting anything.

That evening his father laid the nets along the alley and found a different snarl in every arm's length. Across the water a ledge of blue ice showed, on the side of the headland where the first nesting birds should have been. Finn used to get the easy knots because his nails were stronger than a grown man's broken ones. Tonight his father left a perfectly good loop hanging and went across the alley to look over the heads of two pilots at the ice.

“Is the road through there?” Finn called after him.

“Sometimes. Can you think what color it ought to be out there if there was a way through?”

“Black where there's water.”

“Black where there’s shadow too. You can mistake one for the other till you’re nearly home. Have a look at what the gulls are doing.”

They waited to see a bird settle. There had been a hundred most springs, following the tips of the oars when the fishermen came in, and the sound under the windows was enough to put your teeth on edge. Not one came down while they stood there. His father knelt and put both their faces on a level. The air tasted of snow that had been on the ground for months. Finn had missed the smell of the dead weed drying on the rocks without knowing that was what he wanted to smell.

“My father taught me to put spring in the right order,” his father said. “The ships come last. First we need open water that doesn't close behind them, and enough for the birds and fish to eat. The king could send a letter to every man who owns a sail and he still couldn't put them in a different order.”

They went back ten days later. Dag put the share through the same thin layer of earth. There was another rain. The grass greened on the south bank where the wall caught the sun, enough for a couple of sheaves cut with the kitchen scissors. The cow wanted more. Her hide kept the print of the rope against her breast long after Oda led her inside. They were mixing straw from the bedding into her feed. When Finn's father began to say something about waiting till it was safe to put a boat out, Dag put the share in the shed and closed the door on it with great care. He would have to buy hay with fish from a man who had hungry people of his own.

In the long daylight of the planting summer Finn and Oda carried Poppy's drinking water up from the well in a square bucket that thumped the side of Finn's knee raw. Oda fed the cow little handfuls while they worked. She had taken to carrying stems of green stuff in her apron like a person trying to tempt a sick child. At night a skin of ice formed again in that bucket. One morning there was no milk. Finn could feel the cow's tongue against his hand, patient as ever, looking for a turnip.

The day his father brought the long knives up, Finn was detailed to stand on the turn in the lane so nobody would take a cart through and frighten her. He was there a long time, looking down on a little window in the dairy where a pot of lavender had died. Oda came and leaned her back against his. They were still standing that way when the old man brought them jacket potatoes out of last year's store, slit with a knife and steamed with a piece of meat. Finn ate everything. He was sorry afterward for how good it tasted.

“I got her when my mother died,” Oda said. “People keep asking if I'm thinking about Mam. I'm well able to cry for a cow.”

“Will you come and live with us?”

“The house is still here, you fool.”

“Is there anything you'd like me to do?”

“People are going to say they're sorry and ask Da how he is and talk themselves silly. I'd give anything for somebody to take that milking stool out of there and put it where I needn't see it.”

He took it home. At the end of the week she walked down to the port carrying two jars of liver she had put up herself. She found Finn's father sitting on the stool mending floats. She made him take the bigger jar on the ground that he'd never fit on so small a stool if he had too much good food in him. After that she came down on market days to see if there was work.

At the end of what the calendar still called summer Oda took Finn up to empty the seed chest. They had held the barley back from the pot one handful at a time since Harpa. Now the council was asking how much could be ground for winter. Dag had kept the chest in the driest place in the house, on feet that stood each in its own pottery saucer so a mouse trying to climb up made a noise in the night. There were bits of three old saucers in a corner. Finn knew what it was to lie awake and hear something you couldn't afford to lose getting eaten.

“Da wants one handful in a little bag,” Oda said. “Will you take it home and keep it? He thinks if he knows he can get his hands on it he'll put it in the porridge some morning. If you have it he'll have to come ask your mother.”

“What happens if she needs it?”

“I should think she'd want you to have something to eat.” Oda sat down on the edge of the chest. “Do you remember my telling you I was well able to cry for a cow? You were the only person who believed me. It was that same smell came off Mam's apron after she'd put Poppy in for the night. That got to me after you'd gone. I thought if I told you that part you'd have me down for a liar.”

“You can have more than one thing to cry over,” Finn said. It seemed to him a perfectly ordinary thing to know by now.

“Get a dry bag, then. Enough there to plant a little square should you ever have a place to plant it.”

His mother hung it in the rafters. As the days shortened that autumn she took it down for a baby who could keep nothing else in his belly. She asked Dag first. Finn went with her, and had to watch his old friend lay the barley in a bowl and wash it himself. The little white kernels came clean in the old man's hand. He sang half a verse of the gray goose song as he took them to the mill. It was a song for shifting something along from one hand to another, Finn was beginning to see, and you could set a different pair of feet to it almost anywhere.

When Finn looked for the southern sails that first autumn, as the days began to shorten, he could see all the way to the last black headland. From his house Finn could see men on the harbor boom chipping ice from the iron links. A ship from the south would have brought grain for all that neighborhood. He had seen the sacks swinging in a row, each big enough to make a chair for one of his sisters. People continued to put out an empty barrel where a ship's cook could find the old woman who came round with fresh water. The barrel lasted until the night before the anniversary, when somebody broke it up for fuel.

By the Long Night's anniversary they had eaten the grain from the big chest. There were thin spots in the city palisade where people had pried out a piece of dry wood at a time. One morning a watchman coming on duty saw a man who had been buried the previous week standing barefoot by the gate where he had sold mussels. That evening Finn ran all the way home. Everybody remembered accounts of people who had returned from death under a promise to Hel. Here was somebody who had stood beside an empty cart until the blood froze in his feet and had never once answered to his own name. A woman in the market said she had been warned of it all in a dream. His father took the children down another street the next time they went for fish.

## Chapter Three · The Apple Woman

There was a stranger at Embla's bellows wearing one green stocking and one gray one. Finn came in by the yard and caught her teaching his sisters to spit apple pips into the air, using pellets rolled from the paper the nails came in.

“What are you wasting good paper on?” he said in Embla's voice.

“I could carry you out and dunk you in the trough,” his aunt shouted from the back, “and it would do you good. Come and work the bellows or sit down and enjoy the lesson.”

The stranger's name was Virve. Her ears tapered to little points, particularly noticeable when she pinned what remained of her hair back above them. She was one of the Tonttu, the people of the forest that grew along the Askfoss far to the east. The raiders who had burned her village came for fuel. She had come away with her hands full of the wet clothes she had been hanging on the line. One of her stockings belonged to a cousin who had died before they made the coast. They were speaking of this as the War of Fire now. Finn could look clear through Virve's bundle of kindling to the light behind and see what a man might set fire to half the world to get.

“What's it taste like?” the youngest twin wanted to know.

“An apple?” Finn said. “You know what they taste like.”

“I know the picture. I want to know if it's like a potato.”

Virve made a little hollow of her hands and put them together. “The biggest ones we had for cooking were this size, and if you ate one raw it puckered your mouth till you could thread a needle with it. We had a little tree by the privy that made the best ones to eat. Yellow and brown. My father wanted it dug up on account of the roots in the pit. He never got my mother to let him.”

“Is it still there?”

“No, love.”

“Could you plant it again here?”

“I've seed enough. An apple you plant from a pip has its own ideas. If you want that same tree again you take a piece from a branch and fit it into another tree. We had to leave in a hurry.”

From her coat she produced a little packet. They were the real thing, polished dark brown with a chip on one tip like a toenail. The girls said they could smell apples on them. Virve said they had been in her brother's snuff box for a year and what they could smell was his abominable tobacco. They sat in a row passing the packet back and forth and sniffing until Embla came out to see what was holding up her bellows.

Virve was staying in an empty sail loft beside the forge with five households from up the Askfoss. Finn went over one evening to bring back the crock she had lent them. Somebody was playing a game on the floor with buttons. A woman even older than Livard was making a noise with her lips to imitate the bubbling of a marsh on a summer night. One of Finn's sisters had got herself up the ladder and was sitting there quite at home with her skirts spread out to keep the buttons from rolling into the cracks. It was the good green stocking he recognized on the line over the old woman's head that brought it home to him where he was. Nobody in the loft, from the baby to the old woman who could make frog noises, had a house to go back to.

“Is that where you were when they set fire to it?” he asked Virve. He had looked into the little stove to see if there was room to set the crock beside it, and a twist of burning wood had gone up so bright he had blinked.

“I was at my cousin's because the two of us owned a wash kettle together. She wanted to wash in my yard that day. We went to hers because there was more sun. I expect her neighbors were glad enough of that. We came out with a line full of soaking things to put over their heads. We could get people as far as the river while they were wet.”

He tried to picture wet sheets hung over people running. “How many times did you go?”

“Three that I know about. A person keeps going back to where they hung the kettle, even once there's no kettle any more.” She put the crock in his lap. “I was going to give you a piece of our cake wrapped up to take home. Come back and taste it first. We made it with smoked meal and it tastes of the privy apples. I should like a second opinion.”

He went with her to where a girl was taking little round flat cakes off the top of the stove, turning them with the back of her father's blackened spoon. One bite had him coughing. Virve had her own piece in her mouth. She began to laugh. An old man across the loft got up to give them both water and slap Virve on the back.

“It wants a deal of honey,” Finn managed. “My father's got a sweet tooth. He puts a spoon of honey in peas when he can get it.”

“I thought my cooking was coming on. You know something, Finn? I've told seven people about that wash kettle since I came here. You're the first that's found himself stuck here having to eat my cooking afterward. It feels pretty good.”

He went home with his sister astride his back, the crock under his jacket and two cakes in it. His father ate one without any complaint. The second went to Oda, who helped Virve find a sack of plain meal the next week. His mother sent a bit of salt. Somewhere about the middle of Ylir the twins lost interest in which of the grown people had lent which bit of pottery. They only knew if you went up the ladder with an empty dish you would hear the frogs while somebody was finding something to put in it.

On the shortest day of that second winter the smith went downriver to the road where loaded sledges came in. Finn and Virve went with her. They could do very little by boat now. Glaciers had come down into the northern coves, and news of which stretches of road had disappeared in a fall of ice came south on the next man's sledge. The harbor had lost so much open water that a child with good aim could throw a fish head across it. South of town there was still a strip of sea between the landfast ice and the moving pack. What came from elsewhere came mostly by the back road. Worn-out draft animals stood trembling with their heads down. They were fed more regularly than some people Finn knew.

The sledge from Kolhavn stood at the back. That was the town where charcoal from the burned Greenweald was brought down to the water before the last part of the journey west. Most people now called what was left of that green country the Ashen Woods. Virve stopped with her hand on a sack. On the side of the sack somebody had drawn the ears of a hare in a circle.

“Used to buy apples in one of these,” she said. “A whole peck. There was one over every child's head at the races at our midsummer market. They put your feet inside and you had to jump up the street.”

Finn could imagine the twins doing precisely that across his mother's kitchen, breaking things. Virve undid the tie and reached in. A piece of charcoal came out with the shape of a twig still in it and a little knot where another twig had grown out of it.

“Do you know the wood?” he asked.

“How the devil would I know it?” she said. She raised her head sharply, as astonished as he was at the sound of her own voice. The carter looked round. “Everybody here asks me which bit of black wood came out of my house. It's all gone black. That's what a fire does to a place.”

Embla came over to look. She had enough saved to buy four sacks at last year's price. Today it would buy two. She stepped away to see what was to be had three sledges down. Finn stayed where he was.

“My aunt needs it,” he said at last. “She can do hooks with an ordinary fire if she has to. There's a fisherman waiting for a new rudder fitting. If we don't buy the stuff he won't sail.”

“You want me to tell you it's all right to use it.”

“Yes.”

“I'd quite like to meet the man who'd say he wasn't going to use it, and stand by him a day or two when the wind got up.” She tied the sack. “Take the end with the hare. The other end leaks.”

Together they carried it. On the way home she asked him the fisherman's name. It was his father. He told her he had scraped the old paint off most of the boat himself, and how his father had left the green strip above the waterline because nobody on their shore had such an ugly green to repaint it. By the time they'd got the load inside, Virve had learned all about the *White Gull* and the tendency of Finn's father to throw up over the side for the first ten minutes of a rough trip, although he had been at sea since he was a boy. That evening Finn got to work beside Virve. There were two leather bags that lay under the bench in Embla's forge. He knew how to make the great wooden handles breathe, one bag filling in long sighs while the other blew air into the coals. Virve had dried fleeces by one of these in the wet woods. She worked her end so easily that for the first time he could stand and look at exactly what his aunt was doing with the fire they made. In the middle of a bed of little black branches that had come all the way from the forests along the Askfoss, one piece of old iron stood out orange at the edges and yellow in the thick of it. Months in the weather had gnawed holes in the fastening that held the rudder to his father's boat. Embla meant to turn the bad part out where salt water could go on eating it for another year before it did any harm. His father had her measurements chalked on the flat of his hand and forgot they were there when he went to rub his nose.

The girls were allowed to sit on Oda's milking stool and watch. One of them fell asleep and the other held her up. Their father wanted to know if a person could ride round town with a sleeping sister hanging off her like a sack of onions. The waking twin said she'd charge him for the onions if he wanted any and he ought to know that by now.

Embla's left hand had been scarred that winter. Trying to coax a little more heat out of bad fuel she had reached for a bar that looked black in daylight and still held the heat inside it. She could not bring her last two fingers together. Finn saw his father measure with his thumb the gap that opened in her grasp when the tongs came down hard.

“Let me do that bit for you.”

“Let me tell you when to do it,” Embla said. “Hold your tongue till then, would you? If you make me mad I squeeze, and then it hurts. You'll take the price of that off the boat, I suppose.”

A while later she put the tongs down and nodded to him. He did as she told him without another word. Finn had grown up watching those two standing in a place where anybody on shore could see the weather, each pointing to the clouds and explaining to the other what made him a fool. It took him till that evening to realize how much of the noise was pleasure at finding each other still there.

They took the repaired iron down to the harbor the next day in a leather apron. Across the sound there had always been a house where someone hung a lantern in the window to welcome the early boats. Someone had taken the shutters off it for fuel. In this second winter you could tell which houses were empty by the ones that still had glass. The ones with people in them had gone milk-white from their breath freezing on the inside. Finn's father let Finn hold the new fitting fast to the timber while he took its weight. For once, when he let go, the lad did not have to call him back to take the weight again.

In Góa, the month when the birds ought to have come back, the younger twin asked to go down to the sea. She was old enough now, she said, to see how far winter had come. Finn went down with her. The tide was out. They could stand on the landfast ice, which was rough and brown with weed and old sand, and watch a completely different piece of white, clean ice go by out to sea like a country carrying its own hills with it. There were brown seals lying with their children inside the circle of their foreflippers. They were near enough for the girl to see whiskers on one pup and the look of interest it had in her. The two children waited until the tide turned. That piece of ice had gone out of sight by then.

“Does the house on it go too?”

“There isn't a house on it.”

She wiped her nose. “I meant if somebody goes away with it, what happens when it's time to go home?”

It took him some time to realize she was thinking of the people in the sail loft. “They've got to find somewhere else. Do you think you could tell those children up there what a seal looks like? I don't suppose they've ever seen one.”

On the walk home she thought of a great many things they would need to know before they ever got to the seal. You could watch an ordinary little wave lift up a slab thick enough for six children to stand on it. Salt water could run down off the ice and leave skin on a mitten that you had taken in your bare hand. Their father had to wait for the wind to leave a way through the pieces before it was any use his having a boat at all. She practiced her account on Finn, who had seen it all with her. Once they had climbed up to the sail loft and sat down with the others she said the pup had looked her in the eye, and that was all. It was the thing Finn saw when he closed his eyes that night too.

The supper that came to them from his father's next trip was flatfish fried in the last spoonful of butter his mother had been hiding from them. His father cut the fish into enough pieces for the five of them and for Embla and Virve, who had sweated it out at the forge with him. All seven licked their fingers. Of all the meals in Finn's life he would remember the taste of that one because they ate it so slowly. Even the girls knew how hard a thing it was, in that second year of winter, to put a browned fish in a child's two hands.

## Chapter Four · A Boat in the Next Cove

The third year he was twelve. There was a mark on the door frame to prove it. His father used the point of his knife to give the last mark a little boat of its own. Finn was tall enough to wind the harbor chain with a second person at the handle. He was old enough to sign for the fuel allowance at the gate, which meant going home with enough wood to dry the little girls' wet things and enough left to cook the supper if one thing was done immediately after the other.

On the morning the *White Gull* sailed, his mother said there'd be fish cheaper next week. He put his head into the room where she was rolling up the nets and said, “If there's fish at all next week it'll be because somebody goes for it this one.”

He bent to the youngest twin, who had developed a habit of putting her head under his chin to see if his beard had grown longer overnight. Finn came to him with a frayed loop of rope. It belonged with the spare wooden pin that kept an oar in place on the side where his father steered. His father kissed him on the crown in passing and went out saying something about mending it when he came home. Just an end of line, hardly long enough to do anything with. Finn kept it in his trousers and found it there every time he went looking for something else.

They'd been working together the Laugardagr before he went out. Finn took the week's wash off the rope in the yard while his father hauled the sail up to the loft to scrape salt out of its folds. Finn liked getting to the small waxy clothes pegs before his sisters did. When they had put them all away in the bag his mother kept on a nail behind the privy, he climbed into the loft. His father sat mending sail with a leather pad across one palm. It had a little iron cup in it so he could send a blunt needle through stout canvas without making a hole in his own hand. On the floor under the work lay chips of sealing tar, a short piece of chalk and the gnawed end of an oat cake on an upturned shoe.

“Would you let me come if I could get this done by myself?”

“You mean could you make your mother consent by pushing that needle through two thicknesses of cloth? I've yet to find anything that'll do it for me. I'd pay a little extra for such a needle.” His father moved his bare foot off a clean place on the floor. “Come sit. I'll tell you exactly when I'll take you. I'd like to see you take a boat out with another fellow's father in it. Suppose you both saw my boat come in. You want to go racing down to meet me. He needs time to get his old knees steady before the boat tips. You let me go by and mind the man who’s there with you. That’s when I’ll know you’re ready. When your aunt had the iron in her hand that day, I came in over her shoulder and tried to take it off her because it hurt me to look at her fingers. If I'd done it, how far do you think I'd have got with the iron?”

“Into the coals.”

“Best case. I could have burned her. You can do a dirty thing to the fellow next to you on a boat because of something you want with all your heart. You need a little time to learn when you're doing it. So do I. Do you mind if I'm the one who gets to pull the rope over your head and make you come home for supper for another summer?”

Finn picked up the heel of oat cake and blew the dust off it. “Are you eating this?”

“I had a plan for it. Go on.”

He had missed the chance to say that yes, he minded. He had had the whole week in which to go back and say it, if he had really wanted to, and now his father was at sea and that was the most recent conversation he could think of that they’d had without somebody else in the room. When Finn lay awake the first night the wind got up he kept coming back to the sight of that dirty shoe with a cake on it. Of all the things there were to remember about the man, what good would come to anybody of remembering that?

During the gale they could hear the ice breaking in the harbor. They got doors slammed and children in. Afterwards Finn and his mother went down to the shore each time there was a shout. Passing the gate in the dark he remembered the mussel seller standing barefoot by his cart after his own burial. Listening for his father’s step, Finn found it made a considerable difference whether the man coming home could still turn his head when he heard his own name. On the third walk down the lane he told his mother. She squeezed his hand once, hard. She had been thinking it too. Three boats came in under oars. A fourth came in behind the others in pieces. Nobody had seen the *White Gull* since the wind veered. On the fourth night Aunt Embla fetched a sailor home from the south gate with a split lip and one foot so swollen they had to cut her boot off.

“Her name is Rua. She can have my bed if she'll have the forge,” his aunt said. “But I've the two little ones from the cobbler's in there, and they wake and scream. She's got enough to do to get a bit of sleep.”

“The nets are on Da's bed,” Finn said quickly.

His aunt looked at him. “Yes, I had noticed that.”

Rua was awake when he brought her a little dish of broth. The name on her boat had been *Little Auk*. Of the five aboard her, three had made it to the fishing hut at Split Rock. The other two survivors were still there. One had got his leg between the boat and the shore. The second man had a hurt shoulder but was able to keep him warm. Rua had set out because she was the lightest. She had fallen on the port road and crawled the last bit.

“Split Rock?” he said. “The chimney goes sideways? My father used to put the catch there till he'd a load. Did you see a boat pulled up? Green stripe on her.”

It was plain she'd been asked about boats at every house she'd passed. She put her spoon down. “I remember picking a green board out of the water. You wouldn't know a boat from so little. Will you get somebody to go for those two? I've got to lie down before I fall down.”

There was nowhere for her to lie down in this room except the hard bench built along the wall. He helped to settle her feet on a rolled-up coat. She took his wrist, quite gently, before he could carry the broth away.

“Would you pull that poor thing by the tail?” she said.

He looked behind him. The cat had his whole head in the dish. He lifted him out of the way. Rua wanted to drink some of the broth herself. It had tasted of smoke all winter, but to hear the soft sound she made when she got it into her mouth you'd have thought she'd been given a dish of cream.

“I was ashore with our two while the boat was still there,” she said after a time. “I could see her where the water had set her down. I'd sooner have lost my hand on the rock than look at that lovely little ship heaving herself to pieces. I didn't get to do either. One of our men wanted his mother, and I was the company he had. I sat with him the first night and he talked the whole time he was awake about what a hateful woman she'd been. You see why I want somebody to get there quickly, don't you? You can be worried out of your senses about a thing and have a fair bit of ordinary living left to do.”

“Did you build the boat?”

“With my oldest sister. She says if a fellow goes to sea in something one of us has made, he ought to marry the other. We're both still unmarried.” She opened her hand enough to let him see where little white threads had worked their way in from the line she had carried to the hut. The ground-in marks lay dark across her palm. “One boat for another if it were down to me, boy. I'd pay that. Everything I know about the beach I can give you. That's all it bought us to get me ashore.”

He went to his aunt. The tide still had the path round the headland covered and the ice in the dark would cut their feet from under them. They had to wait till first light. He could help get everything ready. “I know which hut,” he said. “If Da put in there he'd have had to leave the boat. The whole beach goes out from under you when the wind changes.”

“My dear, how many times have you had him alive up and down this coast since he sailed?”

“He's shown me where he goes. I can bring you right to the door. Will you take Virve's sledge? Those other two are there. One of them might have seen him.”

His aunt put on her apron as if to go to work on an ordinary morning. She tied it extra tight. With her left hand she found the key to the shed and made a little space on her palm so he could take it. “Your mother's got to hear from your own mouth where we're going.”

Before daylight they ate standing up in the kitchen. The sky was brightening over the headland by the time they got the sledge down to the gate. It was on runners that Virve's people had been using in the wet woods for a long time before Fimbulwinter. Oda came to take a turn pulling. Nobody in the house talked about what happened at the hut if there were three live men to bring home. His mother put out three pairs of wool socks. She had to borrow the last from a neighbor who had sailors of her own still out.

Oda had brought a dry change of everything in a sack. At the top was a stone faintly warm even through two wrappings. It had spent the night warming at the edge of her father’s hearth so there would be a little heat left for a man's feet when they got there. The first half mile Finn was sure it would fall out. After that he wished she had brought a second one and that she could tell him who would be putting his feet against it tonight.

“Do you remember the day I asked you to hide that stool?” Oda said.

“It's got Virve's green wool and my sisters all over it these days.”

“I know. You did the job I wanted done. You'll have to show me the path before too long. I can't always get a foot into the same little holes you leave in the snow and there's somewhere I'm afraid I'll take a wrong turning.”

She was nineteen now. She had brought up the big water bucket and fed her father's chickens and asked the price of meal at three different mills since before Finn was the age of his little sisters. He had forgotten she could be afraid of a turning she couldn't see. Instead of going on ahead so she would have his tracks to follow, he came back and stood on each rise till she and Embla could see where he'd gone.

The sledge kept trying to twist off the path on the way up the headland. From there they could see the open water in long green rips as the pack moved against the tide. A black spot leapt up and down on the edge of it without ever getting any closer to land. He thought it was a bird. His aunt thought so, too. Then they went down the lee of the hill where it was almost warm, heaving on the runners to drag the load past big patches of bare rock. Finn got to the hut first. It stood well above the beach, with a funny elbow in its stone chimney that his father said had been built by a man who'd been turned round dancing.

Inside, a stranger was shaving another man with cold water. Of all the things he had been prepared to walk in on, that had never occurred to him.

“Got to look well when we get into town,” the man with the razor said. “Did Rua get there?”

Aunt Embla said yes. The sailor shut his eyes. He was trying to hold another man's chin with an arm that could barely get higher than his elbow. The strop had gone dark where he'd brought the razor down on his own finger.

“Is there anybody else?” Finn asked.

“Just the two of us. Arni there saw a boat making the next cove the afternoon of the blow.”

“Might have been green,” said the man laid out on the fishing table. “A long black hull and a bit of color astern. Kept the sail up till it went out of my sight.”

They had brought a sledge wide enough for the man to lie down if he drew his elbows in. His aunt took the man's hand in both of hers and spoke to him almost as soon as she saw his leg. “I've seen a smith's striker go down with one like that and dance again. It took him time. It will hurt beyond anything you've begun to guess when we lift you. I'll tell you before we do.”

Out on the headland Oda held the sledge still while Finn knocked its runners free of ice. The next cove was some way away. The path over the ridge was open. And it was bright enough still to make out the little wooden windmill on the crest, an idle thing a man had carried up there to please his children, its arms going round quite fast against the plain sky.

Aunt Embla came out. “If you're going round there, go now. Oda can go with you. I'll stay the night here if you need the light. Your father is my brother. I'll sit that watch for him gladly.”

Finn put his foot on the path that went up to the windmill. He could see the whole length of it. In an hour or so he would come into the next little bay. He had stood up there on his father's shoulders in midsummer with a piece of bread spread with butter so thick it bore the print of his thumb. The water below had been the color of a field in bloom, every jellyfish taking the long sunlight about itself as it opened and closed. His father's two hands about his ankles had been quite steady. For a small boy it was the nearest thing there could be to flying.

Behind him there came the most wretched sound he had ever heard a man make. His aunt was helping Arni into his coat. “Wait a little,” he heard the man say. “If you'll just give me a minute I'll be able to take the other sleeve.”

He came down the path.

“Fetch the sledge up close, Oda,” he said. “Let's get him home while he can still sit up.”

Oda pulled the sledge up to the door. The sailor with the hurt shoulder could walk as far as the town if Embla gave him her good arm. That left the two of them to pull Arni, who would have to lie flat on the runners for the steepest parts. His aunt nodded. He kept his back to the headland. By tomorrow the tide would have covered the beach again. A man could leave his boat pulled up on it and be walking round the headland right now with no idea how few hours of light he had. Finn would have to spend tonight knowing he had let this one go. When the others went in to help him get the man up, Finn took the short line out of his pocket and laid it on the windowsill, coiled the way his father liked. With a piece of old charcoal he wrote under it where they had taken the men and the time they had left. His father would see his writing and know.

## Chapter Five · The Good Blanket

They cleared the rocky headland in the last of the light and reached the broad port road before the tide covered the path again. From there the walls on either side were high enough to follow by touch. Three times on that road they stopped for Arni. It was nearly midnight when they brought the sledge in. The light above the town rose from the crack in the earth where it had burned since before there was a king in Hrafnborg. There were people underneath it drying their clothes in patches of warmth, quietly making room as one got warm enough to give another his place. When the sailor walking beside the sledge called out that Rua had got them home, Finn's mother looked first at the two men and then across the gate to the unbroken darkness behind him. He had to take her hand before he could get out what he meant to say.

“Somebody saw a boat making the next cove. I could have gone. I brought these fellows instead.”

His mother put her cold fingers against the back of his neck. “Give me a minute,” she said. They stood together while his aunt began to wrangle the sledge round to the house where the healer lived. When his mother was ready they went after her.

At the healer's Oda had to fetch a bowl deep enough for a man's feet. The sailor with the good leg turned out to have worn his boot so long the wool from his stocking had worked into the cracks in his heel. Everything took longer than Finn thought it should, even finding somebody who had time free to tell Rua where the two men were. While he waited his mother got down on the bench beside him. On the floor in front of them a little pool of water from his boot drew itself out along a split between the boards.

“I told your father's cousin about the cove,” she said. “He'll go up at first light with two men who came off the herring boats. I've given him your description and told him to call in at Split Rock. Would you like to come as far as the door when they leave or would you like him to go by without waking us?”

“I can show him. I went the first part of the way while it was still light.”

“Then say so at breakfast. Nobody is going to require you to give your father up this evening. Nobody could tell me to do that for anything on earth. You can still be the boy who brought those men into town.”

“Did you want me to leave them?”

“Oh, Finn.” She took his hand and laid it flat in hers. His was almost the bigger one now, but his thumb would only reach as far as the first line in her palm. “I came down to the gate and saw that empty place behind the sledge. If you'd asked me then what I wanted, I could have put my hands on those men and shaken them. Will you give me a little time to be glad for Rua? Had it been your father on that sledge with a broken leg, I'd be down there kissing the feet of the child who brought him back.”

He sat very still and let that warm hand grow slack around his. The two men from the hut were in there talking at last about something other than the next six inches of their journey, and somebody's mother was finding out from a friend at a gate that she had better come quickly if she wanted to see her boy.

At home the little ones were asleep head to foot on one mattress, his aunt’s big apron spread over their nightshirts. The spare sheets had been torn up for the people off the boats. Virve and Oda were sitting on the floor taking turns over a bowl of what looked like yesterday's supper, with the cat hanging his head over the rim. The house smelled of damp wool, the sour yeasty smell of feet bound up a long while, the good salted smell of the broth. At the hearth Rua lay in a chair. She had got the creases of her sleeve printed into one cheek from trying to make a pillow out of so little arm.

“I have to go and see them,” she told Finn.

“Morning will do. One's shaved and the other isn't. We got there right in the middle.”

She closed her eyes. He went through into the next room. His father's spare nets were stowed clear up to the bed head. It took him three trips to carry them to the shed and come back with them off his forearms, where they caught in the skin. He took the good blanket out of the chest and put the soft side to the sheet. Rua sat down, dragging her bad foot up and over with a hand under her knee. The board beneath the mattress gave the loud, familiar creak that used to send the cat flying off the bed when his father turned over on it.

She started to get up. “Did I break something?”

“That's been doing that since I was little. You'd think somebody in a family with a smith in it could have fixed it by now.”

“I could sleep in the chair.”

He tucked the blanket behind her foot where somebody had slit open the boot. The tears had come into his eyes quite without warning. There was the sound again as she settled into the hollow where his father's shoulder had lain.

“He used to whisper to the cat,” Finn told her. “He gave him the fish from our pie, and he'd whisper so my mother couldn't hear him. Have you ever known a cat tell on anybody?”

She moved a little so he could sit on the edge of the bed if he liked. From the kitchen came his mother's voice, low, pleased to have caught them at it again after all this time.

“I could hear the pair of you perfectly well,” she said.
