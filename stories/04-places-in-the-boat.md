# Places in the Boat

## Chapter One · A Place Below the Oars

The woman who got Una out of captivity had onion breath. It was one detail Una left out when telling the story to her children. She kept it for herself. When somebody had hold of your bare foot in a dark boat and you were six years old, it made a considerable difference to find the great terrible woman above you had recently eaten her supper.

“You can go under there,” the woman said. “Duck when you get to the middle. There's a nail somebody ought to do something about.”

Una went on her belly beneath the rowing bench. For three nights she had been shut up with other children in a fish house while men on the beach tried to agree a price for them. After the first night they had learned to keep quiet when grown people came to the door.

“Can I stay here till the boat stops?”

“Yes.”

“What happens if I want to stay after?”

“I suppose your father will have to come down and get me to marry him. He'll have a job of it. I don't much like island men.”

Water climbed up the little round holes where the oars went through the side. At the top of each swell a mouthful came aboard under the loose leather flaps. Una tried to lift her feet out of it until she felt the woman who smelled of onions tuck a piece of sail beneath her heels. The same woman, sitting above her, was feeding two of the other children from a bag. She could hear the crust break.

“Does she have to eat?” one of them asked.

“If she does, she'll tell us. Let her have a piece to be going on with.”

There was bread in the woman's hand when it came over the edge of the bench. Una took it while she was looking out to sea. She could see a little way up the woman's wet sleeve. When Una had been carried aboard the boat that took her away, a man had wrapped her by both elbows in his coat so his hands would be free for the ropes. His sleeve had smelled of old fish. If the woman had tried to fetch her out from under there, Una would have bitten those warm brown fingers. As it was she ate the bread a crumb at a time while they stopped, farther along the island, for a boy who knew where his grandparents lived. The woman asked at two houses before she got him to the right one. By then it was dark. Una heard the boot tread pass along the seats again. The last of the water had drained out of the folded sail and her feet were warm.

Una went home the rest of the distance with her cheek on a board still warm from the afternoon sun. At the landing her father had to go knee-deep beside the boat before he could see in under the bench. For some reason he had brought the family cat with him. The cat came aboard and walked along Una from her heels to her head. She emerged under her own power with an indignant animal fastened to her hair. The woman overhead had a laugh you could have heard on the next island.

Her name was Asa. She had been a river captain who died and was buried. Before Hel, the woman who receives the dead, she had asked to come back for the people carried off in the river wars. Una's father knew where Hel had set her down, how far she had walked to her first boat, what she had had for breakfast. His account got longer every year. He never did explain why he took the cat to the landing.

On the island of Eldøy, which was home to Una, you learned the sound of a boat long before you learned how to put one together. The loose floorboard under the empty fish tubs had a slap like a bare hand on a tabletop. Under a load of fish that board was quiet. Her father used to wake when the first boat grounded on the shingle and tell her mother from bed whether to put water on for the gutting knives or a meal on for hungry hands.

Years later her brother brought their father’s boat home low in the water on a day of fog. From the shore Una heard the oars go into the pins, then the scrape of gravel. No slap of the loose board. She went down with a fish basket on each arm. Only when Odd stood up could she see past him to the empty bow seat where a neighbor should have been. The man had slipped overboard while clearing the nets. Odd had gone in after him, come up half senseless from the cold and got a boat hook through his own sleeve for his pains. They never found the neighbor. Odd’s injuries healed. After that, anybody on the island who wanted Odd to hurry at a wet landing could go to the trouble of saying why. It was something Una knew of him that few of the others did. He was three years younger and could remember when she was too small to get in a boat herself. These things counted more between them than a captain's title.

By the time the miller king Vargeld came up out of the mountains with an army at his back, Una's father was buried on the hill and Una herself commanded a red boat with fourteen oars. Her children were grown enough to run the smokehouse. When the island council wanted a captain to carry volunteers across to Hrafnborg and tell the mainland kings how many more the islands could spare, Una had stood up before anyone else could get a word in. Her children came down to watch her take the first men aboard. There had been grain enough in the islands at sowing time. Vargeld held the grain ports now. Una had ground their last sack of seed barely an hour before the call came for men to muster at Hrafnborg.

“One condition,” she told the men from the north beach, who came up carrying their own oars. “In my boat it's customary for a man who's afraid to sing. It keeps the other fellows from feeling lonely.”

“What happens if you're afraid?”

“You'll have to sing bloody loud to be heard over me.”

Beyond the boat sheds a black line had been painted across the limewashed wall of the smokehouse at the height of her eldest daughter's shoulders. On a good day the fish came up to that mark. For two days a wren had been picking spiders off the lime underneath it. Hild had just had a child of her own. As the volunteers settled to the oars, she stepped aboard to give her mother a parcel of fish wrapped in sorrel leaves.

“I could come with you if you wanted me,” she said. “He's taking a little soft bread now. His father thinks he could get some goat's milk down him.”

“I've four over my count already. Put your man to feeding you and let the youngster have what's his a little longer.”

“I was considering what you think you have to do in the next month, Mam. Who will sail her when you go up to speak for the islands? Odd won't leave you amongst the mainland kings without somebody present to apologize.”

“When would a mainland king have found occasion to apologize to the likes of me?”

The crew laughed as she intended them to. Hild made an exact little copy of her mother's sweeping bow, then bent closer. “It's a fine thing when they laugh, isn't it? Did you hear me asking for a share of your work?”

Una had heard. She had heard her own mother saying of women who took their children to sea that somebody on the islands had to be willing to stay behind and clean things. “Will you still want the boat when the little one can climb? I'll bring her home between runs. I'd take you now if there were room.”

“Write that down on something you want back,” said Hild. She stepped off, took up the loop round the shore post and let it run down her arm. The three women on the next boat heard the promise. Her daughter knew what she was doing.

Four other captains had put off the crossing. The wind had got up against the tide. There was a dirty white break in the nearer channel, where there ought to have been deep water. Una slipped one foot out of its shoe so she could feel the first shudder of the hull in broken water. She sent Odd forward to watch the point of a rock, black among the whitecaps, off which the two of them used to fish for supper. Spray went clean over his head and came rattling down among the oars. He raised one dripping arm when the red boat had cleared the point. Una brought her round the back of the rock. In its shelter the men could change their stroke and take her into the outer channel, with the headland between them and the wind. Halfway through a dirty song the men from the north beach found themselves rowing in flat water. The crew wanted to hear how the song ended.

“Anybody have a criticism?” Una called.

Odd wrung the water out of his cap. “Next time you want a good look at that rock I'll draw you a picture.”

Out there the water looked like strips of beaten tin, dull or bright depending which way the wind had brushed it. The men who went home at night to their own fishing boats worked their oars with long, easy strokes. The men who had only fished from a beach were spending one pull in three with the blade flat on the water. The two sorts of men sat alongside each other. In a narrow hull, if one fellow got the handle in his ribs the next fellow could lose the skin off his knuckles. Odd set a slow beat by drawing the handle of his own oar up with a little grunt. Beside him a lanky boy tried to despise the old fellow's need to grunt and kept time with him all the same.

When at last there was room to set the square sail, several people went to sleep where they sat. A sail full of wind took the work of fourteen at the oars. Its bottom corner kept fretting against the rope as if a rat were gnawing there. The lanky boy was still sitting upright. Una ducked forward to ask him why.

“I need a piss,” the boy whispered. “I tried putting one leg over. Your brother caught me by the belt. Everybody saw.”

“Everybody aboard has had to piss since we left Eldøy. Wait till you see what the next fellow does when we bring out the pot. Odd! Hand it along. The women of my household would find room for lace on a fellow's underclothes before they'd ask him to go overboard for the sake of modesty.”

His name was Val. At home he could cut handles on combs with his penknife fine enough to look like ships under sail. Men on his own beach called him Little Teeth because he'd once carved too many fine teeth into one comb and a lady of some standing had walked about for a day with the whole lot lodged in her hair. By the time the pot had been emptied, rinsed and put away Una knew all about the lady. When he went to sleep he curled one preserving hand over the pocket where he kept the penknife. Odd laid a piece of sailcloth over his shoulders when the wind turned cool.

Late in the day the harbor of Hrafnborg came into sight. A chain thicker about than Val's thigh hung between the piles at the harbor mouth. Men turned a wooden drum onshore to lower it for the boats coming in. High on the slope the natural flame that had first attracted settlers to the cleft in the hill showed red in the blue of the evening. A person coming off that water would have given up a good deal for a few minutes by that fire. Una had a stiff right arm, one bare foot chilled past feeling and a great elastic pleasure in her chest, as though the whole voyage had been spent getting a full breath.

They ate hot fish on the quay at Hrafnborg. Val waited till the others had gone up to the sheds to offer her a coin with a hole in it for the fare. Una slid it back along his belt. “Buy your mother something ugly as sin when you get back. She'll keep it till she dies.”

Odd found her there once everybody had gone ashore, pulling a little splinter from the place where Val's fingers had bled on the oar. He put the back of a hand to her elbow to let her know he'd come without startling her in the dusk. “You've been counting since we put in. How many?”

“All eighteen I took aboard. I always count them going off. I like seeing every pair of feet hit dry land.”

“I'd an idea that's why you wanted the job,” he said. “You could have told those lads on the beach. There were boys there still making up their minds when you started the singing.”

“Would they have come?”

“I don't know. You might like to let a fellow say yes before you get him to laugh himself onto a bench.”

She folded Hild's piece of sorrel round the last strip of smoked fish, shared it with him and sat tasting the bite of the leaf and the fat against her tongue. At the water stairs the tide had turned. It would carry her out tomorrow to collect another load, and on that shore there were people she had known since they were little waiting to find out if they'd done right in putting a boy like Val in her hands.

## Chapter Two · The Commander

For two campaigning seasons Una took men along the southern coast to Ormsund, the port at the mouth of the Ormá, and up the broad river as far as a loaded boat would go. At the beginning the banks were green. Boys on horseback raced her for miles. By the second autumn she could smell an abandoned village before she saw its chimneys. There were whole companies now that went aboard and to sleep without a word to her.

In Heyannir, the hay-cutting month of the first summer, she brought Hild out as promised. By then the little fellow could feed himself and anybody who came within throwing distance. They put him ashore with his father at Hrafnborg, and Hild took the red boat round the southern headlands while Una slept the longest sleep she could remember taking. At the mouth of the Ormá they bought milk from a girl with a goat on the end of a tether in exactly the place where last time they had been able to float. The brown river was forever bringing parts of the uplands down with it and putting them in new places in the estuary.

“She's our river when we're looking at a chart,” Hild said. “When you want to find out how deep she runs, you're asking after the goat.”

Once past Ormsund, the rows of war tents on either bank went on for miles. Straight pine trunks still had the cut ends of branches on them where men had dragged them into the water to make landings. A wharf in a town was level with a loaded boat on most tides and had old cork buoys and hides tied alongside to keep the hull from grinding against the stone. Here the boat went up and down against green bark that peeled and left streaks the color of butter along her red sides. Soldiers sat under the trees waiting their turn with their books of songs and their mothers' stockings rolled up in their packs. Una's crew had to learn all over again the smell of the river at night after coming up out of salt water. There was a sweetness to this mud, and the drone the gnats made over still water was like a private complaint every rower had learned to make inside his own skull.

One still night she woke to the sound of a wooden cup knocking twice. It was a signal the crew had settled on for a hand up without waking their passengers. A trooper who had stayed on his feet all day to let his friends take the dry seats was unable to get over the side. Odd had the cup in one hand. The man had his arms round Hild's neck and his boots sunk in the soft riverbed between the boat and the logs. The nearest solid footing was up beside Hild's knees. All Una could see of him was the top of his head and the bright naked skin between his belt and the hem of his jacket. He had already asked Hild four times if he had strained her back.

“I can fetch a couple of his friends,” Una said.

“I promised him he could get out without the whole company seeing his backside,” said Hild. “He's done us a good turn all day. Hold the gunwale still with your foot while I pull his jacket down over him.”

Between them they got him ashore. Una found his bag where he'd used his two bread cakes to stop a buckle rattling against another man's boot. He had tickled little black lines in the cakes, the names of the villages they'd passed. She handed him his bag, laid her shawl over his shoulders and sat with him while the feeling came back into his legs. He wiped his face on one woolen corner.

That winter on the island she made Odd eat supper at her house every Laugardagr, washing day, when everything in it from the lamps to the children was polished. They had found it easier to talk of the places they'd been if she put his hands to getting the small bones out of herrings for the children. He wanted Hild to have more of the helm come spring. Una had often thought that the best thing she could do for her daughter was bring the boat back in one piece so it would be hers. Odd asked her to remember how long she'd been willing to wait for her own father's boat.

“My teeth hurt with wanting it,” she admitted.

“I know. The neighbors all knew. He knew, for that matter. I stood by him on the beach the first evening you took her out without him. You remember what happened?”

“I ran the stern aground. You could hear him swearing all across the bay.”

“Well, what I remember is having to keep hold of his arm. He wanted to get on and do it for you, and I made him watch till you got her off. I would have liked an older brother to do that for me.”

In the second season, when they were collecting the island boats that would serve the armies all the way up to the hills, Una put Hild's name beside hers on the red boat's steersman's board. The clerk asked which name he was to cross off. She made him go and get a wider piece of wood.

It was on that river she saw Asa again. Una had put on the good red coat she wore to solemnize a marriage or settle a bet. The command tent at the landing was full of officers. Their army had been pushing inland for two years to reach the pass through which Vargeld supplied his people. Carters coming down had seen the people Vargeld kept underground there making and mending gear for his army. Some had been taken from their homes so young they could barely remember daylight. Above them at Titan's Stair a flight of steps went clean across the mountains. All Una saw of it from the river was the white shoulder of a peak above the willows. Tomorrow somebody in that tent would have to order people up those steps.

Asa sat on a meal barrel with a strip of leather round a cracked shoe and a bowl of peas going cold on her knee. Una waited for a speech. Asa asked the laundress by the door how many sheets had come back from the wash.

“Are you expecting company in clean beds?” Una asked. Some of the men grinned.

“I am. What's your name?”

Una gave it. “I used to be one of your passengers. You took me out of a fish house and carried me to the islands.”

“Did you get home?”

It took Una a moment. She had thought it so likely Asa would enjoy meeting the girl who had grown up to be a captain that she had brought a second bottle of her own blackberry wine. “Yes. That's a daughter of mine I can hear out there haggling over a sail. My son-in-law made the sail, which puts him at something of a disadvantage.”

Asa set her peas down. “Will you take charge of a barge for me tomorrow? I've people going into the mountains enough. I have nobody I trust coming back.”

“I've just brought a fast boat upriver. Ask me what I can do with her.”

“I asked the people on the bank. They told me.”

“Then give me the men your army needs. I'll have them up to the old landing before the others are awake.”

A small woman sitting on an upturned pail tried to get a word in. Asa let Una finish.

“My people came to fight,” Una said.

“So did I. In the morning I go up with them. The barge has a broad floor and a high side. Everyone who has looked at her has told me what a great many people she can take. Meret, will you tell the captain what your brother does for a living?”

“Hauls stone,” the woman on the pail said. “He fell down a cutting once. To get him over the side of that barge I'd have to stand him up on the leg he'd broken.”

Una put down the bottle of wine. She could see it now, the last two feet of the lift, somebody trying to push from underneath. “You want the old wool wharf. The barge was built for wool. There's a door cut in her side so the men can roll the bales straight in from that low wharf.”

“That was what I came to ask about,” said Meret. “Do you suppose we could try it with a live man to see where he'd scrape?”

Meret had brought the worn leather belt her brother had been wearing on the afternoon he fell. She set it down in Una's hand. One of the metal loops was bent like the lip of a soft pewter cup. “Four nice men on a work crew did that trying to get him out of the pit by his belt. He never would let me straighten it. Said it was a figure that might come in handy if anybody asked me how much a man weighs when he can't get himself up.”

Una ran her thumb inside the metal. “All right. Try me.”

At the wharf Meret and four volunteers laid her in a blanket between two poles. At the door the front two had to step sideways onto the deck. There was barely room to turn their elbows inside the rail, and the two on the wharf had the whole of her weight to hold. Una spent an instructive minute looking at the sky while five strangers decided which bit of her they could decently get hold of. Odd unpinned the little length of rail inside the door and they went straight through the second time. When she came off the poles she had marks in both her palms from trying to help herself. Odd had a hand under her back. “Now tell me where to stand when a man's legs give out and he needs me,” he said. “I need to have it right. I keep thinking of that poor lad trying to get ashore in the mud with his arms round Hild’s neck.”

Meret was already showing Hild where on shore she wanted the men with water to stand. The two of them had got to work as soon as there was an agreed piece of work to do. Something about the sight put Una in mind of the woman who had filled empty places in a boat with other people's children while their captors ate their supper. She hadn't intended to keep anyone waiting for her vanity. It did seem she had.

“Give the red boat to Hild in the morning,” she said to Odd. “She can fetch the last companies to the head of navigation. You come with me if you will. I'll need a fellow who can hear a cup knock under that whole lot of wounded.”

Her brother had the good sense to let her walk back to the tent alone.

“You could have told me what you wanted,” she said to Asa.

“I didn't know how to get a big man off a wharf without hurting him. Now I do.” Asa had at last got a spoonful of her peas. She offered the bowl to Una. “You were expecting someone else, weren't you?”

“Somebody with a bit more noise to her. The story my father told, you kicked the door off that fish house.”

“Did he say why we waited until those men had gone down for a drink? Took me three afternoons to find out when they went.”

“He'd got it so you fought seven of them. And a bear, when he'd been drinking.”

“How fine to have had your father.”

She had wanted to put her arms round the woman and make much of all they had in common. It vexed her to find she could call to mind a stranger's breath from a distance of forty years and the stranger could look her in the face and see a captain who might be of use tomorrow. Asa pushed the peas between them so each could reach. Una opened the second bottle. Asa drank half a cup and corked the rest herself. She had promised to sit up with the grain carters when they came in. “They tell me things at supper,” she said. “In the morning they tell me everything went well.”

## Chapter Three · Downriver

By noon the following day Una was singing the filthy song from the north beach to a man with a broken thigh. It took the healers three verses to get him settled on the barge. He filled in the part of the second verse she'd never learned. Then he put out a trembling hand. In it lay a coin with a hole.

“I've seen you before,” she said.

“She likes a comb with ships on it. At the market in Hrafnborg. I left my knife up on the mountain. If I'm still laid up, you'll see she gets a comb?”

She put his hand back on his chest with the coin in it. She had given him his fare back two summers ago. He had made the long march since, eating and sleeping and getting his hair cut in any number of damp places, and carried the thing the whole way.

“Tell me your mother's name,” she said. “I'd like to be able to find her.”

His name was Val, hers was Hervor, and she was particular about combs. He would have liked to explain this at some length. Meret, who was helping him drink, gave Una a look over the cup. This was a man with a good chance of explaining things to his mother himself. Una realized she had been braced since morning to be given last messages. It came as a relief to have a tiresome passenger aboard.

Halfway downriver on the first loaded passage she began to wish she'd taken time to be afraid. The barge went on moving after a stroke of the steering oar much longer than her own boat did. To the men who had to lie still on the floor the little correction she made at a bend, and the answering swing, were two separate journeys, each with its own little private burst of pain. Val let out his breath through his teeth every time. She could hear that from the steering platform. By the next bend she had her brother at her elbow.

“Would you go down and count how long he's quiet when I let her come round slowly? Tell me what his hands do when he hurts. He won't say for fear I'll think he's a baby in front of my crew.”

“I can do a better thing for you,” Odd said. “Give me her for an hour and you go sit with him. I take a better quiet turn than you do even when you're sober.”

Val's hands looked like a carver's. His nails wore away to nothing on the first two fingers of his working hand. Half asleep he rubbed his thumb against the rag that covered the wound across his thigh. “You have your brother watching me because I complained.”

“I had him watching because I wanted to know what this old tub feels like down here. She has all the delicacy of a sow in a churchyard. Have you room beside you for a steering lesson?”

He moved a little on the bedding to make room and she hadn't the heart to tell him to keep still and hurt by himself. Lying alongside a passenger on a working boat was an experience she remembered entirely from under the rowing bench. She had remembered the plank against her cheek all her life. How old had the woman above her been? Old enough to be tired and take the next watch anyway. She lay till they reached the next turn and when the bottom went from sliding sidewise to coming easy with the current she saw his fingers spring apart on the cloth. Odd had brought her round in one long pull. She could tell where they were by the wet gray light on the willow leaves above her. The rest of that trip she let him take the bends.

They had the wounded from Stairwatch all afternoon. The little town stood at the foot of the pass, a wagon ride from the river. Vargeld’s men had driven the first of Asa’s companies back down the steps into its streets. From there the wounded came to Una by road while a second allied army climbed the pass from the opposite side of the mountains. Many Veilfolk families, Meret’s among them, had traveled here with shelters they could take down and carry. All day there had been men coming out along the road in litters made of tent poles and bedding. By evening the families who had made them would have nothing to sleep under. A wagon turned up at the wharf with all the great awnings from the grain market, rolled and tied. The driver asked where Commander Asa had wanted them put. Within an hour Meret’s aunt had a merchant’s big green awning up on borrowed poles and three exhausted families asleep under it.

One of the young soldiers from the island had come back down the road with a litter across his two elbows and a boy's swollen hand tight round his hair. The boy could have let go but he hadn't yet understood that part of the journey was over. Una weighed the awning cloth in her hand. “Do you know what it costs to fit one of these over a whole market stall? My aunt traded a dry summer's fishing for one half this size. Somebody will want it back.”

“I'd like my boots back,” said Meret. “A woman went downhill in my boots yesterday and hasn't come up again. If you want somebody to search the camp tonight for a piece of canvas, I'm not applying.”

“Who'll take the blame if a merchant asks?”

“Read his mark. Each awning's still got his mark. Asa sent word he can name his price for mending it after the fighting. She'll answer for it out of the winter stores. If I see the woman who has my boots I'm going to thank her for taking them when she needed them, but I'll stand over her just the same until she gets them off her feet.”

“Did Asa send for these today?” Una asked.

“Three days ago. She gave me a devil of a time about the pegs.”

On her second trip upriver for another load she found Asa at the wool landing. She had come down on a relay horse and was waiting for a company of island reserves to march up with her. Meret met her with a scrap of paper covered in mud. Her brother was still missing. She wanted to know who had held the end of the lane at the fall of Stairwatch. Asa could give her a company. It would have to do. Somebody had brought the company cook along with a wounded wrist. Asa held his dinner for him while he looked over the handwriting. He remembered a stone hauler going down to the laundry sheds along the river in the last wagon. He remembered the enormous boots and the poor man apologizing for taking up so much room.

Meret went tearing down the path the barge horses usually took along the bank, still in her stocking feet. Before she was out of sight the cook had eaten half his supper one-handed. Una had a fair idea now why Asa had held his bowl instead of telling him she wanted the whereabouts of a soldier this instant. He'd been asked after so many people that it was the first time anybody there had waited long enough to let him remember a pair of boots. Una would have got a stirring shout out of him, if he could still shout, and he'd have been alone with his cold dinner as soon as the crowd turned away. Asa ate what was left of the cook's supper when he offered it. Her reserve captains had got their people together. She gave them the hour they were due up on the heights and left each of them to work out how early they had to start. A few got it wrong and she waited, listening, until their own men put them right.

“I'd have picked them up by the ears,” Una said when the others had gone.

“I should like to see you do that to the big one.”

“What would you have done if his men hadn't spoken?”

“Asked the youngest what time he'd want his breakfast. The boy knows how far he walked yesterday.”

Asa was trying to get up on the saddle. She had been on her feet long enough for the leather to cut through her stocking where it bound her shoe. Una made a step for her with both hands.

“I don't remember you,” said Asa, looking down at her. “You were hurt by that yesterday. I'd like to have been able to give you what you came for.”

Una got very busy adjusting the stirrup leather. “Do you think you can remember to come and tell me tomorrow if they win?”

“If I'm able.”

Word came down in the night. Asa’s people had fought their way up from Stairwatch to join the allied army coming over the crest. Vargeld was dead. Meret heard it on the wharf and ran down to tell her brother, who had exactly the temper on him you would expect of a stone hauler found in a laundry shed wearing someone else's smallclothes. At first light Una went downriver with sixty injured people, Meret and the dirty shirts of a company that had ceased to exist. At the low wharf downriver she found Val awake on the bank. He had asked the orderlies to turn his bed toward the water. He raised a hand, and she brought the barge close enough for the two of them to exchange news without shouting.

## Chapter Four · What the River Took

All winter the allied armies kept the mouths of the mountain workings while the last captives were brought out. Una ran the barge. When the river skinned over at the slack edges, Odd had the last of the oars aboard every evening before the dripping blades could freeze to the landing. Meret knitted under the awning. Her brother had gone back to work and sent a parcel of good felt from the quarry where they lined their clogs against the cold. Meret cut a pair of insoles for Asa. She drew round Una's feet because Una had worn the commander's spare stockings once and found them a fair fit. It pleased Una to have acquired by ordinary acquaintance a piece of information so completely absent from her father's account of Asa.

Early in Góa, the month the birds usually came back to the islands, a company brought down nine people from the workings. Una made a great occasion of welcoming them. There were cakes of meal with a bite of dried apple in each, and a cedar chest full of clothes including her good wool dress. One of the nine stayed on the bank rolling his cake between his palms. He was old enough to shave. The too-small jacket he wore ended a whole hand's breadth above each wrist.

“Where are you bound, sweetheart?” she asked. “I can have you anywhere down this river by tomorrow.”

The others were talking and shaking out their clothes. He said something like a place name. She repeated it loudly up to Odd. Two of the others turned and looked. The boy dropped his cake into the river.

Asa, waiting on the wharf for a lift, got up and took him round the side of the shed. Una could see their feet below the wall where the boards had shrunk. The boy's feet came to rest at last, toes turned inward, dust from his shoes falling on the clean snow. When Asa brought him back he had her felt insoles tucked under his arm. She asked Una if she could make an extra stop at a dyer’s landing halfway to Ormsund. She would go ashore with the boy there.

“As far as I'm concerned you can have the boat. What was it I did to him out here?”

“He's been making handles for their hammers since he was seven. He told you the name of the village where his father sold him. He thought you were asking where he belonged.”

An hour before, Una had pictured herself running up the beach with this boy at her side, to a house where somebody must surely be looking out of a window the way her father had. The thought of herself shouting the name up to Odd turned her stomach. “Where is it he wants to go?”

“Ask him once you’re away from the others. I told him about the dyer’s landing because he asked how far he could get on foot if he got out there. He has an aunt inland. He spent a whole year once with her girls. Remembers a lime tree outside her door.”

Una spent the first part of the passage with him. There had been a bee hole in that tree about the height he had been at four. His cousin had got her thumb stuck in it looking for honey and hadn't uttered a sound for fear she'd have to share. He had walked all the way round her, he'd been so interested in seeing the look on her face from several different directions. By the time they reached the landing he had described the mill on the stream beside the house well enough for one of the grain carters to recognize it. His aunt still lived there. One of the girls had married that carter's boy. He could go up tomorrow with their wagon.

Asa and Una ate their apple cakes that night in the bottom of the empty barge. A piece of old fleece wedged under a board to stop the draft had come loose. They could hear the water through it, slurring past the length of the hull like somebody running a finger along a line of writing.

“I keep trying to introduce myself to you at six,” Una said. “It doesn't occur to me half the time that you might want an introduction to the present article.”

“I should rather like to see this island people keep asking me to send their pay home to. Is there anywhere a woman can sleep there without hearing eighteen of your captains arguing where to put her?”

“You could have my eldest's room. Her boy comes in the morning and opens your eyelids to see what color you are underneath. About as much privacy as you're likely to get.”

Asa pulled a bit of dried apple out of her teeth. “My sister's child used to run one little cold finger along my gums. What age do you think that girl would be by now?”

Una hesitated. Sea songs spoke of the Barrowbound walking out of their graves to keep the promises they had made before Hel. There was little in the songs about working out the ages of living people who had been babies on the day you died. “Tell me when she was born,” she said.

They counted on the back of a grain tally, putting marks for summers the two of them could pin to an event. The niece, if she lived, would be a woman with grown children. In the thin morning sun the little scratches were still there in the wood beside Una's hand. She left them where they were. When Hild came up the river with a chest of warm shoes and news of the island, Una put her on watch while she slept and heard her daughter and Asa talking easily under the awning. She could distinguish Asa's laugh now through a plank of oak.

The day the last prisoners came to the river, Asa wrote the order sending the armies home. Una stood behind her at the writing table, making rude observations about the length of it. A boy took it down the wharf at a run. The soldiers cheered as he went by. Some had shaved for the occasion. As the last wagon emptied, Asa sent a rider downriver to Ormsund with a second copy of the order. Una remembered how quickly it had grown dark the day her father had come down to the water with a cat inside his coat, and how carefully he had plainly shaved to be seen by a girl of six.

She was carrying the table back aboard when she heard a horse come hard through the shallows. Three riders broke out of the brush on the far bank. All winter there had been men out of Vargeld's companies living hungry in the gullies. These had come to the crossing at the worst possible moment. One rider drew on the men unloading the wagon. A soldier on the near bank shouted to the others to get the families under cover. An arrow passed through the skin of the drum with which a small boy was welcoming his father home. The boy stood looking into the hole. Asa was standing under the awning with a young prisoner who could barely keep his feet. He was trying to lift the feet of an older prisoner over the sill. Asa got the older man aboard and pushed the young one through the loading door after him. Una saw the next arrow strike her high in the side. She saw it come through the front of her gray coat. Then the river heaved the barge away and back, and she was on her knees on the boards with Asa against her.

Odd took the boat and got her clear. Meret got the coat away and did what she could. The two women looked at one another. Una would have given every good tooth in her head to be ignorant for five minutes longer.

“It's the boat, isn't it?” Asa whispered. “I can feel her going.”

“Yes, love.”

“You never called me that before.”

“I hadn't finished being cross with you. Give us a little time yet.”

Asa drew a breath and concentrated on getting it out. “Tell me about going home. The first time. Tell me something you can remember.”

Una lay down so Asa could see her without lifting her head. She told her how the nail under the rowing bench had caught her head when she crawled out. Her father had sewn up the scratch on her scalp with the same linen he used to mend the cuffs of her dresses. For weeks afterward he had sat outside her bedroom when she woke sweating, and soon she could go back to sleep listening to him scratch himself. Then one night she'd gone a whole night through. In time she had married. There were three children. The youngest could lift her mother clean off the ground. Her father's little boat was hauled up in a kitchen garden nowadays with chives growing out of the seams.

“I owe you all that,” Una said. “I came up the river wanting you to remember a small girl. I ought to have told you what happened to her. You didn't get to come home and see.”

“I'd have eaten at your table,” Asa said.

“You'd have got the chair with the good bottom.”

“I'd have liked that, Una.”

Behind them somebody began to hush the people aboard. Una had sworn her wedding vows into a headwind before Njoror, the Sea-King her people asked to bring them home. She spoke up now, in the full voice that carried the length of a tossing boat.

“Njoror, you had my father's father and you'll have me one day. Hear what I'm giving while it's mine to give. Asa, the passage you gave me is yours to give again, out of my hands. As long as I can float a boat, whoever's standing on the shore frightened and far from their own can call on me in your name or in no name at all. I'll take them, though I've a whole tide's work to unload to make them room. I'll find out where they want to go. If the fare's beyond them I'll pay it. When I'm too old to steer I'll sit on the quay and find them somebody who can. When they ask what I'll take for the last crossing I ever make, the fare will still be yours to give.”

Asa's hand moved a little on the boards. Una put her cheek into it. A bit of rough skin caught under her eye.

“Will there be room for a cat?” Asa asked.

“Oh, you damned woman. You did remember.”

“Just now,” she said.

Her hand grew heavy against Una's face. Una held it there for some time after Meret put a blanket over the two of them. From beneath the blanket she could hear her brother at the steering oar, crying quite openly, carrying the boat on down the Ormá.

## Chapter Five · Free Passage

In the first spring of peace Una took three bales of good dyed wool off her red boat. The merchant in Ormsund made her come inside and set her mark against the charge for keeping them. In a room that smelled richly of oak bark and old wool he wrote down what she owed for storage until she could fetch the wool. She paid him then and there. The price of another trip back for it would come out of her own pocket, too.

Hild had been waiting on the step. “You needn't go in and do that on your own in future,” she said. “We've been partners on paper a good while now.”

“Love, I gave away my fare. You never said your boy would eat at half rate because his grandmother promised a lady a passage.”

“My boy eats whatever he finds in reach and makes up the difference in sand. I didn't say I'd give away his dinner. I said you could talk to me. You could have asked me what we were doing with the combs.”

She opened a long drawer under the foredeck. Wrapped in the clean scraps of sailcloth were two dozen combs, the handles carved like ships under sail. Val and his mother had taken a room at the sailmaker's. Hild had agreed to take their work home to the islands for a share of the sale. A man still too lame to go out after fish could live quite nicely ashore if people cared to take the trouble to remember what he did well. Hild let Una see the price.

“Mercy on us. I could have stayed on the island and combed my head in public for a living.”

“Mother, you couldn't sell combs with that head if you carried gold in your ears. Go on up and see Hervor before we sail. Tell her which one you want for your own.”

Val’s mother picked one out for her. She put it through Una's hair herself, holding the tangles up off her scalp so the teeth would come through without hurting. Her hands were broad and polished at the fingertips like her son's. Una had turned over a number of nice things to tell the woman about getting Val down the river alive. She sat under those hands and let herself be thanked. There were families on the quay at Ormsund trying to get back to the islands after a winter in the grain sheds, and they had all the belongings of their old households and their winter ones besides. Her brother held up a four-legged stool. Una ducked and let him set it upside down on her head. “Anyone else putting a roof on a new house?” she called. The people waiting laughed and came aboard.

Halfway across she heard a small sneeze where a sneeze had no business coming from. She knelt on the boards and looked under the rowing bench. A girl in a grown man's coat was down there, one eye visible between two woolen scarves. In her arms she held a chipped blue dish.

“Would you like me to mind that?” Una asked.

“It's my mother's. It's the last blue one.”

“I know a thing or two about breaking dishes. You've picked the safest place on this boat.”

“Do I have to come out?”

“You can stay there till she stops. And after, if you like.”

She started to get up. One small hand came out and caught the edge of her red coat.

“Could you stay down here?” the girl said.

Now that was something she had never thought to ask. Una called up to Hild and heard her daughter's easy answer from the steering oar. Then she got down on her belly with the girl and the dish between them. All these years there had been room enough for two.
