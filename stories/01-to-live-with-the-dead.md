# To live with the dead

## Chapter One · Under the hull

My earliest memory is of the underside of a boat. I was lying on my back in the shavings. There was a knot gone from one of the planks, and a penny of sunlight moved across my wrist when I moved my hand. On the other side my mother was trimming a new plank. I knew her adze by ear years before she let me lift it. Two bites, then the scrape where she brushed the curls of wood away with the flat of the blade. I put my finger through the hole. She kissed the end of it.

“There you are,” she said. “I’ve promised this gentleman a boat by supper. What do you mean by lying down on the job?”

I remember his laugh and the smell of the apple he peeled for me. I couldn’t tell you his name. My mother was Aldis of Cairn Ness, and people would row halfway down Lake Urdvatn with water coming up round their boots just to have her tell them where it was getting in. They generally got told what was wrong with the way they rowed it as well.

I live in Cairn Ness still. My house stands on the western headland, where the land rises steeply enough from the lake that a woman at an upstairs window can look straight down into an empty chimney pot on her neighbor’s roof. From my kitchen I can see the burial ground and, beyond it, the eastern shore. There the reeds grow a long way out into the shallows. The roofs you see above them belong to Reedbank. The people over there caught most of the fish we ate when I was small. We had the good timber and the deep landing. We buried their dead. My mother’s oldest friend, Thora, lived over the water, and on wash days I could tell by the shirts strung across her gable whether her brothers were home.

Our deep channel stays open most winters, though a wind off the mountains can put an end to visiting in an hour. One winter, long before anyone quarreled about the dead, we stood on our side and watched the roof lift off the smokehouse at Reedbank. It went up in one piece. Underneath, the herring went on swinging from the rafters, hundreds of little silver bellies all turning in the snow. I was young enough to laugh. Mother made me put my boots on. By the time we got down to the landing, three households had brought sledges and one had brought a door with the hinges still on it. That door made the best sledge of the lot. Its owner set me in charge of the loose hinge pins. I walked with both fists tight inside my mittens, knowing the woman couldn’t put her front door back on that night if I lost so much as one of them. We spent two days bringing fish round the head of the lake to smoke them in our own houses. Our blankets smelled of herring till summer. When my mother broke her wrist the next year, Thora came across and did our washing for six weeks. Neither woman ever mentioned the fish.

I was apprenticed to Mother at twelve. By nineteen I could fit a plank so snug the lake had to swell it very little to make it tight. I liked that part. I liked the wages and hearing her refer people to me when they asked about a repair I had done. For months after I knew I wanted to leave, I let her measure out floor space for my own bench.

It was a woman called Bera who made the decision hard to put off. She lost her husband through the ice. When they found him, I kept his two little girls with me in the passage of the burial house so their mother could go in and see him first. The older girl had brought a comb. Her father had a part on the left, she told me. Everybody got it wrong. She asked three times if she could go and show them, and each time her aunt hushed her. In the end I took the comb and went in with her. She climbed onto a stool and made that part herself. All the way home she talked to me about a pup they were getting when it was weaned.

I went back the next morning to return the stool. His younger girl had needed it to reach a high bed in her aunt’s house. Yrsa, who kept the burial house, was struggling to pull a clean shirt on a dead man. His left arm had set crooked years before, and the neck of the shirt kept catching on his ear.

“Would you give us a hand a minute?” she asked. “Mind that corner. His daughter made the shirt when her eyes were a deal better than they are now.”

My own hands were well acquainted with the movements you make to get a work smock over a woman who has fallen asleep halfway out of it. I had done that for my mother half my life. Between us we got the old fellow dressed. Yrsa stood back so I could set his ear right where the shirt had folded it over. I was surprised to find myself telling him the worst of it was over. Yrsa took her cold tea up from the windowsill. “I find myself talking to them, too,” she said.

The burial house always wanted another pair of hands. I started coming in before the yard opened. By the first long evening of the year, its keeper had offered me a place. I stayed out in the yard that night rasping an oar that needed no more taken off it.

“You’ll have a spoon soon,” Mother said from the door.

I put the rasp down. “Yrsa has asked me to work with her. I told her I would.”

My mother sat on the trestle. For a moment all I could see was the pale strip of wall where she had moved the wood rack to make room for me. “I should have talked to you before I gave her my answer,” I said. “I kept meaning to.”

“Well, I hope you got her to say what she pays.”

“I thought you’d be hurt.”

“I am a little. I’d got used to having my dinner with you.” She picked up the oar and sighted down it. “Rann, I ate at Bera’s last week. Do you know what she remembers about the day we found her man? Everybody in the house kept calling her poor Bera, and you were the only one who remembered she took milk in her tea.”

“I’ve known her all my life.”

“So have the rest of us. And that lass with the comb. Any of us would have made a better job of his hair. You saw she needed to do it herself. You have a strong stomach and a pair of hands people trust near a hurt. You can keep your mouth shut when a woman is trying to find the word for something. I’ve never altogether enjoyed that last quality when I’ve wanted a little news.”

I had turned my face away. She let me get it under control.

“Whereas,” she said, “you promise a boat to anybody who asks, you forget to charge cousins, and you insist on giving a name to every crook of oak. I had to ask your permission to cut up poor old Bent Auntie. How’s a woman meant to make a living under those conditions?”

I laughed into her shoulder. She folded me up, shavings and all, and for a good while neither of us let go. Her chin rested on my head the way it had when I was small. I could feel her laughing still.

## Chapter Two · A guest of the house

I had worked beside Yrsa for nearly twenty years before I heard of anyone walking home from a grave. The first I heard was from a boy with a bucket of eels. His aunt had been buried up the shore. Yesterday, he informed me, she had come home and objected to what her sons had done with her good bed. I bought the eels and told him he ought to have more regard for his aunt now that she was dead. Before a month was out I wished I knew where to find him and ask how his aunt was getting on in that bed.

Yrsa and I were airing linen on the wall when a man I had helped lay out the previous autumn came up the steps. His name was Hedin. I knew the set of his shoulders before he turned round. He had died in a fall of stone, and on his left hand two fingers were joined from the middle knuckle, a thing I had discovered trying to ease a clean glove onto him.

“My brother’s boys,” he said. “Their mother used to live by the lime shed. Are they there yet?”

I dropped the sheet. He stooped to pick it up, saw me step back, and left it on the ground.

“I’m sorry about your washing,” he said.

I had spoken over this man the words my grandmother had taught my mother and my mother had taught me. I had asked Hel, who keeps the dead, to receive Hedin as a guest at her table. I was as sure of having been heard that autumn morning as I had ever been sure of anything. Here was Hedin wiping his nose with the back of that joined hand.

Yrsa told him the children he wanted were at their mother’s old house. He thanked her. I watched him go down the steps one at a time, favoring the leg that had broken under the rock.

“You spoke as though he’d come in from cutting hay,” I said.

“He asked where his nephews were. I knew.”

“Was he dead when we buried him?”

“Yes, Rann. I sat with his wife, and you helped me wash him. We kept him three nights. You can set that fear down.”

It was something she was good at, putting her finger on the thing you were ashamed even to ask. The rest was harder. People were beginning to call such folk Barrowbound. They said an oath to Hel had brought them out of the grave. I wanted to know whether the words I had said could have been wrong, or whether I ought to go up to his grave and say them again.

Yrsa shook out the soiled sheet. “When my little girl died I wanted to cut a lock of her hair. My mother told me Hel would send her through the door exactly as I sent her in. I spent half a night with the shears in my lap, frightened she’d be ashamed of the bare patch among strangers. In the morning I put the shears away. There has scarcely been a day since I wouldn’t have given a week of my life for a lock of that hair.”

Yrsa was rubbing her thumb over a little nick in the doorpost. There were others below it. I had always supposed they came from catching the washstand against the door when we carried it outside to scrub it. She laid her hand flat against the highest nick and held it there a moment. Meg had been very keen on getting tall enough to reach the soap.

“All the years I’ve been here,” I said. “You never said.”

“You see why I say it today? If I tell a woman that a dead child has need of all her hair, and she believes me because I wash the dead for a living, it’s her child she goes home without. I’ve quite a lot to say about how to ease a glove on a stiff hand. After that I hope somebody will tell me when I ought to shut my mouth.”

“What would you tell another mother?” I asked.

“I’d fetch the shears. I’d sit with her either way. I can say what it was to lose my Meg. I have no more recollection of being dead than you have.”

That left me with the sheet to wash and considerably more to think about. When I got home that evening Mother was shaving a plug for the hole in the bottom of our rain barrel. She made room for me on the step.

“I put him in the earth,” I told her. “I held his wife up while they filled it in. What am I to say to her when she asks why he’s gone to find his brother’s boys and hasn’t come looking for her?”

“Has she asked you?”

“No. But she’ll come. They come to me when the door shuts and they can’t bear the quiet. I tell them their kin have gone on ahead and there’s a place by the fire for the ones that follow. That’s what you told me when Grandmother died. Did you believe it?”

“I still do.” She tried the plug with her thumb. A little bead of water gathered under it. “I hope my mother’s got a place there. She could never abide a cold backside.”

“I’m serious.”

“So am I, love.” She put down the knife. “What frightens you about Hedin?”

I thought about his dirty cuff and the linen lying at his feet. “That I let somebody take him back. That a man’s brother can leave two little ones and a widow, and the work follows Hedin clear into his grave. Our people promise you rest when we put you down. Someone ought to keep that promise.”

“Did he tell you anybody took him?”

“He said he’d come for the boys.”

“Perhaps he was fond of them.”

“Of course he was. That’s how you get a good man to work past his strength. You tell him somebody needs him.” I might have done better to say less. My mother had spent the best part of her own strength on me, and we both knew it.

She rolled the plug slowly between her palms. “When I make a boat I get to decide a fair amount. I know the depth she’ll draw with two people in her. I know what angle to give the keel to keep her steady going across this foul wind. Nobody can make me pass off a rotten plank. But once she’s in the lake? Thora can take her into the reeds and use her for a duck blind if she likes. She can row across to me for a cup of tea. I don’t own her day on the water just because I built her boat.”

“I don’t think the dead are boats.”

“Neither do I. I’ve seen you do with your hands what I’d be proud to do with mine. You gave his wife a husband she could look at once more. You sent him decently to Hel. Did Hel undertake to tell the women of Cairn Ness what she would do once she had him?”

I picked up her knife and cut the wet splinter from the plug. “If I stood in that woman’s house and told her Hedin had gone to his kin, I have some part in it when he comes walking past her door.”

“Yes,” she said after a while. “I expect you do. What will you do for her tomorrow?”

“Take her clean bed linen. Ask if she’s heard from him. Sit awhile if she wants.”

“Could you do that much while you and I are still arguing?”

“I should hope so.”

“Good. Now pour a little water in the barrel. We’ve been at it since supper and I’m still getting my foot wet.”

I went to his wife in the morning. She had a low window open and the whole of her table taken up with thick clay-coated washers from the wheels of her handcart. Hedin had called in on her after seeing his nephews. She had lent him the cart for the day and he had come back in the evening with a wheel under his arm.

“Has seeing him eased you any?” I asked.

She looked round from the window. “Would it have eased you, Rann? I went in there and put my hand in his old coat while he was making those little boys laugh outside my door. I’d kept it on the peg where he left it. When he came in and asked should he take it, I told him I wanted it back before market. What am I to do, sit and smell a coat for the rest of my days with the very man in it shivering on the doorstep?”

She rubbed the back of her wrist across her nose and left a streak of clay. “The same goes for the cart, only if he means to take it again he can mend it first.”

She let me lend her a bed sheet and stay while she scrubbed up. For once I’d had the good sense to bring clean linen with me and leave my ideas about what she must be feeling at home. I went on saying the old prayers at work. Mother and I let our quarrel lie for a time.

## Chapter Three · The spring boat

I was forty the winter Ottar came to order his boat. I stood outside our kitchen with my arms full of sticks, listening to the old man argue that he could get in and out of any boat on the lake. Mother let him talk awhile, then pulled out a stool. I heard his bad hip click as plainly as if it had been my own.

“Would you like to go fishing with those grandchildren of yours,” she asked, “or would you sooner I told everybody how beautifully you can sit on dry land?”

There was the chink of money on the table. “What do I get for the lot, Aldis?”

“A boat you can step down into. A seat that suits your hip. She’ll be yours when the reeds put their green tips through in spring.”

“Then take it all now. If I keep any back I shall only spend it on the children.”

When he had gone I put down my sticks. Mother was counting out what we owed the miller and what it would cost to put sound boards over my bedroom before the next snow.

“You could have let one order go by,” I said. “You had to stop for breath twice coming up the steps yesterday.”

“I did stop. Here I am.”

“We can live on what I bring in.”

“We can eat on what you bring in. I should like to keep you dry while you eat.” She wrapped the money for the roof separately. “Of course if you’d married that fine broad roofer I took the trouble to send round, we might have got both jobs done for a kiss.”

It was an old joke of hers. There had been boys enough once. As a girl I could scarcely go down for a jug of beer without coming back the long way with somebody. I remember an evening in the overturned ferry with a boy called Finn. Rain ran off the keel in two curtains, and I put my fingers inside his shirt and made him jump so hard he cracked his head. Years afterward we could still make each other laugh by rubbing the spot.

For a time after I took the burial work, Finn still came to our back door. One evening I came straight from washing a woman who had died in childbed. I had scrubbed up to the elbow before I went out to him. He kept smelling my hair while he kissed me, and finally he asked if I couldn’t use another soap. I never heard him at the back door again. Others kissed me on a dark walk home and next morning found something fascinating to look at across the street.

Most of the Ness grew shy of me in broad day. Somebody always had an errand the moment there was a place next to me at a feast. Yet let a man die in the night and the very woman who had moved her chair would come to my house in her shift, take my hands in hers and kiss them for coming out with her. I loved those women. Sometimes, when I got home in the morning and put the damp kindling to the stove and waited for it to catch, I was still angry about that empty chair at the feast. It seemed an odd belief that you could catch death off a woman picking onions in the market and be put in no danger at all by fetching her out of bed.

“The roofer chews with his mouth open,” I said. “You’d have lasted a week.”

“A week’d have seen the roof on.”

Ottar used to come into the yard on Þórsdagr, the day the neighbors turned out to help one another with the heavy repairs. He always made a point of coming late enough for Mother to have a fire going but too early for her to have eaten the heel of the loaf. She had stood a pair of planks on edge, such a distance apart that they were the sides of his boat. He lowered himself sideways between them onto a stool.

“Now get your line back when your big fellow drops it over that side,” she said. He leaned across. The stool tipped and he let out a word that brought me straight out of the kitchen.

“Well, thank you very much, Aldis. If there’d been water there I’d have been in it.”

“That’s why I’ve given you boards and the ground under your feet. What happens if we take a finger’s breadth off here and give you somewhere to put your bad foot while you reach?” They worked at it till he could reach over and pick a shaving off the ground with his eyes shut. On the back of each of those boards Mother drew a little loop with the nub of chalk she kept behind her ear. Each loop meant a place his knee or his elbow had come to when he was comfortable. He gave me a little bow as he went out, as a man might if I had seen him dance well after years of having to sit and watch other people. Next week it was a piece of smoked trout he brought her, done the way she liked it, skin and all.

She fitted Ottar’s keel, the long oak backbone of his boat, before the next fall of snow. After that I could tell where she was in the yard by her cough. She worked with a wool strip over her mouth. By noon the outside was white with frost and the inside so wet I could wring it over the sink. I laid kindling for her every morning. I bargained with her for an hour at the fireside in the afternoon and lost half the bargains I made. The sound of the adze carried up to the burial ground. Some days she took so long between strokes that I would stop with a folded cloth in my hands waiting for the next.

One morning I came down to a cold kettle. She always put it on before I woke. I found her lying with one knee bent and her hand above her head. She was still wearing the shift she had coughed through in the night. When I sat beside her she opened her eyes and looked at me with such relief that I knew she had been awake a long time wondering how she was going to sit up.

Three days we had. Thora came over and slept by the stove. On the second evening Mother wanted me to tell her where I kept the spare lamp oil, who owed us for the last two repairs, how long the flour in the stone crock would last me. I answered her until I could stand no more of it.

“Would you like to know how many pairs of drawers I have put by too?”

“I suppose you’ll go on coming in by the back after dark,” she said. “I keep thinking who’ll light the kitchen for you.”

I could see what the effort of getting it out had cost her. I lay down with her, the way I used to when a gale shook my little bed upstairs. “I’m frightened too, Mother.”

“Will you get me ready yourself?”

“I could ask Yrsa, if you’d sooner.”

“Rann. I’ve been trying to ask you since yesterday. I’ve watched people go into that house half out of their minds, and come down the steps with their children by the hand and enough of themselves left to ask which way’s home. My daughter does that. I should like my sisters to see me come into Hel’s hall and know whose hands had the dressing of me.”

“I might make a poor job of it this once.”

“You can do it in bits. Sit down when you need to. Who’s going to complain of the delay?”

We lay with our foreheads touching. Her breath smelled of the pear juice Thora had coaxed into her at midday. “I wish I’d taken you up the lake more,” she said presently. “We went everywhere there was a job to do. I can’t think when we last went out because it was fine.”

“I remember plenty of fine days.”

“I should have liked one more.”

In the small hours she breathed in and took almost until morning, it seemed to me, to let that breath go. I stayed still waiting for the little tug against my cheek. Her eyes were open. A minute before, when I moved my head, her eyes had followed mine. I moved again, and what was left of the lamplight lay across them without stirring. I have closed hundreds of eyes. What I remember of my mother’s is how long I let her look at me after she could no longer see.

Yrsa brought hot water over from the burial house and set it where I could reach. I took the pitch out from under Mother’s nails with the worn corner of a cloth. There was a burn like a white bean on the inside of her wrist. I had been with her when she got it, helping thaw a rope outdoors. Under the cloth her hand made the same little hollow against my palm it had when she bent my fingers round the handle of my first tool. Nobody had told me how easy the work itself would be. I had done it all before, knew which way to turn her wrist, how to undo the string at her neck without cutting it. I kept wanting her to sit up and say that I had learned my trade all right after all.

The hands were the hardest. I washed one and went out to sit on the back step. The lake lay flat. I came in when I could and did the other.

I dressed her in the blue gown she wore to weddings and close gatherings. Thora combed out her hair and I plaited it. In our house it was the custom to put the guest money in the mouth. A dead woman should go to Hel with the price of her own lodging if she wanted to offer it. Mother had sent her sisters away with the same courtesy. I chose a silver piece worn thin along one edge so that it lay easily beneath her tongue. I told her who had come to carry her. At the Ness we speak to the dead until the earth has them.

The men had opened the old ground on the shoulder of the hill, below the worst of the frost. We laid her with her head toward the lake, in linen pinned with the plain wood pins our mothers had used. Each of us gave back a handful of earth before the grave was filled. The pipes played all the while. It was a lakeside air she knew, drawn out so slowly I could hardly bear the space from one note to the next.

I said what had been said there for my grandmother. “Take her in, good Hostess. Aldis is her name. There are people who will be watching for her.”

Ottar took my elbow on the way down. Our house would scarcely hold the company that night. Thora got up on a bench and told how Mother had gone out to mend our old ferry in a new green skirt and come home in the ferryman’s breeches. I had heard it all my life and never known until that night who went home in the skirt. The ferryman himself stood up to show how becoming it had been on him. He had his youngest with him, the lamp boy Leif, who laughed so hard he spilled his drink all down my neck. The player who had near broken my heart at the grave played a reel. We pushed the kitchen table back against the wall. I had been on my feet since before sunrise. I thought if I sat down I might somehow spoil what was happening. Then Thora brought a plate of buttered bread to the settle in the corner where she was sitting and made a place for me beside her and pulled me down by the hand.

“I’d never have gone home in that skirt,” she said. “Five yards of green wool between your feet, and the wind coming up the shore besides.”

“Tell me what happened. Nobody ever got that far.”

“She stepped on the hem on the way up out of the boat, was what happened. It was either let her go home in his breeches or carry her up the high street with her bare behind hanging out. I could walk up to the shop tomorrow and put my finger where I sat down with her to sew the thing back together.”

For the first time that long day I laughed and wanted to go on laughing. All along the settle people were calling across to say that was hardly what they remembered, they could tell us how it really happened. Thora ate her bread over my plate to catch the butter.

When everyone had gone I put the table in its place. Next morning a man came for a winding sheet. His wife’s father had died in his own bed at a good age. I measured the linen. I ate the rest of a cold pie. I went home at the usual hour and turned my head to Mother’s rocker to tell her something the ferryman had said. I kept on doing it. On the ninth evening, when I looked, there she sat.

## Chapter Four · The fare to Reedbank

She was looking at her hands. There was wet brown clay in the cracks of the rocker where her fingers had rested. The left sleeve of the blue dress was torn from shoulder to elbow. I remember thinking, very distinctly, that I’d sewn up that seam only last summer.

“Have you got a splinter?” I asked. It was what I had asked a hundred times when I came home and found her looking at her hands.

“I don’t seem able to get them clean.”

I filled the basin at the kettle, tested the water with my elbow, and set it on her knees. She gave the water the very same test. It was the first thing I had done for her in nine days that she could do better for herself. Where I had put clean linen to clean skin the dirt rolled off in little gray cords. I wanted to kiss her. I wanted Yrsa. I wanted to go back outside, count to ten and come in to an ordinary evening.

“Did you hear the pipes?” I asked.

She shook her head.

“Does it hurt to come up through the ground?”

“I remember the cold when the wind got inside my dress. I was on the path by then.”

“Who opened the grave?”

“I couldn’t tell you.”

“Did you see Grandmother? Were your sisters there? Did anybody say my name?”

“Love, if you keep asking I’ll begin making up answers just to see you easier, and then neither of us will know what happened. Come and sit where I can look at you.”

I sat on the hearthstone. The water dripped off her finger ends onto my sleeve, and all at once I was angry with her. I’d spent nine days learning what to do with myself for the length of a supper. She had walked back into the kitchen and I could feel myself beginning to reckon again how much food there was for two.

“What did you promise her?”

“To build Ottar the boat he paid me for. To put a boat made for his hip in the water for him. I told her so with my own mouth.”

“Why wouldn’t you rest? All my life you’ve told me about the halls of our kin. You’d only to take your place there.”

“I could hear a woman singing in the next room. It might have been my sister. I’d have given most things to look. But I still had that sickness in my chest, Rann. Each breath as bad as the last one in this house. Hel had her hand on the door and she asked me whether I would stay in that pain or go back and keep a promise. You talk as though I could have walked in and sat down to a supper laid for me. Perhaps some can. I’m telling you what she offered me.”

“Did she name Ottar?”

“No. I did.”

It came into my head to grudge him every day he would have on the lake with his grandchildren. I had never grudged that poor man anything before. “And when you’ve finished it?”

“She didn’t say.”

Out in the lane somebody stopped beneath the window to stamp the slush off a boot. I had my hand on the lamp before I knew I meant to put it out. Mother saw.

“You can’t stay here,” I said. If I live to a hundred I shall still hear how quickly I got the words out. “Half the town saw me lay you down. They’ll say I kept you back from Hel. Every mother whose child I buried will want to know what I did for you that I wouldn’t do for her.”

“And what would you say if that woman coming up the road was me, and my little girl was in your ground?”

“I don’t know.”

“Neither do I. I’ve had the walk from the hill to think about it. I mean to take the ferry to Reedbank. Thora will give me a corner in the net hall. Those people think Frigg expects a bed offered to anybody who comes cold to the door. I could finish my days there.” She wiped a drip from my sleeve. “I didn’t sit down here to make you find a way to keep me. I wanted to see you.”

Somehow that was worse. “You’ll work in this weather until you’re sick all over again. And I shall have you where I can see your roof and never see your face.”

“I missed your face too.” She reached down to put a wet hand on my hair. “Let me send for my tools when I’ve got a place to put them. I can pay to have the hull taken over. Come when the spring boats go in. Give us both until then to get used to this.”

I drew her hand to my cheek. There was grit in the palm. I kept it there while the lamp guttered and the wick filled the kitchen with smoke. She went down to the first ferry with one of my dresses wrapped in a towel. The blue one I kept to mend.

A week later Thora’s brothers brought a low scow to move Ottar’s hull. I packed the good adze, a mallet and two planes in Mother’s tool chest. I laid the mended blue dress on top. In among the shavings I slipped the pot of grease she used when her knuckles cracked. I saw one of the brothers sit on the chest and eat an apple while the other rowed. I watched him till I could see no more of either of them.

I meant to wait till spring. What sent me over early was a pair of boots. I was at the cobbler’s having mine soled. Half a dozen of us sat about the stove, and Signe, who kept the scales where our fish were weighed, said her son had walked all the way to Reedbank for a day unloading rye, only to find the work done. One of the men from the net hall had done it before daylight. What was a living boy to do against somebody who never slept?

“Who’s supposed to be up working all night?” I asked.

“Well, the ones that come back. You ought to know, Rann.”

Then somebody wanted to know how many had gone over in the last two years. They did the reckoning aloud while I sat with my bare feet in my lap. Hedin, the man who had come to the burial house to ask after his brother’s boys. Little Vigdis, who used to sing at the scales, taken by a fever ten days after she was married. Old Fen with the red beard. His widow had let his loom go to pay for his funeral, and he had walked all the way round the lake to take work at another. Last of all my mother. They missed a quiet woman called Runa who had gone away with a pack on her back. I held my tongue and so kept one name for myself.

“Near a score at Reedbank now, if you count the ones from upriver,” said the cobbler. “It’s these winters. My father could remember laying his nets out to dry in autumn. These last two years we’ve had ice on them before they’re in. Every winter your bell tolls oftener, Rann. Plenty going into the ground with work left on their benches.”

A voice behind me said you couldn’t cut the skin on the Returned, that they had no blood to spill. Signe asked what use the burial house was to people who would never fall sick or die. How long would there be a day’s wages for any of our children? She looked tired. Her son had come home with his dinner still wrapped in paper because he had been too ashamed to sit down alone and eat it.

“I’ve been in your house when somebody came in hungry,” I told her. “You’d have sat that man down to eat with your boy.”

“At my table I can make the bread go round. I can’t make work for him, Rann.”

Then Ottar’s cousin said that if putting the dead in the earth didn’t deliver them to Hel, fire would. They ought to go across together and bring every last one back and burn them at the stake. A woman I had known all my life asked in quite an ordinary voice, “Even Aldis?” I got my feet into my boots. The left sole was only half sewn. The cobbler came out after me with my mittens. I left him in the street calling after me that nobody had meant me to take it personally.

I went home and took down our money crock. If Ottar had his silver back, Mother could tell Hel the man had been satisfied. It seemed a small chance to buy at so great a price, but as I rolled the coins in cloth I could see her asleep in the rocker. I kept hearing her say how her chest had hurt with Hel’s hand on the door. I had no business telling myself there was a cure for that on the far side of a piece of silver. But I had lain beside her and heard her cough through the night, and now she was back in a cold shed trying to do the work that had worn her out. I wanted her to have the choice to put her tools down. What I wanted for myself was to think of her with her sisters and feel easy again. I knew what this town could do with a bad idea once the boats lay idle.

It had taken Mother’s advance to pay the miller and mend the roof. The silver in my crock was what I put by against the day my hands should shake too much to dress a body. I could still pick a lice egg off a seam in bad light. I reckoned I could earn it again. I counted out the whole price into a piece of cloth for Ottar. There was a little left in the crock. I put it in my purse for myself. My left shoe would need paying for, to start with, once I dared go back and ask the cobbler to finish the stitches.

Ottar was packing to leave for his daughter’s house down south. I set the sum he had paid Mother on his table. “Take it. If you ever get your boat you can pay for it then.”

He counted the silver. “Will this do her some good?”

“I mean to go and ask her tomorrow.”

He untied a thin stick from his belt. I recognized the cut in its side. Mother had made one notch through both halves before she gave him his, so they would fit together again if the price was ever disputed. He cut off the notched end and put it into my hand. “Show her that, then. She owes me nothing. Tell her about the little one, if there’s time. He’s learned to swim.”

I found Yrsa before I went to bed and told her what I’d heard. She got word to Thora by the late ferry. We lay awake in my kitchen listening for the scrape of boats drawn down the beach. None went that night.

I found Mother in a shed behind the net hall. She was fitting one of the curved timbers that hold the planks in shape. She took two strokes with the adze and sat down. I thought she was stopping for me until I saw the damp hair on her temples.

“You’re a little early,” she said.

“So are you. It’s barely light.”

“I get more done in the morning. By noon I’m ready for bed again.” She took a bite from a heel of bread with a little bacon in it. “Thora says I eat better than I did when I was alive. Once she said it we sat there looking at one another until I laughed and she felt she could.”

“People over home say you work through the night.”

“I should like them to have the mattress I’ve got. There’s a lump right under my tailbone. I fold my coat and put it under my hip. Those are the great secrets of sleeping in a house full of the returned dead.” She held out her hand for a clean rag by my elbow. A shaving had run up beneath her thumbnail. A bead of blood welled there when she drew it out. “Gods, that hurts. Hold this tight for me.”

I did. “Can you die, Mother?”

“Of course I think about it. I’ve done it once. There was a long time last night when I couldn’t get warm and all I could think was, here we go again. As far as I can tell, yes, I can die.”

“People are saying terrible things. Yrsa sent word what some of them want to do.”

“Thora told me. I lay and listened to a mouse eating my supper in its paper and thought about burning. I was afraid to put my feet on the floor to fetch the supper back. Did you ever think your mother would be afraid of the dark?”

There was nothing I could do with my hands. For years there had always been something I could do with my hands. I got out the cut end of Ottar’s tally.

“I’ve paid him. He knows why. He says if it does any good, he’s glad. Mother, I want this to be over. I want to think of you sitting among your own folk so that when it’s my time I can come in and see you there. I’ve wanted it every night I’ve lain down since you first got sick. Haven’t you worked long enough?”

She took the tally and rubbed the new cut at its end. “Tell me the words you used when you put the silver in his hand.”

I told her.

“Now tell me what I promised Hel.”

“To build him the boat.”

She waited. That had been her way with me when I was a child learning to fit a plank. Let me see the mistake myself, however long she had to wait.

“I know an oath once made can’t be broken,” I said at last. “I thought if he gave you leave it might count for something. I thought you might be willing to ask.”

“It counts for something with him. It was a good thing to put money in an old man’s pocket before a journey. But I gave Hel my word. I’ll have the boat ready when the reeds come up this spring, and then we’ll see what happens. I can’t ask you to be easy about it. I do ask you to come and see me while I’m doing it.”

I wanted to come so badly that I resented being asked. I kept my fare and left the coins I’d meant for my shoes beside her bacon. Thora could get her some fresh straw for that lump under her hip. I did not trust myself to stay while she thanked me.

On the way down from the net hall I passed Thora carrying breakfast to a woman newly out of the burial ground, with her feet still raw from the walk round the shore. Thora took a little cloth off the tray to show me what somebody had given her, half a dozen eggs with the straw from the nest still sticking to the shells. I found myself asking which neighbor had brought them. She said it was the woman’s brother who had left them at Thora’s door before daylight. He couldn't take her in at his own house in the Ness, where his wife's people had a shop. He had walked the whole night to bring her breakfast. I should have recognized the straw. It was the long, pale kind that had tickled the backs of my knees the last time I helped him bring an old man home to us on a cart. I had a great many neighbors whose minds I imagined I knew.

Leif was rowing without his father on the way back. I was his only passenger. For years he had been the boy his father sent to fill the two landing lamps on our shore. In fog he used to climb the mooring post, which brought him about level with my chin, and offer to guide me home for a farthing. Somewhere in the months my mother had been ill he had come into the long arms and level voice of a grown man. He could hold the boat straight in a crosswind while looking back to see how I did. When I was his age I generally managed one or the other.

“You’ve been crying,” he said. “Would you mind taking the little bucket out from under your seat? There’s a dry cloth in it. The blue one’s for passengers.”

I wiped my face. “Do a great many of us cry on your boat?”

“Enough to want two cloths. I take people to the burial ground.”

“What’s it like to take someone across again when you’ve already rowed them to their burial?”

“I didn’t know where to look when I saw Vigdis on the landing. In my head she was still lying in her box on the seat you’re on. Then she stepped on my foot getting aboard. After that it was just Vigdis.”

He dipped his oars again. “She used to sing when my father rowed. She wanted to sit quiet the other morning. I was cross about it. I’d got her back and I wanted the singing too.”

“I should like to have my mother back exactly as she was,” I said. “I suppose that sounds greedy.”

“I don’t see why. You ought to be able to tell it to somebody, anyhow. Has anybody asked you to sing for them lately, Rann?”

I nearly told him I couldn’t carry a tune. Then I understood. “After the cobbler’s wife died he used to ask me in to supper. All evening he wanted me to tell him what she’d said at the end. After a time I took another way home.”

He nodded. “Last time I had Vigdis aboard I told her about a white otter down in the reeds. She got down on her belly to see over the side. Neither one of us had a particularly good reason for laughing, but we near tipped the ferry. I think she’ll come aboard easier next time.”

We were at the steps. I got out and put the fare in his hand. “Thank you for bringing me over. And thank you very much for talking to me. Most people try to have a word with me about somebody dead. It’s been a long time since anybody asked how it was going with the living one.”

He undid the bowline to let the next boat in. I remembered all the pencil marks his father used to put up the doorpost to keep track of his height. A deal of growing had gone on in that boy since the last mark, and here was his father with no place on the door to show it.

## Chapter Five · What the lake carried home

Three mornings later I saw a crowd going down to the Ness landing in their good winter coats. Several had brought back the tall posts from the old cattle fair. There was no mistaking what they meant to do with those. The burial bell had scarcely stopped when somebody came up to fetch Yrsa. Would she come to Reedbank and say the words for the people they meant to send back to Hel? She stood with her hands in the suds where she had been washing a dead woman’s shift. While she considered him he took a step away and then another, till I could scarcely hear him say they had other people who knew the words.

I went down by the short way. By then the first boats were clear. There were men I loved in them. I called out their names, and a few turned where they sat. Thora had sent her youngest brother to watch our landing. He put out for home with me in the bow.

I was there when our people went up the street at Reedbank to take the Barrowbound from the net hall. I have heard some brave accounts of it since. What I saw first was a grown man twisting a girl’s arm to make her open a door that opened outward against his own feet. It was Vigdis. I heard her shoulder go. I got her behind an overturned cart and held her while the sickness passed. By the time I could look up, my neighbors were coming down the street again, some at a run. One was losing blood from a cut above his eye. One had been bitten. The posts lay in the road. At the end of the lane Thora had hold of Mother’s blue sleeve. I shouted to her. Mother got one arm up over the heads of the people between us. Then Thora took her through the gate into the net yard. Our people had taken nobody, and up to that moment everybody who had come across was still going home.

Leif was on the beach. He had been taking frightened people out of the crush. One of the men running home to our boats caught his boot in a length of rope and went down hard. Leif came running up from the Reedbank street behind him and got both hands under his arms. I heard him call, “Here, I’ve got you.” A man already aboard one of the boats saw dark coats coming down behind his fallen neighbor and shouted that they were being set on from the rear. A bowstring sounded. Leif was looking down as if he had dropped something. Then he sat down very hard on the gravel.

“I thought they were coming after us,” somebody kept saying in the crowd. To this day I couldn’t tell you whose voice it was. An old friend once asked whether I blamed the hand that loosed the arrow. I have wished, sometimes, that I could lay the whole weight of that boy on one person. There were so many of us to carry him home.

We got him to my bed on the Ness with the blue passenger cloth still in his pocket. His father took his shoes off. His brothers came in and out. Towards evening he picked at the blanket and I bent close enough to hear him asking about the lamps. I sent the older brother down with oil. There is a window in the burial house from which you can see the upper lamp, but from my house all you see is the light on the whitewashed wall opposite. When it came on I helped him turn his head so he could see it. His father had his hand on Leif’s ankle under the quilt, keeping the foot warm. He was still doing it when I went to find the boys.

After we buried him his father came round for the blue cloth. He buttoned it inside his coat. Then he took the long way home, along the high road where you couldn’t see the landing. I shut the door and caught up with him. Halfway round he told me that when Leif was learning to row he sat with his tongue sticking out, just as he did when his mother cut his hair. We went round a second time.

For a little while after his death people from either shore came to his father’s door. One morning there was a boy on the step with a little battered hand lamp. His mother worked the reeds on the far shore. She could find the channel to the ferry at night by keeping Leif’s two lights one above the other until she came around the bend. She wanted his father to know his boy had brought her home safe more times than she had ever counted. The old man took the lamp out at dusk and climbed the ladder and hung it where you could see it from her stretch of shore. By then it took a brave woman to come over the lake even in daylight to say thank you for such a thing.

The first men I saw come in answer to an oath brought oars. Reedbank had sent word up the Ormá, where the river people kept the fords. A generation before, when the flood went over the roofs, Reedbank’s fishers had hauled their boats across country to get those families out. The keeper of a ford had promised that whenever Reedbank took to the water to fetch one of its own, an oar of his house would go too. Two mornings after Leif had brought me home, the assembly had forbidden the ferry to take the Returned across. I had seen men from Reedbank come over in their own boat to fetch a man left standing on our landing. Now a girl who had stayed at the Ness to care for her grandmother sent word that men were watching the house. Her father wanted them both home. He could have crossed alone, as he had done for every market of his life. This time he sent for the oar he had been promised. I remember the ford keeper’s son standing on the eastern beach with it over his shoulder. They brought the girl back with her grandmother before nightfall. The river men stayed. By morning the Ness could see a dozen of their boats drawn up in the reeds. On our side someone sent a chip off the burial-house lintel to the miners up in the mountains. Years before, we had helped dig their children out of a snowslide and bring every one of them home for burial. They had sworn to come if ever our headland needed defending. The woman who led them down had a nephew sleeping in the net hall at Reedbank. She stopped at my house to borrow ink and send him word herself.

That first spring I watched for the reeds as I had promised Mother. A strip of green showed one morning. I went to the landing with a bundle under my arm and was sent home. Our boats could go across under a white cloth to exchange the wounded. If a boatman were seen taking passengers, the other shore could refuse him next time he showed the cloth. The ferryman who told me this was Leif’s father. I could no more have asked him for a place than I could have asked him to put his second boy where the first had sat. By then the injured were coming home in boats with winter cracks that opened under the weight. Thora sent me a shaving off a new ferry plank and a few words from Mother. She had taken Ottar’s hull off the working bench and put it up on blocks to free the space for repairs. “He can manage without his boat a little while longer,” she had made Thora write. “I mind very much having to make him wait but I’d mind having one of these split halfway over with somebody's child in it a good deal more.” She never got back to his boat. I tried to send that word south to Ottar. I never heard if it reached him.

Things that were years apart crowd together now when I remember that time. A miner in my yard on a hot night trying to find words for a letter to his little daughter. In the end he wrote, “Mam and you must get the hay in. I shall miss eating in the field with you.” An empty ferry turning in circles where two fishing crews had fought out on the lake. Snow all over the boots of a boy who came in to tell me there were men lying in the hazel wood. I knew those woods. I had been kissed up against one of those trees. For the rest of that winter nobody from our shore would go in for firewood alone.

When a king sent his guards to take the grain ford, there was a killing in the keeper’s house. River families bound to defend that house sent their sons. The men who came home wounded asked for their brothers. Up at Rindgate the rulers shut the eastern road to an army coming over the pass, and the next week we had boys drinking from our well who said every household in Rindgate had been ordered to send somebody old enough to carry a shield. The council at Brimstead called up the southern ship crews when the fighting cut off its grain. One evening I unfastened a coat and found a little shell with a hole worn through it at the neck of a man who had never seen Urdvatn till the day he died there. His people had put it round him on the chalk cliffs at Brimstead. I knew because his sister walked all the way from the sea and asked for that shell. She said their mother would want it back.

In the first year I could have told you which shore a man had come to fight for. In time I was lucky if I could learn his name. From the east and all down the southern coast, then inland clear to the western kings, they came in such numbers that the Ness could no longer feed the men at our landing. They cut down orchards for fuel. We buried somebody nearly every day. The armies that finally drove us off the headland belonged to a miller called Vargeld. When last I had heard his name, a woman was blessing him for a sack of flour. By the time his captains reached the lake, mothers were hiding their children from him in the hills.

We took the burial linen out under our coats. For one night I was a guest at Reedbank. Mother had gone east with the small children and those too sick to march. Thora laid me down in her bed still wearing my wet shoes. After that we did the work wherever we could find a room with a door big enough to bring the dead inside. One winter we shared a storehouse with a woman who baked bread for the men holding the road. The first day she came in with an armful of dry wood and stopped dead at the sight of us. Yrsa asked how many people the fire in her oven could keep warm overnight if we hung up our clean sheets to close off the end near the door. We had three families outside looking for shelter.

“Your sheets?” the baker said. “Would a child know what they’ve been off of?”

“They’ve been off the line in the yard since dinner,” I said. “Smell for yourself.”

She spread one between us. There had been hard frost the previous night. You couldn’t get cloth near enough to your nose to smell it without getting the print of the fold on your cheek. She touched it to her face just where I had, and together we put it up. By the end of that winter she and I used to sleep back to back under one cover, trading places when the one at the edge got too cold. It was years since anybody outside the family had let me that near her in a bed. Some of the things I miss most about the war I can hardly bear to say out loud. I miss her telling me she could always feel when I was worrying, because I would try to warm one foot with the other and they were both on her side by then.

Once in a while I could get a letter through to Mother. I asked if she had seen anything of a white otter. Six months later I got my answer on the back of a scrap of sailcloth. No otter. Would a red squirrel eating a candle do? I kept the scrap in my dress.

I learned the war was over from a woman who wanted the table I was working at. It was her kitchen, she said, and we had been welcome to it, but her man was home and he would like to eat in his own house. I looked beyond her and there he was, holding his cap. They’d made peace some days before. I had missed it altogether. I still couldn’t tell you which king first put out his hand or what he said to the next. I could tell you how we washed the table and put it out in the sun, and how for the first time in years we had the afternoon to ourselves and Yrsa and I sat on an upturned trough, too tired even to go indoors.

People were already calling it the War of the Dead. At our makeshift burial house I had laid out several who had died a second time, Vigdis among them. Her husband stayed late to help me comb her hair. He had been too frightened to come in the first time he lost her. When we went home there were more Barrowbound living around the lake than there had been the morning our boats went out to burn them. I saw them with the families coming down from the hills, helping carry the beds and the children and the hens. At the landing somebody was asking how many mouths there would be for supper.

It took time even in peace for a ferry to get back to ordinary work. The first week the passengers were mostly people carrying letters, and mothers going to see a son they had been told was in somebody’s house with a wound. I waited my turn. On my way through Reedbank I found Signe, our own fish weigher, struggling with a shutter that had hung crooked. The man on the sill holding the other end was Hedin. She said his brother’s boys had lent her the handcart and a wheel had come off before they got a street from home. Hedin rolled his eyes at me. After all that had happened, there was still no trusting anybody in that family with a borrowed cart.

I asked after Mother. She was back in Reedbank. Signe gave me directions, then caught at my sleeve. “Did anybody tell you what we did at the Thing?”

It was the first assembly the settlements had managed to hold together since before the quarrel. Signe had been sent to speak for Cairn Ness. She was the first on her feet, Hedin said from his window, to say the Barrowbound should be able to rent a house or hold one in their own name, hire out for a fair wage and keep it. A person could take up a debt from before burial. A creditor could never demand it on the strength of a promise someone had made in their first life. When the votes were counted, all of that had passed.

“Signe who sat at the cobbler’s?” I said. It was ungracious, but out it came.

“Yes. Well.” She had got the shutter straight and she leaned against it. “My son came home from the eastern road on a door with four people carrying him. Two of them I’d have turned out of that net hall. What changed my mind was what happened afterward. One of them came back to see how he did. She stayed three weeks in my kitchen. By the end I’d as soon it was her house as mine. I thought she ought to be able to get one of her own.”

Mother was in the yard of a house with two steps up to the door. She had pinned her hair on top of her head. I had meant to call her name so she wouldn’t start, but when she turned round I had no voice. She took both my hands and pulled me hard against her. I could feel the wooden pin coming loose against my temple. She smelled of clean wool and the brown soap we used at home. Against my cheek I felt the ridge where I’d sewn up the sleeve of her blue dress.

“I’m glad you’re alive, Rann,” she said into my neck. “Let me have a proper look. Oh, it’s good to see you.”

She made a mess of trying to wipe my face with the dry end of an apron she was using on her own. We stood like that till we could bear the sight of one another. Then she let me go to catch the loose hair, and I went back and gathered her up myself.

“I’m glad you came back,” I said.

She turned her face against my cheek. For once my mother hadn’t got anything to say. I liked having the holding of her for the time it took.

From the yard I could see the long shed where she had worked the morning I went over with Ottar’s silver. Its doors were open. I asked if the boat was inside.

“It was gone when I got back. Somebody may have taken her off the blocks and used her. Or she went for firewood with the boards she stood on. I’ve looked at half the boats on this lake, love. I should know her even upside down. She’s gone.”

I could scarcely take in the waste of it. I had felt every stroke that boat had cost her. “You mean to go through all that again? Mother, I don’t even know whether Ottar’s alive. The last I saw of him he was taking the road south.”

“I should like to know that myself,” she said. “I’d go south to look, if I had a boat.” She eased her hairpin back in. “It doesn’t matter whether there’s anybody left to come and ask me for it. An oath is an oath. Tomorrow I’ll go up into the wood and find a tree fit for the keel.”

Once, at nineteen, I had stood beside her bench till well after dark, looking for the courage to tell her the one thing I wanted to do with my life. This time it came easily.

“Mother,” I said, “I’d like to go with you to find the tree.”
