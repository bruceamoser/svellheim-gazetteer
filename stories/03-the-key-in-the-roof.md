# The Key in the Roof

## Chapter One · The Goose

My uncle let me put six toes on the goose. I knew how many a goose had. There was one in our yard that had chased me to the privy often enough for me to know a good deal about geese. But the clay was sweet to cut, and the first three toes came out so well that I went round the foot again.

“There's a bird that'll stay on a wet roof,” my uncle said.

We were sitting on a board across two upturned tubs in my father's yard. I was five. Ari, my mother's youngest brother, had come to get tiles for a roof he was mending. Our tiles had a little lip bent over the upper edge while the clay was soft. Once they were fired, that lip would hook over a strip of wood under the roof. My job was to run a wet finger along the lip to close up the little cracks it got in bending. This particular tile was to go over the door where everybody would see it. When my father came out to inspect us I put my hand over the bird.

“There's a thumbprint now as well,” he said.

“I could smooth it.”

“Then how would you know it was yours? Off the board with you. It has to dry.”

My father carried it in flat on both hands. Things I made were generally rolled back into a ball before supper, and I watched to see if he would set it with the others in the drying shed. He did. I turned round on the plank to tell Ari. He was rubbing his bare feet. One heel had an old scar through it, puckered and white like a worm in an apple. I put my thumb to it.

“Did a goose get you?”

“My own sister. Your mother pushed me off the landing stage. There was the bottom of a broken jar in the mud.”

“She did it on purpose?”

“I had put her best shoes into a boat to see if it would float away with them. She put me into the water to get them back. This trouble you have with geese, little one. Do you ever consider leaving the geese alone?”

I liked him better than any grown person I knew. He was the only one who had given me useful information about my mother.

A week later he let me go with him. The horse could smell the sacks of oats stowed under our seat and kept bending her great head round to ask us for some. We set out on Þórsdagr, the day our neighbors put aside their own work to lend a hand at a heavy job. My father drove. A roofer and his son walked behind to steady the load on the steep parts. At every rut I could hear our whole week's labor chatter against the straw it was packed in.

Our town stood in the foothills below the long mountain ridge that people called the Shield. Long before there was a town, the rock had opened there and a fire had come up out of the ground. There was no chimney and no woodpile. Smoke from all the cooking fires round about blew sideways down the street, and the red flame in the cleft went on folding and unfolding itself under the open sky. People had put their houses as near it as they could. My mother could take me there to warm my feet on market mornings without having to buy anything first. That morning a woman in a man's boots was clearing fallen stone away from the cleft. She was its Keeper. One old fellow wanted her to let him put a stool on a stone that still steamed from the rain. She spat on the end of her broom and touched it to the stone. The bristles hissed. He carried the stool a little farther back.

On the far side of town we took the road the herds used going up to summer pasture. With each turn we came abreast of something I had only seen above our heads. The miller's upper window went by, then the tops of the elms where the rooks nested, then the quarry where the stone in our threshold came from. By noon I could look down on the back of a buzzard hanging over the valley. I put my chin against Ari's coat to feel him talk without having to take my eyes off it.

“Does your fire come up out of a mountain, too?”

“Wait till you see it. If you find a mountain inside that house you can tell me where it keeps its feet.”

The house had a roof on one side and daylight showing through the other. There was a little porch where an old woman could sit and get her breath before taking the two stone steps. In the wall opposite the door a stone basin sat low in a niche, the hollow worn black and smooth. A little red light burned steadily in it. Ari put his hand in front of it and the red light shone between his fingers. He took a wooden scoop from the wall and lifted out the crumbs of plaster the rain had brought down from the roof. They made a little scraping noise as they came away from the stone. Even at five I could tell what would happen if that basin filled with fallen plaster while everybody at the farms supposed somebody else was looking after it. I could warm my fingers there. For boiling water there was an ordinary hearth by the sleeping benches, a stack of split sticks and an iron hook for a pot.

“Is that all of it?” I said.

“It's kept that light since my teacher's grandmother slept here. Give it credit. How did you know which side of the road to come to when we turned the corner?”

“I could see it through the hole in the wall.”

“That's a window. A fellow coming down in the dark sees it too. His knees are shaking and it's raining down the back of his neck. What do you suppose he's going to think of our fine little fire when he gets inside and the rain's coming through the roof as well?”

He was an Ember Tender. Along this stretch of road the farms fed him, and in return he went from one shelter to the next to care for the small Embers in them and keep a dry place ready for whoever came along. When he was away another pair of hands with a stake in the shelter did the day's tending. Here it was a woman who brought goats down to the well each morning. She brushed the drift of seed husks off the sill so the red light showed down the road. She mended the sacks of bedding when the goats got at them. She had no way to carry a cartload of tiles up from town. That was why we had come.

Ari made an apron out of an old piece of sacking for me. My father threw the broken tiles from the roof into a heap. I got to pass the sound ones along until the roofer wanted his own son back on the ladder. They stripped the rotten strips of wood, fitted new ones across the rafters, and covered the bare places, one row laid a little over the one below so rain could run all the way to the eaves. Some of the old tiles had been taken from a much older building. You could see grooves in them where people's shoes had worn them down when they were floor tiles. The whole thing smelt of damp straw and the pitch the roofer had got in his hair and sworn at everybody for laughing about.

When we reached the roof over the porch Ari held me level with the eaves to set my goose into the row. He slipped something heavy into my apron. It was an iron key, rusty in the teeth, with a ring I could put two fingers through.

“In wet weather the front door twists on its hinges,” he said. “Sometimes the only way to get it open is to go in through the shed behind and give it a pull. That key fits the shed. I'd hang it up, but the goats have learned they can knock anything off a peg and have a chew at it. Find a place where it'll stay dry.”

Lying on my belly across his shoulder I could reach under the goose and set the key on the beam. He let me climb down. Standing on the porch step an adult could reach it easily. A child needed a stool. I shut my eyes, climbed back on the block of wood we had used as a mounting step, and felt for the little notch where my thumb had marked the clay.

That evening it rained. It came over us like a sack emptied on the roof. People talking on the benches looked up as one. Along the eave the water gathered in a shining rope and came down clear of the wall. A small boy climbed up beside me to see. He put his hand out, made a fist of rain and dabbed it on my nose. We fought over who had made more of the roof. He had handed up twelve tiles. I had made the goose. I was so full of pride I let him win.

My father took the horse home. Ari and I stayed the night to see where the bedding got wet. I slept beneath my own roof for the first time, lying head to foot with a woman whose breath whistled when she turned on her left side. It did rain in one place. Ari moved the flour chest in the dark and went up in the morning to see where he had gone wrong. I remember him telling the woman on the bench, quite happily, that he had been so busy admiring one particular tile that he had left a gap under the next.

## Chapter Two · Somebody Awake

In Sólmánuður, the month of long daylight, a person could get supper into a child at the normal hour and find her still sitting up in broad sunshine when everybody wanted to go to bed. The summer I turned seven, my mother let Ari take me out for three nights. She gave him my spare shift rolled round a lump of soap. I heard her tell him I still wet the bed sometimes, and the shame of it ached right down into my fingers. I went out to the lane ahead of him. He found me with my bundle at my feet, trying to get it undone so I could leave the spare things at home.

“She used to tell on me too,” he said. He put the bundle under his arm. “Suppose I wet mine, what am I to change into if we've left our washing on the road?”

“Do you?”

“Generally it's the roof that does it for me. I did once sit clean down in a pail of milk. Ask me about that when you've been walking long enough to need something good to think about.”

At the first turn I took my bundle back. I wanted him to see I could carry my own things. He had judged the distance to my first complaint exactly. Below the hazels, when my heels began to slide up and down in my shoes and the straps of my bundle bit, he confessed to mistaking a cloth-covered pail in a dark kitchen for one of the low stools people used for milking. I could see the whole sorry business in my head. The farmer's dog had tried to drink out of his trousers. By the time we got to the farmhouse he had me so far into it that I asked for milk with my bread and everybody wanted to know what the two of us were laughing about. He let me tell it on him. I tell it now almost as well as he did then.

It was there I first heard how people in our valley came to have Tenders. Bera the farmer's wife was filling a sack for us. It was barley crushed between two stones, coarse enough that a grain sometimes stayed whole and popped between your teeth in porridge. This was her household's turn to feed the strangers on the road. I asked whose idea it had been for everybody to take turns like that.

“My grandmother gave the very first sack,” her husband told me. “A good big one, too.”

“Your grandmother was a baby when Sela fell,” Bera said. “You'll have Sif telling this wrong to children of her own. Look, love. My grandfather drew us a picture.” She got a broken roof slate down from the shelf and put it on my knees.

Nobody expected me to read the words at seven. An unmistakable little picture showed a woman stretched out on a door with men at either end. I asked if she was dead. Bera touched a nail to the little woman's head.

“Very much alive. That was Sela. She kept the little Ember down by the sheep bridge. The year she broke her hip the rain went under the shrine door and stood deeper every hour. The men remembered a drain under the flagstones but nobody knew where. A stone as long as you are is a heavy thing to lift on a guess, and there were twenty of them. Finally my grandfather said, get the woman who knows where to look. That's him at the end with the door latch up his backside. I know because he drew it himself.

“Sela had them carry her there. She pointed at a tuft of grass and told them to lift the stone beside it. Out went the water. My grandfather thought about what would have happened to that fire if he'd gone to fetch Sela six months later and she'd been in the ground. Come harvest he and half a dozen others brought her enough meal for two, and the widow's daughter went to live with her and learn the work. Other farms took up with their nearest shrine. That's how our people began doing it. A traveler coming over the top will tell you something different about the next valley. He'll probably be right, too.”

“My grandmother did give a sack,” the farmer said.

“Two, once she was old enough. If I ever give twice what I owe, I hope my grandchildren remember it too.” Bera rolled the neck of the full sack and tied it with the twist of grass she had been keeping between her teeth. “There. That one I can answer for.”

I asked Ari as we went down the lane why Sela kept a fire beside the sheep bridge in the first place. He had to think. My own mother told a story about Hallinskidi, the Watchman who kept the crossing into our world. He once walked a company of lost children home, she said, and at every place he stopped to count them a little Ember came up. The shepherds in our hills said the first road led west into the place the sun went to sleep. Ari knew a stretch that went straight into a duck pond.

“Couldn't we look on the other side?” I said.

“I did. In summer it's a bog up to your waist and you disturb a great many nesting ducks. When there's a path I use it. There's nobody left who can tell you where the people who laid those stones wanted to go. What people need a road for changes. Bera's grandfather built that bridge for sheep. Your father's brought more clay over it than there's been sheep this spring.”

“Did you see the Great Green?” In bedtime tales the trees of the Great Green grew so close you could pass underneath them on the brightest afternoon and see stars. A girl who strayed in might come home a hundred years later with the berries still fresh in her hand.

“If every place in your mother's stories was on my round I'd wear myself out long before I got to the ducks. Come on, let's have a look at those heels.”

We were at my goose shrine that night when Bera came in with her son's wife, Halli. They had been on their way home from visiting the woman's sister when Halli's child began to push its way into the world. She gripped the doorpost and let out a noise that made me look straight at her belly, so sure was I something had burst through the cloth. Then she was Halli again, a woman I knew, apologizing to me for frightening the wits out of me. Bera sent a traveler running for Halli's husband. Ari drew the shutters on the back room and gave me a folded dry shift to lay ready for afterward. He told me to sit by the door and call when the husband came.

I was waiting with my chin on my knees when I saw that a man was sitting on the step beside me. There was a horn slung at his belt, the polished, hollow horn of an ox with a mouthpiece of beaten brass. Men blew them to call the cattle down from the upper pastures. This man had been trying to clear the mouthpiece with a straw.

“My little finger will go in there,” I said.

It came away with a damp grass seed stuck to it. “I wondered why I could only get one sorry squeak out of it,” he said. “What are we waiting for?”

“Halli's husband. And the baby. I want to go in to her but I'm supposed to call out if he comes.”

“Would you like me to call? You can go and see your friend.”

I considered. I didn't like the sound she was making. From the lamplit crack at the side of the shutter I could hear my uncle telling her she'd done well and Halli telling him he'd never in his life been obliged to do any such thing, and the two of them started laughing. “I think she has everybody she wants in there. I can watch with you. Is that what the horn is for, waking people up?”

“It got two brothers home once. One had a lame leg. The other carried him, and I had to keep a way open till both were across. That's how my business goes.” My mother had told me that tale too, and in her telling it was Hallinskidi himself who stood there long after dark, leaning his weight on his spear while the brothers made their way into our world. I pulled my feet in so the man could put his on the step.

“Are you the Watchman? I made the tile above your head. I'll show you how to find it.”

He reached up without ever standing and took the key from under the goose. Even my uncle had to stand to do that. “I've seen you doing this, Sif. A great deal older than you are now. Somebody's going to have you like you have your uncle. You're going to tend a road.”

“But what if he wants to tend it still?”

“You'll have time with him, child. You're going to find out why anybody takes on a stretch of the world to look after. When you know, you can tell me.”

I wondered how I was meant to find him to tell him anything at all. Then there was a young man on the path from the bridge with yellow flowers in his fist. “I can hear her,” he said. “Is that good?”

I was on my own step with my cheek creased from the sleeve I had pillowed it on. There was a taste of grass seed on my tongue. If I had fallen asleep I had let my uncle down. I drew breath to call Ari, but the young man was already at the shutter. “Halli? It's me. I've come.” There was such relief in her voice when she called him in that I forgot to wonder whether I'd been sleeping. A small, ordinary, furious cry rose up behind the shutter. I have heard babies born since, and every time there comes back to me the long light of that evening and those yellow flowers, their stems all crushed in the palm of a boy's hand.

A hand came out through the shutter. I put Halli’s dry shift into it and took the bowl of bloodied water she wanted me to empty. By the time we had her comfortable again I could scarcely keep my eyes open. Ari carried me in to bed. I asked him, very privately, whether he'd ever seen the Watchman. “I haven't. Is there something you want to tell me about the door tonight?”

“I might have gone to sleep.”

“You came when Halli needed you with the shift. She's got it on now. Did you see the flowers in the water jug? She made him go get something to keep them in before she'd let him hold the baby.”

I could have told him the rest. Already the way I remembered the man had begun to change. By morning I was sure the man had worn shoes, and by supper I could have drawn his bare feet. So I kept him to myself, as a child sometimes keeps a secret simply to have one thing in the house that no grown person gets to put straight.

## Chapter Three · Hay Supper

The first time I had cause to be thoroughly angry with my uncle I was twelve years old. It was Heyannir, when the valley people went out with their long curved scythes to cut hay and came in smelling so sweetly of crushed stems that even the dogs wanted to lie with them. A family that got its hay stacked and thatched against the rain gave a supper for the neighbors who had helped. There was a dance after. My uncle could dance a mug right round the room on his head. I had seen him do it at home, jumping my father's left leg as it stuck out from under the table and never once steadying the mug with his hand.

The first hay supper of the year was to be given at Bera's. I had been making something for the occasion. My father had a little wheel he could turn with his foot while his hands worked the clay that spun on its top. It was much harder than it looked to bring up anything with even sides. Half my attempts leaned over like men trying to see around a corner. The cup I made for Ari had two handles. I put one too high by a finger's width and thought better of pulling it off, since I'd had to pull the first four off and start them again. I colored the rim with wet clay rubbed through cloth, which fired a darker red than the body of the cup. It had a good broad bottom to sit on a man’s head and the two handles stuck out like ears.

“Isn't it a bit young for him?” my mother asked.

“What's young about it?”

“Two handles. For someone whose hand shakes, or a baby learning to drink.”

I wrapped it in my clean dancing apron and tucked it behind the spare shirt in his pack. At noon Ari put his head through the door and said he had come for his partner.

We walked up the valley together. The old paving showed through the earth in humps under the hedge, while the road we walked kept mostly beside it, having gone gradually where hooves and wide cartwheels found the going easier. Hay wagons came past with the great piles lashed down, every rut sending a soft hissing slide down the side. In one field men were singing the parts of a song. We lost all but the deepest voice on the far side of a fold in the ground and got the whole tune back again fifty paces later. I used to love that sort of thing about a day out with Ari. There was always time to go back fifty paces to hear it happen twice.

Ari had brought a pan in his pack, onions, an egg in a box of sawdust and the last of some smoked mutton. By the river he got me to lay flat stones for a windbreak while he shaved curls off a stick into a little nest of dry grass. I had seen him knock a spark off his fire steel into a scrap of charred linen a hundred times. He was good at letting the red creep along it till a breath was enough to kindle the nest. I thought there was nothing I could do in the business until he set the unbroken egg in my hand.

“Can you put an egg between two people who both want the yellow?”

I cracked it into the pan. The fat leapt. I broke the yolk with the eggshell and drew it over the white. He looked so unhappy I believed for a moment he would be sick. “There,” I said. “Nobody got robbed.”

“Nobody got an egg worth eating, either. How would you like it if I broke your fine new cup so we could each have an equal bit?”

“You saw it? How?”

“I know the shape of a pair of handles when they're sticking into my back. And I saw the rim in my sister's kitchen. I've worn that cap you made me three summers running. Did you suppose I was going to wear this one to a wedding?”

“You could just drink out of it.”

He did. He took the apron off and set his thumbs on the two handles. He turned it until the high handle lay under the long fingers of his right hand, with the blunt old nail of his left thumb tucked nicely into the lower loop. He took a good drink before he said anything. “I could get a drink into my old teacher with this. One hand to hold it between us and one hand free to put behind her head if she needed it. Or a man might like to drink a mug of ale and let all his clever friends find out for themselves why it has two handles. What's the matter, Sif?”

“When we get there will you dance with it? I told Bera's granddaughter that you would. She said if there was any ale in it nobody would stand near you. I said I would.”

“So you want a whole cup of it down your neck? Done. Help me wrap it up or we'll have some explaining to do at supper.”

At the sheep bridge we found a man sitting on the stone edge with a cut down from his eyebrow and blood all over his shirt. His dog had the lead of a ewe in its mouth. Across the water another man was trying to see out of an eye that would not quite open. I knew them. The one with the cut was Oddi, who used to make me dolls out of the joints of lambs' feet when I was little. The man across the bridge was his older brother, Geir. After their father's death the year before they had divided his flock between them and now neither man could go to market without his sheep smelling their old company and trying to start a family reunion in the middle of the road.

“Ari,” said Oddi. “Look at that notch in her ear and tell him whose she is.”

“I'll have a look at your head. Has the dog been holding the ewe all this time? Sensible creature. Any chance of my getting him to take your knife back to the shed for me?”

Oddi spat on the ground and looked at his boots. “I'll carry it there myself if he's leaving his there too. I want my case heard. I've a son old enough to be walking these ways without being called a sheep thief every time somebody knows him by his face.”

On the porch Geir unbuckled his belt and laid it down with the knife still in its sheath. Oddi looked at his brother a long time before he did the same. I had held a brush for oiling those two men's boots and a ladle for serving them supper. This was the first time either had let me pick up his knife. A man who gave up his weapon at a shrine could stay in safety until morning. This particular law had got the two of them dry many times, in weather when they had been glad to see each other. If either wanted his neighbors to hear a complaint in daylight, Ari could fetch the neighbors. Till then both men had the same right to the benches and the little fire.

I went in through the shed. It smelt of damp harness leather and old dung on the shoes of the men who had come before us. From its inner door I could lift the swollen front door by the latch and pull it open, while the men outside would have had to ram it with their shoulders. I put the weapons in the shed and hung the key up under the goose. I had grown tall enough to do it without the stool.

We got the two brothers in out of the sun. I brought water and cloth. When Ari had washed the blood off Oddi's face there was a little pale fold in the cut, like the place where a finger has gone too deep in wet clay. It took some time before I could make myself look at the man again. Geir was sitting very straight, breathing through his mouth. I gave him a cloth full of cold water to put on his eye. He said thank you in precisely the voice he used to ask my father how much he wanted for a butter crock. In a peculiar way that frightened me more than the cut. I wanted to be at the hay supper where people had ordinary faces.

The hoofprints outside had dried hard and there was a smell of frying onions on my sleeves. Ari heated what was left of the mutton in water for the dog to drink once it cooled. He put a meal together for the two men. He borrowed my comb and very carefully, from the ends upward so he'd pull as little scalp as possible, combed the blood out of Oddi's thick hair.

“I suppose that's supper gone,” I said from the doorway, where neither brother would hear us.

“You might make the dancing yet. Wash up and go if you like. Bera's eldest will walk you home.”

“That isn't what we said.”

“No. It isn't.”

“Couldn't you leave both of them to sit by themselves? Nobody's going to cut anybody. I've got their knives. You've told them how much trouble they'll be in if they break the peace.”

“When we go down that road they'll be alone again. I should like to try to have them speaking before they're alone. When my father was dying my brother and I left the room to get a bundle of sticks for his fire. My brother hit me with one. I still have the tooth here that never came through straight.” He put his nail against a little ridge inside his lip.

“I thought Mother pushed you into the mud.”

“That's a different injury. I was in a large family and all of them had good reason. I'll tell you about this one if you'll sit with me a bit. I'm tired.”

I sat. It mattered to me suddenly that he was actually tired. Usually he could get from here to our kiln without once forgetting which of us was the child.

“My father was dying,” he said again. “His hands were so cold they hurt him. We both heard him ask for heat, and both went out of the room, each expecting the other to stay. What we had to say to each other in the yard was cruel enough to smash up a family. I told my brother the sticks were all green because he was lazy. He said if it mattered so much to me that my father had a good fire I could come home and do some of the work. It was true. I was out learning the road, which I liked, and he was at home with a sick old man who wet his bedding and had forgotten his eldest boy's name. Our sister took the sticks away before I could get up and hit him back. She made us go indoors with her to warm our father's hands.”

It was the most he had ever told me about my grandfather. My grandfather in my own mind was a large gloomy man with a beard, the owner of a crook-handled stick which hung in our house. There at the shelter he acquired hands that could hurt him, sons who had shamed themselves in his yard, a name somebody had gone to a great deal of trouble to choose for a little boy and then forgotten.

“I think you ought to have had time to learn your work,” I said.

“I think my brother could have done with a day fishing once in a while. It took him knocking a tooth loose to get me to see it. I wouldn't mind finding out what's got across these fellows. I like them both. I'd like them to be able to go on sharing this road. And I did promise you a dance, Sif. You get to be angry about it. Go put your apron on anyway. You've a right to a good evening.”

Presently he went inside and put my clean cup on a bench in the light. He handed it to Geir to see the goose I scratched near the foot of everything I made. Geir brought it right up against his good eye. I could look at him now that he couldn't see I was doing it. That eye was cloudy round the edges, a milk-white ring like a wet scrape on horn. His brother saw it too.

“Since when?” asked Oddi.

Geir looked helplessly at Ari. Ari gently turned the cup round so he could find the handle. “Since milking time last summer. I've been hanging the yokes on the rail in the same order so I can tell my red cow by feel. I thought I could still see which of the ewes went into which pasture. If you'd let me keep the brindled dog I'd have some chance of making sure.”

“You told me to take her. I offered to take the young one.”

“You did. I was a damned fool.”

They got a good deal farther than that by slow stages. Once, coming back with more water, I heard Geir chuckle. Oddi agreed to send the brindled dog home with Geir and take the young one to train himself. He would keep the ewe until their neighbors could look at her ear and help them settle whose lambs she had borne. They agreed to meet on Týsdagr, the day people in our valley brought their cases to be heard. Ari promised to be there. In the meantime Geir tore off a hunk of cheese for his brother, who'd let his own supper go cold worrying about his eye. You could have fed three men and a dog in considerably less time than all that took. It mattered a great deal that someone had it to give.

We never made it to Bera's. That evening as I rinsed my cup at the bridge I heard a fiddle tuning up a little way off, in the stone house whose door we had left open. Geir played with his poor eye closed and the heel of his left foot tapping. Oddi and his dog were posted solemnly at the door. Ari took my cup, poured in a little of the bitter ale a traveler had left in a crock, and lifted the thing to the flat place on his head.

“I won't wash your apron if this goes wrong,” he told me.

I put my hands in his hands. The room was narrow. We had to go sideways where the bed stuck out, and Ari had to go backward coming up to the hearth or the chimney breast would have brushed the cup off his head. I was laughing so hard I was of very little help to him. I almost had it down at the last turn. He got a knee under me, lifted me right off my feet and over his crooked leg in a great traveling step, and set me down dry. We never danced better together. A little ale had got into his left eyebrow. He kept his face quite grave and drank what was left over my shoulder.

## Chapter Four · The Upper Road

Two years later we left in the dark to get to the upper ford before the river rose. The mountain snows were melting that Harpa, the first month of summer. Around our town the early lambs could already be put on fresh grass. Up by the ford we could hear loose lumps of ice rolling around under the alder roots like stones in the bottom of a wooden pail. We had a parcel to deliver, and there was a little shrine beyond the ford where no Tender had spent the night since the snow fell. My mother had packed the two-handled cup among our clothes. Sometimes the old people at the shrines asked for a drink from that cup in particular. It ate up room in the pack, but by then everybody at home understood it had to go in.

Two travelers had stuck a branch upright in the middle of the current to show where the ford shelved away into deep water. That branch was knocking from side to side. Every time the downstream twigs disappeared I thought it was coming free, and then it rose again. Ari watched it till I grew tired of watching him. This was the first season I was going the long round, out through the hills for the best part of a week, and I had been waking early for days for fear of being left behind.

“Do you think I can't cross it?”

“I think I can't. I don't feel the cold in that bad heel till I've been on it too long. The last time I came this way I took my stocking off that night with the skin stuck to the wool. When I start to go over you're going to catch me, are you?”

I considered it. At fourteen I was growing, and at fourteen you sometimes think a few inches of leg gives you the weight to stand where a man of twice your size would go down. From the look on his face I gathered he could remember feeling that way.

“Can't we stay with the miller?” I asked. Below us the roof of the little grain mill stood out among the willows. We could just hear its slow clack from where we stood. It had been louder on the way up from town. It was one of the few places in those hills where water could be made to do a man's work. A channel took part of the river to a wooden wheel. When the water pressed the paddles round, a shaft and wooden teeth turned a great flat stone on its mate. The miller fed barley between them from above. At home, when the meal ran out before the next visit from a miller's cart, I'd turned the little upper stone of our hand mill till I would have married a miller to be rid of the ache in my shoulder.

“I promised I'd sleep up there. There's a pot of lamp oil and a bundle of linen owed to that place. Your mother thinks you and I are going to borrow blankets out of the sky?”

“You promised to sleep up there tonight? Who to?”

He did not like that question. I liked having thought to ask it. The parcel in my pack, wrapped in wool, was a little jar from my father with new glaze the deep mottled green of holly leaves. Ari had sat with a skin of wine and a lamp and drawn a bird on paper for the old man to copy onto it. My father had done it, with only a few questions about the occupant of the house the jar was to come to. The name that came up on the road, whenever Ari found himself unable to explain why he did something exactly so, was Ilve. *Ilve says a wet blanket's all right for the dog. If I put that away dirty I'll never hear the end of it from Ilve.* She was the Tonttu woman who had trained him young, one of the woodland folk of the interior. She and Ari wrote to each other in the tiny lettering that would fit on a roll tied to a pigeon's leg. I had long wondered what she said to him that took a whole evening in our loft to write an answer to.

“Bera's cousin will bring a bird back from Ilve's place to the upper shrine for me to carry. I said I'd meet him. He'll take your father's jar on his way back. I don't want him standing out a second day.”

“Then why don't you call down and ask if anybody's got a boat?”

“Because I've walked this road since before they built the mill. The boat goes up on the bank the week the ice breaks.” He looked downstream again. He put his hand up and I held my breath. We stood above the ford looking down at the mill. The wheel missed a beat. We waited for the next and heard only the river pulling at the alders.

We walked down. The river had put a finger of dirty water through the door. Flour lay on it in white plates. The miller had tipped all the sacks up on the benches and was trying to move one more with the arm that was still good. His daughter was up to her ankles carrying barley out in her skirt. We spent most of the morning carrying meal up the bank in borrowed buckets. Ari worked with his jaw set in a way that made me want to turn back on the road. He liked being able to do everything somebody needed and he had already made one promise more than he could keep. Once he snapped at me to use both hands on the bucket. The miller's daughter took it away from me.

“Girl's got a cut. Can't you see her thumb?”

I had a sliver of oak in the pad, right on the place where I pushed the clay up on the wheel. He sat beside me on a sack of meal, took the splinter out and bound the little split. It hurt like fury when my thumb beat inside the bandage.

“I beg your pardon,” he said presently. “There are days when you think I've got my eyes in the back of my head. I'd been looking at your hands all morning and only seeing how few of them there were. I don't get any better at being wanted in two places.”

“Is Ilve the woman you love?” I asked.

The miller's daughter came up the bank with a dribbling skirtful of grain. We sat till she emptied it. I thought I had contrived to ask an unspeakable question of a man who was, as he would have put it, too tired to make up an answer fit for a young person. When the girl went down again he laid his big hand on the sack.

“When I first learned the upper road I used to have pains here,” he said, touching a place beneath his breastbone. “I'd get them toward evening. All day I could get on splendidly. Then at dusk I'd begin to think about what would happen to the people depending on me if the fire went out, or somebody died in the night, or I took the wrong fork and never got home. My teacher used to take her coat off and leave it where I slept, and go to her bed at the next farm. I couldn't stay awake feeling clever and sick about the things that might happen tomorrow with my nose buried in that smelly old sheepskin. Smelt of sour apples, woodsmoke and her hair.

“I'm a man with some gray in his head now, Sif. When I get home with the heel hurting and there's a little bit of paper under my door with her bad drawing of a foot on it, there is nothing I'd rather see. She won't come down and live with me. I've asked twice. And I won't give up this road to go and live in the woods. I don't think I'll ever feel quite settled about that. There's your answer, such as it is.”

I put my head against his shoulder. Both of us had our legs stretched out with our feet in the wet, watching meal settle on the floodwater. “Wouldn't it be easier if there were more of you?”

“I've thought so for years. If you're offering, you'd have to tell me. I promised your mother I wouldn't go after you for this work. You loved your father's wheel long before you had any idea what my living was.”

At fourteen I couldn't imagine being called upon to say which of two people I loved better or which work I would be doing when the pair of them had gone out of my life. “I like making pots,” I said.

“So do I like your making pots. There's an old man a way up this road so well able to use both your handles that he spills his ale all by himself now. Have you any idea what that means to a fellow who used to have to get his sister to wipe his chin for him? You can help keep a road and still go to bed white from the kiln yard.”

The miller called to us from inside. In under the roof he had two flat grain punts stored, lifted high of the winter flood with ropes running over a beam. His daughter ran the ropes out a little at a time. We watched the hull of one come down past the open door as easily as a pie being taken off a shelf. The miller had brought them in for repair that winter. He could get us over if we would take his girl aboard to bring the boat back. Ari looked at me. “It would have been worth asking about the boat after all, wouldn't it?”

We slept at the upper shrine that night. The little pot of lamp oil was there, to give a woman light to see her needle after dark, and a roll of linen for making bandages. The bird was in a wicker box cooing to itself, and Bera's cousin had drunk most of the beer we had been counting on. He had left a note to say he would come back in the morning for our parcel. We never begrudged him his drink. I sat on the floor with Ari while he had a look at his foot. He'd taken off his boot. In the stocking his toes were knocking under the wool in little jumps he couldn't seem to stop.

“Could I do that?” I asked. I eased the wool off slowly with warm water from the pot. He had a bad heel and a beautiful high arch. It seemed indecently young skin for a part of him I always thought of as the old part. I dried his foot. He showed me how to get the stocking on in the morning without dragging the skin. In the dark, with the pigeon's low chuckling at the foot of the bench, he groped for my blanket and pulled it up over my shoulder as he had when I was five. I pretended to be asleep so he could.

## Chapter Five · The Price of Barley

When I was seventeen my father began marking the bottom of the pots with my name when I had made them. That autumn I bought my own boots and took an order in my own name for a set of good drinking cups. I was carrying the finished set up the old road for delivery. My uncle had a pigeon in a wicker box on his back. Every now and then she got a beakful of his hair through the wicker and pulled. Ilve had lent him the bird at the last change of the seasons, to send her word in case of trouble. A pigeon taken from home and carried along a road could be trusted to fly the way back to the loft where it had been raised.

It was Tvímánuður, the month when people settled up for what they had borrowed earlier in the year. In those years a person who owed silver for seed could walk out into the field at harvest and see whether he would have enough to pay. All the way up from town we had watched the cut barley lying so thick on the ground that even the poor children who came after the reapers had full skirts.

There were armed men outside the grain sheds. New gates had gone up across the cart roads. A woman in the next village used to tell Ari how many geese had got into her garden. Now she told him how many wagons had been let through and how many turned back, and whether the watch still admitted anyone after dark. I delivered the drinking set at an inn before noon and took the innkeeper's silver, warm from her apron, in my hand. Beside her kitchen the town carters were watering their horses on the way up the valley with meal for the shelters. Some might think it strange work in a summer like that one. All we knew at first was what we could see. People went on nodding us through. We had walked that road for so long I sincerely believed it would always be there to walk.

In the first shrine past the barley fields we found a girl kneeling with her face deep in the meal chest. It was a great oak box, raised off the floor on stones so a rat could not get its teeth to the bottom. When her father lifted her out she had meal the whole length of her forefinger and a layer of white on her bottom lip. She had been wetting her finger to get flour out from between the boards. The two of them had come down out of the hills to find work with the harvesters. He'd spent his last pennies on a new blade for his sickle.

Ari made porridge out of the barley we carried for ourselves. When he touched her shoulder to tell her it was ready she shrank from him as if he meant to slap her. The gesture bothered me more than the sight of her tongue on that wood. I was at an age when you can be very brave on another person's behalf and loud with it. I put the bowl down hard enough to spill porridge on the bench.

“Who in this house hits children for being hungry?” I demanded.

The father looked up and I realized I had managed to accuse him in front of his daughter. “The last place put the price of a wooden token on warming yourself,” he said. “At the door my girl reached in for a twist of barley straw with the heads still on it. The man there gave her one across the fingers with the back of a spoon. We kept walking. She was good as gold all night even with that hurt hand. If it was you giving us supper I wish you would take some yourself. She's going to think she's done something wrong here too.”

I sat down beside her. Ari had put out spoons. For a while we ate without saying anything. With every second spoonful the girl gave her father a little nudge in the chest to tell him it was his turn. Eventually I told her about the pigeon who had Ari by the short hairs. She wanted to see the bird do it again. We spent a happy five minutes trying to move Ari, quite unaware, into a position suitable for getting his hair pulled.

The meal chest bore three recent chalk marks where the grain carter made his count. A town carter sometimes brought emergency stores out when an upland crop failed. The farmers would lend him a store key so he could unload while they were out working. In this case the cart tracks outside went back the other way and the last rain had washed them almost away. Nobody had unloaded three sacks that week. The keeper of the store key lived across the ford. We had delivered a mug to her the week before with a little ledge to rest her swollen thumb on, and I knew she had been at home for days with both hands bound up. We went over in the evening and heard what she had to say.

“They brought me a piece of paper. I put my mark to three sacks being sent down. They said they would be late and I needn't get up to let them through. Your pretty cup sits on the shelf there. Will you put it down where I can make use of it? I don't much like asking.”

“We'll have to hear what the farms got for their barley,” Ari told her. “And we ought to go to town and tell the people there what we're finding. How far may I go with your part in it?”

“May as well say I was a vain old fool for thinking I'd go down on a paper as the person who fed a hundred folk. What would you have me do now?”

“If someone comes for your mark again, send for me. Will you be able to do that without getting anybody into trouble?”

She sniffed. “It's you I send for when I'm already in trouble. When did we start trying to manage the order in which things happen?”

The next farm up belonged to Veka, who was building a byre with the silver she'd got for her crop. A cow kept dry in winter ate less hay than one that stood all night in wet straw fighting the cold. Her old shed had been leaning against the house so long the plaster inside had the smell of cow's breath to it. Now her husband stood in the yard fitting a new oak door for the byre. There was a great piece of good luck in the weather. The door could be hung and the roof laid before the rains.

At supper Veka told us the men who bought her barley had paid handsomely, provided she put her mark on the paper to say she'd delivered it to the shrines herself. There was a dark half-moon at the base of her thumbnail that clay seldom left on mine. She had drawn her mark with ink someone held out to her in a shallow shell.

“What do you mean, delivered it?” I asked. “You've no way to take a load like that across the yard, never mind down the road. You showed me the mare's old cart.”

“I said so at the time. The man said that was an old term everybody used in business. You've been selling your father's cups on credit. Am I meant to know less than a potter's girl about the sale of the grain I grow with my own hands?”

I ought to have kept my mouth shut. I was sure of her guilt the instant I heard her make light of my work. My uncle had finished his meal. He had the uneven tooth out on his lower lip, worrying at a strand of mutton. He could have answered her better than I did, and was waiting for me to find my own answer. This I found infuriating.

“I sold those cups to the woman at the inn. I'd think twice if a man bought them at my door and wanted me to swear I'd put them on her table. Will you come down to the bridge tomorrow and see the people whose barley you sent?”

It sounded like an accusation. I had meant it as one. Veka put the rest of her supper down and walked outside. Eventually Ari went out to her. I stayed to help her husband with the door. The oak kept sagging on my foot where the bottom corner overhung. He took the weight while I shifted my foot. That was the night I found out how remarkably difficult it is to persist in thinking a man a thief when the two of you are holding a door up between you and he has two crushed toes to go with yours.

I could hear Veka speaking to Ari in the dark by the house. “I could put the barley price back in the common bin, what's still in hand. Will they take my house from me?”

“I don't know,” he said. “I think there'll be a great many marks in that book when we find it. Somebody has bought the barley up here. Somebody else has made the farmers in the valley believe all their share went to the shrines. The town is paying to send grain to a place where last year's price would have bought two sacks for every one it's now buying. Your barley went onto a wagon you saw with your own eyes. Which way did it go? If we can follow just that one load it'll be something I can stand up and say in town.”

It seemed very quiet in the yard. She was on her way back to show us when I heard Ari quietly ask the question he had put to the woman with the lame hands, how far he might go with what Veka had to say. He had put that question when there was no one else to hear. It came to me that she had no reason whatsoever to open her mouth in front of me. She had brought us into her home and set out her best and I'd stolen the good of it from her in about the time it takes to drink a cup of milk.

In the morning she took us to a quarry road with deep ruts plowing its black earth. We walked it together to where the buyers had taken a little shed for counting out freight. Bera's cousin met us there, come over the ridge with news for Ari. He knew their mark from buying fodder in the passes. The same men who had moved Veka's barley had been provisioning armed companies in the hills. Some called their master the Hollowking. As children we had been warned about him by people as old as Bera. Once upon a time he had been a miller. In the last great war he had taken command of so many mills that he could compel starving families to give up a child in return for bread. His name was Vargeld. Everybody in our town believed that in the end the river had come up through the works below his mill and drowned him. My uncle asked the cousin whether he had seen this man with his own eyes.

“No. The companies have good boots, though. Grain enough to feed them. Ask the farmers where the leather's gone.”

We came down together. Veka took my hand at the place where there was loose stone and I have never been able to think of what I ought to have said to her then. At the meeting in the village Veka stood beside Ari. He asked the woman with the sore hands to show everyone her copy of the paper. “You signed for three sacks,” he said. “Veka sold the buyers three sacks. Look at the two marks. Same buyer. We saw the wheel ruts where he took her grain up the quarry road to the men under the mountain. Who fed the people down here in its place?”

A drover in the doorway said his sister had sent a share from her town. “She's paying more for her own meal than I've ever seen in a good year. And she’s angry. She thinks we're eating it all.”

“Then tell her where ours went. Veka got a good price for those three sacks. The soldiers got her barley. Your sister paid to put meal back in that chest, and the little girl still went hungry. When the town does send us food, we owe your sister thanks for it. We never sold the town a seat beside our fire. I want somebody who saw a child turned away for want of a token to speak to the town guards about why they're asking for them.”

The girl's father stood up. So did a woman who knew the captain of the town watch and meant to take him over that quarry road herself. Veka took her place beside them. An hour earlier I would never have believed she'd let anybody see the paper in her apron. While the captain's friend was still speaking, a man in a damp green coat on the bench nearest the door got up and went out. His shoes had the black earth from the quarry road all round the soles.

## Chapter Six · The Weight of a Man

On the third morning after that meeting we came to a beech laid across the road by a gale. I walked ahead so I could give him a hand where the wheels had cut deeply into the earth. I had an excellent view of the four men waiting on the trunk. One of them wore a green coat which had dried stiff all up one side. Wet wool shrinks if you put it too close to the fire. My mind got busy with that fact to avoid the thing I could already see, which was his hand going out to forestall Ari before he could catch up with me.

“You've been telling stories about where the king's barley goes.”

“I've been saying where it went as far as I could follow it. I'd be glad to see where you took it after that.” I could hear him coming up on my left. He put his pack down and the pigeon shook herself in the wicker.

“We'll take your little girl on ahead. You can talk to somebody about having her back when we're sure you're through spreading rumors.”

“Run, Sif.”

I did start. A hand caught my wrist and took the skin clean off it as I twisted away. For some reason I could hear cherries falling out of a bowl, the noise they made rolling in the dirt. Once on the kiln step at home I had been eating cherries while the woman who shod our carthorse taught Ari to fight with the sword and the little blade called a dirk he carried at his belt. They went barefoot because the heats of summer had split the ground and they wanted to feel where they put their feet. They had wooden blades which rang almost cheerfully when the one struck against the other. I had laughed at Ari the sixth time she put him on his back. The smith had helped him up, put the wooden dirk in his hand and said, very levelly, “That's six times he's trusted me to let him learn.” I had got up so fast the cherries went over. He had sat in the dust and picked them up with me, flushed from the work, pleased with what he had learned, as easy in his skin as he was on the far side of the river with an egg to share.

It was the look in his eye, when the man grabbed me, that brought me back to the road. I had seen that fear before, once when I stepped on loose stones above a gravel pit. I had never seen him look at another human being with such anger. Then there was the trunk between us and I was on my knees in the leaves below it. I heard a blow through the wood under my palms. Somebody trod on my skirt. A man fell on his face in the leaves beside me. His neck came down across my arm. I remember exactly how much heat one patch of living skin could give off on a crisp morning.

Ari hauled me up. All four men were down. I went from one to the next looking to see if any were breathing. I couldn't find breath in any of the four. When I turned to my uncle he was seated hard up against the fallen beech, pressing his coat against his lower side with both hands. Blood had run down into his boot. There was red on the handprint he had left on my dress when he got me up.

“Come and sit with me a minute,” he said.

I held the folded cloth he gave me to his side. For a little while he put his head against my shoulder, hanging onto me so hard we rocked a little together. Up in the branches I heard the pigeon's familiar chuckling. The fall had sprung the wicker lid on its leather hinges. Ari whistled, and held out some barley on his palm. I got the bird down and he had me take the little glass vial out of the wooden tube at her leg. It had a twist of paper inside with *a bed for two* in his small writing. He'd hoped to let Ilve know we meant to come at the end of the round. Now he told me to get the message to her as fast as the bird could go. “Tell her the pond path, Sif. I know my way when the water's down.” I kept his writing at the top and wrote very small underneath it. *Ari hurt. Taking the pond path. Come with wheels.* I put the vial back in its tube. Her wings struck twice beside my cheek and I had lost sight of her.

“Is Ilve's the nearest house?”

“Nearest we can reach without going back through the village. They've stopped wagons on the road. Where we're going I haven't seen a wagon for thirty years.” He was very pale below the place where his cap shaded his forehead. “Do you remember the lost road that goes into the pond? There's a shrine just past it at the entrance to the Great Green.”

I had grown up knowing about the old Greenweald that clothed the slopes to the east. You could buy wood from it in any town. The Great Green was the forest you walked into in stories, where a child counted a dozen paces under the boughs and her grandchildren came to lead her home. The notion that a person I ate supper with could give directions there exasperated me in a way I had no time to sort out. “You let me think we would disturb the ducks.”

“We would have, in nesting time. It's nearly autumn. There's a ridge round the pond that dries enough to walk on after haytime. I know where it starts. Sif, give me a little help up. I'll do what I can for myself.”

The first day was the worst for pretending. We both hoped the first half mile would get him going. He said the boot felt better once we were walking. I told him the weather was holding. In truth my left knee was already shaking and the sweat was running cold down his face. Every time we came to an open patch I could see clear across the valley to farms I knew. The sound of a wooden mallet reached us from some roof a man was trying to get finished before morning. The places that had once been a night's dry lodging were places where hoofbeats might announce us long before we arrived.

At a little clump of birches he slid down onto a flat stone. “This might suit us for dinner,” he said. There were only two pieces of Bera's bread and I found myself stretching the cloth out very neatly on his knees as if all the rest of dinner would appear if I fussed long enough. He could hardly swallow. I took the cup out of my clothes and went down to the trickle at the bottom of the bank. Once, walking back up from there, I had wet a finger and run it round and round the rim to try and make my own cup sing. Here was that same walk, the cup brimming, and the man on the stone unable to lift it.

I put the high handle against his hand and got my arm behind his neck. He managed it. There were blackberries down by the trickle. I crushed three between finger and thumb and let him smell the juice before I put it to his mouth. The taste brought some color into his lips.

“Do you want to lie here while I go for help?” I asked.

“I'd like that old cart to come and find me. I won't make it back up this bit after dark, and you don't know the dry ground beyond. Stay with me if you can.”

I was ashamed of how much I had wanted to put the full weight of him down and go. I had imagined everybody who had ever carried a sick uncle over a hill doing it all in one swell of sorrow and devotion. In my case I had thoughts on the order of *If he tries to lift that foot while I'm still trying to turn, I shall scream*. I told him this some distance later when he had managed to sit down on a grassy bank without taking me under him. To my surprise he laughed. It hurt him and he put his hand on his side.

“Next time say it when it does some good. I can't see your feet below me and even if I could I have never done this business before.”

“Neither have I. When I tell you to stand up it feels like giving an order to my grandfather.”

“Do you like it when I give you orders with that awful wise look on my face?”

“No.”

“Imagine what it's like to sit here with you doing it to me.”

I set my face the way his went when I did something that he could see going wrong a long way off. He shut one eye and tilted his chin until he achieved a fair likeness of me looking at my pottery on a rich woman's shelf. We got something very like an honest dinner hour out of those few minutes on the grass.

We spent that night where a charcoal burner had once piled wood and covered it with earth to let it smolder slowly into black fuel for a forge. A low, half-collapsed hut stood at one end of a level patch of ground stained black. It gave us two walls against the wind. I laid a little wood fire, warmed water and got the sticky stocking off his bad foot, as he had once let me do by the ford. His wound had bled again. I folded clean linen against it. He was too tired to help me. In his pack, under the breadcloth, was the smooth leather case around his sword. I wiped it where he'd laid bloody fingers on it and couldn't bring myself to touch the hilt again. Up close I could see it had worn to the shape of his thumb as the broom at home had.

His sister would have given all she had to be in my place. I couldn't keep from seeing him rise up on hands and knees that morning amongst the men who lay so still. I sat in the hut and wished I could say what was in my head without putting him back on that road with them. After a time I found he was looking at me.

“Is your arm hurt badly, Sif?”

“It's sore. Will you tell me something? Did you ever have to do that before?”

“Yes. Once before you were born. I could tell you his name if you wanted. I never did find out anything about the men this morning except that they could have walked past us, all four, if they'd wanted.”

“I don't want to know a name.”

“I could bear having you put your hand on mine for a little while.”

It was only his hand, onions and fresh clay long gone from under the nails. I put my palm down. His fingers closed, and after a while they opened and lay at ease. In the night his hand came up to feel my forehead, checking as he had when I was little and had been too long walking in the sun. I let him do it. I wished I had spoken more gently to him on the way up to our dinner place.

In the morning I woke to the smell of soap in the linen under his coat. At home it was Laugardagr, washing day. My mother would be up before sunrise heating water, with our back door open to let out the steam. I wanted that sound of the sheets being slapped against the smooth stone at the well. I wanted it so badly that, for a moment, I stayed where I was and listened. Then Ari called my name. I had to help him onto his knees and wait till he found the strength to come up the rest of the way. He could walk the length of a house, then not much farther until he had sat down. The little red veins at either side of his nose had disappeared. His foot came up against a log and I had to sit him sideways on it, kneel and move his heels round to the far side. At every resting place he gave me something to look out for on the next piece. That way if he died before we got to the alder trees I would still have some chance of finding my way. I had come of age in the course of one very long day and had no desire to be a day older.

I was so busy watching his face that I nearly walked into the pond. Ari bent his face down toward my neck. “Listen.”

There was the noise of a whole flock of ducks coming off the water. There were their untidy nests, well empty, in the sides of the alder. I laughed till he sat down beside me, and then we looked at each other with our cheeks wet.

On the far side the line of old paving turned and came up narrow and hard out of the reeds. The trees appeared to close behind it, although beyond their roots I could see a much greater space, all green above and below with a cool yellow light wavering between. A woman with long pointed ears was pushing a little two-wheeled cart straight at us along the paving. It lurched over a root. The wheel squealed when she took a turn too sharply. She had made a bed of straw inside with a quilt laid out ready for a sick person. On the handle rode the pigeon from Ari's basket.

## Chapter Seven · Before We Set Out

Ilve had put on an old sheepskin coat over her nightshirt when the bird came. By the time she had got the cart to us a wheel had sunk twice. She had spent the night sitting in it with a blanket about her legs, then gone on where the path held the wheels. Her bare knees under her nightshirt were painted with lines of dried mud.

“What made you go getting old on me in that wood?” she demanded. “You could have stayed as you were the last time I saw you till I got used to you looking that way.”

He held out a hand. “I seem to be past the age of lifting myself into your cart, sweetling.”

“Then you'd better give Sif room to get at you.” I knew Ilve through three years of letters and all the stories Ari told whenever anything went wrong on the road. I had never heard her voice before. She said my name as easily as my mother did. All those evenings he had been writing about me, too. I wondered whether he’d ever told her how we divided the egg.

I could get him to lie comfortably with his head on my lap. She rubbed his feet between her two hands and gave me the quilt to tuck round them. She got into the harness and drew us down between the trees, stopping to ease the cart over each root so it hardly jolted. It was warm so far into the forest, moist as the inside of my father's drying shed. From the high branches water fell on us even in the sunshine. Little ridges of bright green moss grew along the seams in her roof. Ilve lived at the shrine itself. One little Ember burned in the stone beside the door for strangers coming out of the trees, as ours did above the ford. Beyond it was the water barrel, there the privy, there the sleeping room with a raised threshold to keep the mice from getting straight through. At the entrance to a country children only got to see in tales, somebody had forgotten to take in the washing.

Ilve was Tonttu. Among her people the healing herbs grew in warm earth round their woodland springs. She had a great sheaf of dried leaves hanging from the rafters, each stem identified with a different knot for finding it by touch on a winter night. For all that stood at her hand, I could see by the way she felt his pulse through the thin skin at his temple that she was only looking to keep him easy. Once she had the dirty linen off and his hurts washed and dressed she brought him broth in the green jar my father had put a bird on. It had lost half a wing in a chip. She knew just how soft to keep her voice to let him follow her, and she waited a long time after she spoke to hear what he wanted to say.

“There's a pigeon that knows your sister's loft,” she said. “Will you have Sif send for her?”

He nodded. I had some difficulty getting the words small enough, with the light gone out of the window and my wrist stiff. My uncle used to be able to fold a year of my life into a square of paper that hardly covered the second joint of his thumb.

When I got back he was saying, “Of course the girl must have the choice. If she's made up her mind for the kiln she'd be good at that living. I think there's work in her for the roads. Could you bear to have her with you?”

“I have been waiting since she was knee-high for you to let me meet her,” Ilve said. “If you want me to keep her company I'd take an introduction quite kindly. She's just come in.”

He turned his face to me. “What do you think, little goose?”

“I think you should have introduced us long ago. I knew you loved each other. I never saw you at the same table. I helped wrap that jar and a hundred times I've wondered whether the color came out right in her house. What did you imagine I'd do if you brought me to supper?”

“I should have looked so foolish going home of a morning with my shirt inside out and a girl asking where I had been.”

In the face of losing him I felt so much pain at the image of him blushing by our back door in his good years that I thought I might fall down on the spot. Ilve took my hand and drew me down beside him. Ari made a little room between his hand and the edge of the blanket for mine.

“I want the road,” I told him. “I want you to teach me. If I can't have that, I'd like to learn with someone who knew you when you went to bed afraid.”

He gave Ilve a look. “I told her about the old coat. On that understanding, will you take her? I want her to know how to make a healing compress and how to lift it off a wound without taking the new skin with it. Teach her to find water off the old road. Teach her the blade, too. I know what she saw in the wood. I would like her to be able to remember something of you when she takes up a blade for herself.”

Ilve put a hand to the side of his face. He turned his lips into it. “I'll take her out with me when she's ready to go,” she said. “Did you tell her how long before I thought you were fit to go alone?”

“Couldn't possibly have been more than a week.”

“Three years. Nearly four.” She bent her head to kiss him. His hand came up to the back of her neck with such easy familiarity I looked away. There were the fire, my own coat on a peg, three teeth of a comb he had once had at home in our loft and that must have gone with him into the forest. I fetched the stool and put it on the other side of the bed for her. Then I drew my knees up beside his pillow. The last thing I remember he did for himself was to turn toward her, the two of them making room for each other under a cover the way people do who have gone to bed together often. He died with the pair of us beside him a little before morning.

My mother arrived that evening. She stood absolutely still inside the door in the clean clothes she'd put on in hopes of being his sister sitting up with him through a bad night. Then she saw the shirt we had taken off him hanging on the peg with the bloodied linen ready for the wash. My mother was the woman who had pushed that same boy in the water, lifted him out herself and carried him screaming to a dry place with a sliver of pot in his foot. I had never seen her with nobody to tell her what to do with her hands before. Ilve took one and led her to him.

In the morning we buried him in a patch of bare earth under the hazels. He had a cherry stone in his pocket, ground smooth at one end where he used to work it between finger and thumb. His sister knew it at once. She and her brothers had carved little notches on cherry stones to wager in guessing games. She put it back with him. Then she sat with Ilve by the bed while I went to have my say with whoever was listening at the fire.

I knelt on the packed earth and thought of those four men. Even at the very last, with family on either side of him, there had been a twitch at the corner of his mouth when he tried to get his breath. I could hear the man in the green coat calling me his little girl. I'd made and sold a whole drinking set in my own right. He hadn't even asked my name. A man might have a sister or a daughter and still be willing to tell somebody else's uncle what he'll do to his niece.

“Is that what I was to find out?” I said.

There was a man sitting on the step, working the dull brass mouthpiece out of a horn. “Tell me what you've learned, Sif.”

“That a man can get up and dress himself and have breakfast and go out with three of his friends to do what they did. That you can pass a little girl eating meal off her finger and the price of her supper will buy a new door. I think I could have lived very well never knowing those things. And I want him back.”

I sat down and put both hands over my face. The smell that came off them was of crushed hazel leaves and his old coat. Presently I felt the man's knee beside mine on the step.

“What was he like on a good day?” he asked.

I had been trying all morning to remember him before those two days' walking, to put the color back beside his nose and get him up off that log on his own feet. A finger of sun came through the eaves and fell on my left eyebrow. I remembered ale caught in somebody else's.

“He could balance a drinking cup on his head and go through a whole dance with it full. He took me to a hay supper once and we never got there. Two brothers were fighting at the bridge. He sat up with them till they could go home, and when they'd had enough supper he got the elder man to play for us. He'd seen the fellow couldn't see to tend his own sheep. He knew how much it would please him to be asked to play. And it was my own stupid cup, which he liked well enough to carry round the country with him for years.”

Somewhere in the shrine behind me my mother was telling Ilve how my father had fired that green jar three times to get the color on the bird. They might have known one another all their lives if he'd only owned up sooner to a woman he wanted to keep company with in the woods.

“Yes, there are men like those four,” the man on the step said. “But on the morning you knew what they were worth, had you guessed that before he went out that morning he'd written you into his visit to the woman he loved? He'd had that twist of paper ready all along. Or that she'd be waiting up all night in a cart to come and fetch you?”

I thought of the twist of paper with room for two in his own handwriting before anybody had struck him. I saw him asking me again by the mill if I wanted the road, and I thought of the cloudy eye of a man who'd once wiped his fiddle and set it under his chin so a child could have a dance. This was the first thought of the future I could bear, that one of these evenings the old man might like somebody to come round with a cup made for his hands, and I knew where he lived.

“Is this how it starts for you?” I asked. “One day there's somebody out there who needs you to turn up, and you go?”

“It's how I came to be at a crossing with a horn in my hand. You'll have your own road to tell of.”

I let him sit beside me until I had warmed my knees. When I got up the door was open. Ilve had a basket full of clean linen ready to go out and word from the neighboring farm that rain had got in through the roof and soaked the beds. We could give the children something dry to sleep in that night. My mother had put my hair ribbon and a small knife for cutting clay back in my pack. Of all the things she could have said about what might be waiting for me out there, she took my face in her hands and asked whether I'd let her know when the time came to fire another load of tiles.

I took the linen and went out with Ilve. I could hear my mother's broom going over the threshold behind us. Ilve's walk was quicker than Ari's. As we came up the first rise the cloth began to pull on the bruise under my arm. Ordinarily I should have tried to keep up and hoped she would never see me struggling. I set the basket down.

“Could we change sides?” I said. “That arm still hurts me.”

She changed without breaking stride for more than a moment. Then I lifted my end. Between us we got it up the hill.
