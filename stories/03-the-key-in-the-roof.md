# The Key in the Roof

## Chapter One · A Potter’s Daughter

Her father marked every tile he fired with a goose walking backward. When people asked why it walked backward, he said that if they knew which way the goose intended to go, they were welcome to take over the business. Sif had learned to draw that goose in soft clay before she could write her name.

One wet evening they came up the old road to the shrine where her mother’s brother kept a little fire alive for travelers. On stormy nights you could see the red light of the Ember a good way down the road. Her uncle kept the spare key under a loose roof tile so latecomers could find their way to a dry bed. Sif knew the tile. She had marked it herself. Her goose had six toes on one foot. Tonight there was a guard posted directly underneath it.

“Have you a token?” he asked.

Her father opened his hand to show the two coppers he kept for road tolls. “One of these?”

“From the city,” he explained. He was young enough to have a little red patch at his chin where he had cut himself trying to shave. “It shows who paid for supplies.”

Her father pointed up. “I could bring you down half the roof as my receipt.”

The guard looked worried enough to make Sif believe he might say yes. While the men argued she went round the back and rattled the drain cover. When she was five, her uncle had taken her there after supper and told her to listen. Three soft rattles, a pause and two more. “Only children or rats,” he said. “And I should hope a rat would come better dressed.” He had kept a dry shift her size on the peg long after she outgrew it.

He took one look at her and opened the door to her father. “You’ve grown,” he said. “Or the coat’s shrunk. Either way, take it off.”

In the morning the guard came for her uncle. He had admitted two travelers without asking to see their tokens. Sif watched the irons go on. She and her father had eaten at home. All they had wanted from the shrine was a dry place to sleep. Her father stood there in his dry coat with a hand on the back of her neck. She felt his fingers shake.

“I knew how to get in,” she said. “I rattled the cover.”

“Go inside,” her father said.

The guard shifted his grip on her uncle’s elbow. “Would you have had me leave the door shut on her?” her uncle asked him.

“I have to take you to the lady’s court. Ask her that when you get there.”

Nobody had ever gone off in irons for a thing Sif had done. She used to think you could tell anyone anything if you could only get a minute to speak. She got her minute now. The cart went all the same.

## Chapter Two · A Better Witness

The town sent a man to ask for her uncle’s hearing. Sif knew him by his boots. He had brought them to her father for clay to stop a hole. Now she saw the dried plug drop out on the step as he undid his sword belt. The blade never cleared its sheath. A man came from beside a wagon, the envoy fell, and the yard filled with people all trying to see over one another’s heads.

A carter held Sif still while the crowd went past. From where she stood she could see his face, and beyond his shoulder the man who had struck the blow. They had the same long cleft chin.

“Yours?” she said.

He let go of her so quickly she stumbled. “My eldest.”

She thought he meant to go after his son. Instead he went to harness the horse. “I have to tell Lady Inga what happened before her guards tell it for me.”

“Take me,” said Sif.

“I’ve room beside me for one more. If I find my son, he’ll be in that seat.”

She understood how big a thing he meant to do only when she saw him shake out the folded rug under the wagon board and lay it on the bare seat. He must have been driving this road with his son beside him since the boy was small. She thought of her father’s hands the day they had unloaded the first roof tiles here. The chipped ones were hers. He had let her carry them herself.

“Then take the tile,” said Sif.

“What would I want with a tile?”

She got a ladder. Her father held it while she worked her goose out from under the eave. She slipped the spare key into her pocket and handed the tile down. “Tell her what we were doing here in the first place.”

For the first mile the carter could be heard cursing every time it knocked against the board under his seat.

## Chapter Three · A Patch of Sky

They heard the war coming before they saw the first soldiers. The river carried sound a long way on a still morning. First bells from the landings, then horns, and by evening whole families were coming in from the passes. Men loyal to Vargeld, the king everyone had believed drowned beneath his mill, had come up behind the mountain guards and taken the roads.

Sif helped her father at the shrine. The guard who had once asked for a token came back with a little girl riding his hip. He had lost his cap. He stood in the rain looking at the place where the goose had been.

“Her mother was on the other side of the ford,” he said.

Sif went for the blanket her uncle had once fetched for her. Until then she had supposed she would enjoy the day that guard needed a favor. She found she wanted very badly to wring somebody’s neck, and equally badly to get the wet child into dry clothes. She stripped the soaked sleeves off the girl. The little shift on the peg fit her with barely a finger to spare.

“Were you going to stand there in the rain till somebody came for you?” she asked him.

“I took a man out of this house in irons. I didn’t think his brother would let me in.”

“His sister married my father. That makes me his niece.” She buttoned the child up crooked the first time and had to do it again. “You can fetch me a cup of water. They’re behind you.”

He stayed the night. Before he left he wrote out what had happened on the back of one of his orders. The Ember Tender had opened the door himself. Two travelers had slept in blankets the shrine already owned. No food had been taken. Sif signed underneath him and gave the paper to a woman traveling down to the coast. She had wanted to ask for his captain’s seal as well. In the end she decided a man could answer for what he had seen by putting his own name to it.

The tile came back late that autumn in her uncle’s hands. For one foolish instant Sif was so glad to see the goose that she called it by name before she said a word to him. He grinned. He had been set free at the coast, he told her, and walked home alongside the carter, who had brought the tile the whole way wrapped in a shirt. She found the man outside resting on a stick. He had lost his horse.

“Did she see it?” Sif asked.

“Yes. She turned it over and counted the toes. Wanted to know how old the maker was.”

“What did you tell her?”

“I told her to come up and ask you herself.”

“Who spoke for my uncle?”

“He did. He read her your paper. Then she sent for my son.” He dragged the stick through the dust between his feet. “My lad will stand trial for the envoy. Your uncle walked out the door.”

Sif sat with him on the step until her father came out with a bowl for him. She had imagined that getting somebody back would feel like the moment a fired pot rang true in her hand. Her uncle was inside laughing over the six-toed goose. Beside her was a man who still had to walk into his house alone.

Sif set the spare key back on the rafter and laid the goose over it. She was still on the ladder when she saw another family coming, two adults supporting somebody between them on a door. The carter saw them too. He opened the gate and went down to meet them. She waited on the ladder long enough to show the smallest child where to find the key.
