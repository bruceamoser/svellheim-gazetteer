# The Key in the Roof

## Chapter One · The Goose

My uncle let me put six toes on the goose. I knew how many a goose had. There was one in our yard that had chased me to the privy often enough for me to know a good deal about geese. But the clay was sweet to cut, and the first three toes came out so well that I went round the foot again.

“There's a bird that'll stay on a wet roof,” my uncle said.

My father came to see what we were laughing at. He took up the tile we had made together, turned it over in his hands and set it with the good ones for firing. Ordinarily a tile of mine went under a crock to keep the kitchen table from rocking. I was five. I would have given either of those men my wages for a year if I had had any.

My uncle's name was Ari. He was my mother's youngest brother. When he came off the road he smelled of wet wool, onions and a salve he rubbed on his feet. He slept in our loft with his long legs sticking out past the mattress. I used to go up there in the morning and bend his big toe until he admitted to being awake. I was allowed these liberties because he generally got home after I had gone to bed and left again while I was asleep. A girl had to make some arrangement for seeing him.

He took me with him when he went to lay the tiles. Our cart went first through town, past the flame spring where a seam in the earth stood open and fire came out of it. Houses and shops had grown up the hill on either side. The woman whose business it was to look after the flame had a broom in her hands. She was sweeping grit out of the way of an old man with bare, swollen feet. She and Ari exchanged news. Then we went out by the drovers' road and came to a stone house small enough to have fitted in our kiln yard.

Inside, in a cup of black stone set into the floor, burned a little Ember. I had expected to see a woman with a broom.

“Where's its Keeper?”

“At the spring, where you saw her. Out here it gets me.”

“What are you called?”

“An Ember Tender. Fetch that broom if you want to know the difference between riding with one and being one.”

Under the bench I found a baby's stocking, three plum stones and the missing ear of a very small clay horse. Ari put the stocking and the ear on the windowsill. People sometimes came back for things. We swept the dust away from the fire, got fresh straw down on the sleeping benches and climbed the ladder. Under the lip of my tile he laid an iron key. He made me reach up from the top rung and find it with my eyes shut.

“The door sticks when the weather swells it. Come in through the shed and lift the latch from inside. This opens the shed. If somebody can't find it, what can you tell them?”

“Find the goose. It's the one with the right number of feet and the wrong number of toes.”

I had my face close to the tiles. I can smell the baking clay yet, the first time the sun got on that roof.

That evening two brothers came in separately. The elder had the younger's sheep, and the younger had a split lip the shape of his brother's ring. Ari made them leave their knives in the shed. Both men were entitled to supper and a dry place until morning. My uncle put me to bed behind the grain chest, where I could listen to them quarrel in whispers for fear of waking me. By daylight two neighbors had come to hear the dispute. The older brother showed them a mark in the sheep's ear. A ewe had got into the wrong fold in the dark. The younger brother went home with the ewe and a wedge of cheese for his lip.

“Why didn't you tell them whose sheep it was?” I asked.

“I could barely tell which was the sheep. You have to ask somebody who knows the flock. All I had to do was get them through supper.”

He let me ride home on his shoulders. For years I thought that was what a day's walk cost him, the weight of a child who occasionally put a heel in his throat.

## Chapter Two · A Man at the Door

As I grew bigger, I went farther along his round. At supper on a farm I once asked who had decided to pay Tenders to walk the old roads. The farmer said his grandmother had put barley into the first common bin in the district. His wife took a different view.

“Your grandmother was still in nappies when Sela broke her hip.”

She told me how rain had come under the door of Sela's shrine and stood round the little Ember. All the men on that road had sweated over the paving with crowbars till somebody fetched Sela herself, flat on her back on a door. She told them to lift the stone with the grass growing beside it. There was the drain. At harvest the farms agreed to feed an apprentice as well as a Tender, so the next person in charge would know where things were. Other roads made their own arrangements. The farmer's grandmother had given her best barley every year she lived.

“She was in nappies once,” his wife conceded.

As for who built those roads, my mother said the Watchman had once walked a company of children home in the dark and that the small Embers had sprung up wherever he stopped to count them. The shepherds above our town said you could follow the shrines to the place the sun went underground. Ari knew three lost stretches personally. One ended in a duck pond. I wanted desperately to know which story was true. He said I'd have to go a good deal farther than the duck pond to find out.

I was seven the night I met the man with the horn. A woman had come in off the road in labor. I had been sent to watch the door and give a shout if her husband arrived. For a long while I sat making myself look responsible. Then there was a man sitting on the step beside me, taking a little brass horn apart to get a grass seed out of the mouthpiece.

“Are you the husband?”

“Do I look worried enough?”

I considered this. “She has sworn at everybody in there except me.”

“You're well placed, then.” He gave me the mouthpiece. I had smaller fingers. “What will you do when the child comes?”

“Go get clean sheets. What will you do?”

“Keep the way open. I have a deal of that to do.”

I asked him what my uncle had asked of me on my first morning, whether he could find the key with his eyes shut. He reached up, sitting where he was, and took it out from under the goose. No man's arm could reach that far from the step. I was much too interested to be frightened. “Are you Hallinskidi?” I asked. My mother said that was the Watchman's name.

“You'll keep a road of your own, Sif. I've seen you at it.”

“Who will have this road if Uncle gives it to me?”

“Ask him again when you know why he does it. You'll see that too.”

I had wanted him to say something about the children and the duck pond. Then a man came panting up the road with a bunch of wilted yellow flowers in his hand. “I can hear her,” he said. “Is that good?”

When I looked round again I was on the step with a grass seed on my tongue. I might have dozed there waiting. My uncle had my promise to tell him the instant the husband came. I shouted so loud that the new baby began to cry before I got the door open.

## Chapter Three · The Empty Bins

At seventeen I walked the round in boots I had bought myself. The guards who stopped the wagons waved us through. They could see what we carried. There was oil, salve, a change of linen and whatever some traveler had left behind and then remembered a shrine too late. That summer we walked eighty miles with a dead woman's teeth in a box before we caught up with her daughters.

One evening we found a little girl on her knees running a wet finger between the boards of a meal chest and licking off the flour. Three sacks, according to the carter's mark on the wall, had come in that week. Ari made some porridge from our own store and sat the girl in front of it. Her father had let the city's carter have a key so he could unload. The grain came from farms nearer the coast when the upland barley failed. This year the barley stood thick in the fields all the way up the valley. We had watched men taking it in.

At the next farm a woman called Veka was building a byre with the silver she had got for her barley. Her purchaser had paid over market price, provided she put her mark on his paper to say she had carted the grain to the shrines herself.

“Have you?” Ari asked.

“You saw my wagon. It hasn't had a rear axle since the old mare sat on it.”

“Would you say that before your neighbors?”

She took us into the yard. Her husband was there fitting the new byre door, a beautiful thing rounded at the top. They had not had the money for such a door in all their married lives. Veka put her hand on it. “Will they want me to give this back?”

“I don't know,” said Ari. “I would like you to come and see what a child was eating yesterday. You can decide for yourself whether you want to say anything.”

She went down the road with him that night. I stayed to help her husband hang the door. In the dark, with a good oak plank balanced on my foot, I began to understand why my uncle was sometimes days late getting home.

There were other farms, other empty bins. In one town a guard told us you had to pay toward provisions now to get shelter. The town had been buying barley for its own hungry households at the price Veka had been paid. Its people thought the shrines had eaten the difference. Ari told them what he had seen and named the agent who had bought the grain. Somebody near the door said the same man had bought horses under the mountain for Vargeld. That made the room go quiet. The king who once held children for flour was supposed to have drowned under his own mill generations ago.

On the third morning after that meeting we found four men sitting on a fallen beech. They had provisions for a day and clean boots. They had come out from somewhere quite close.

“You've been telling stories about where the king's barley goes,” one said.

Ari shifted his pack. “I've been asking where it went. If you'd like to show me, I'm willing to go see.”

“Give us the girl. We'll see how willing you are on the way home.”

Ari glanced at me. “Run, Sif.”

A hand went round my arm. For the space of a breath I was back in my father's yard, eating cherries on the kiln step. On summer evenings my uncle and the woman who shod our cart horse used to practice there. They took their shoes off. I remember their feet in the dust and how badly they smelled when they took up their coats again. One evening I had laughed at Ari for falling down six times. The smith had passed him his practice dirk, helped him up and said, “That makes six times he's trusted me to let him learn.” I had thought being a Tender meant you got to stop and do whatever pleased you on your way to work.

Then I saw my uncle's face. He was afraid of what would happen to me. The man holding my arm saw it too and grinned. I have regretted seeing that grin for the whole of my life.

I heard a scuffle, a cry that went high like a hurt dog's, the ring of iron on the road. I was on my knees under the beech. My uncle was calling my name. I came out. All four men lay about him. One still had the skin off my wrist under his nails. Ari himself was standing with a dark patch spreading down both legs of his breeches.

“I should have run when you said.”

“I should have let you finish picking the cherries that day. Come here and hold me a minute.”

## Chapter Four · The Great Green

It was his bird I heard first, its low familiar chuckling up in the beech. A Tender took birds from one shrine to the next so there was always one that knew its way back. He'd been carrying this one for a friend. The wicker top had come off his pack in the fight. Ari poured some barley into my hand and whistled her down. He had me slip a little vial into the tube at her leg. There was one twist of paper in it. In his own writing, quite plain, a request for a bed for two.

“You had that written already?”

“I like going places where they're pleased to see me. We're going to the shrine at the entrance to the Great Green.”

I knew the Great Green. In nursery tales people slipped through a gap in the trees at midday and came out the same age a hundred years later. “We could go back to Veka's,” I said.

“Their friends know that way. I know this one. But you're going to have to lend me a leg.”

We got as far as a charcoal burner's old camp by nightfall. I found a piece of sacking with a man's name picked out on it in green. I put it under him. Once in the night I felt his hand on my face, feeling for the fever I used to get after long days in the sun.

“It's me who should do that,” I told him.

“I was doing it before you could hold your head up. Give a man a little notice if you mean him to stop.”

He slept after that. I took his sword out into the clearing. I wanted very much to have it in my hand if I heard anyone coming. I also wanted to set it down somewhere and leave it behind when we went. It had the worn place on the grip from his thumb that the handle of our old broom had.

The second day we walked the length of a house and rested, walked the length of a house and rested again. He had lost the red veins beside his nose. He could no longer lift his foot over a log. He sat on one side and I took his heels and moved him round like a hand on a clock. Once he was silent so long I bent down to listen.

“Sif,” he whispered. “There's the damned pond.”

It was full of ducks. I began laughing and had to sit down with him. There was a road after all, on the far side, under the overhanging alder. At the end of it a woman with long pointed ears was coming toward us pushing a little handcart, the pigeon on her shoulder. Of all the things I have prayed for, that was the one sent me before I thought to ask.

Her name was Ilve. She was one of the Tonttu, the elves whose people had made their homes in the great woods. She had black earth on her knees and a gray streak in one eyebrow where her hair had grown in white over a burn. She got Ari into the cart and called him a great sack of onions. He called her sweetling. The smile she gave him made me wonder how much he had done with his life while I was waiting for him to come to our loft.

At the shrine she warmed water, eased the cloth away and dressed his wounds. He asked her how he stood. She sat on the edge of the bed and put his hand into her lap.

“I'd send a bird for your sister tonight,” she said.

“Thank you. There's my niece to consider.”

“She's in the room, Ari. Her ears still work.”

He turned to me. His eyes were as alive as ever. “Is this still what you want, little goose?”

“I want you to teach me.” It was the first time I had said it to him. I could have been at his heels a hundred years and I would have waited for him to ask.

“I'll have to leave something for Ilve. I want her to teach you how to put a compress on without skinning your patient when you lift it. And how to read the land well enough to find the road to my sister's.”

“And the blade?” Ilve asked.

He shut his eyes. “Yes. She ought to know what she can do if a man puts his hand on her. I would like her to have a good, patient teacher.” He looked at her again. “I learned a deal from mine.”

She bent and kissed him then. I could see by the way his hand came up to the back of her head that it was a thing they had done before. I fetched her a stool, put it on the other side of the bed and took my place beside him.

He lived long enough to hear the pigeon come back into the loft. I was telling him my mother would have the whole family on the road by morning when he died.

## Chapter Five · Before We Set Out

We laid him in a patch of bare earth under the hazels. My mother came in time to see him buried. She had brought a clean shirt from home and the cherry stones we used for counters at cards. One was lost. According to her, her brother still had it in his pocket from cheating the night before I was born. She went through his pockets and there it was.

After she had gone I stayed at the shrine to tell the Watchman exactly what I thought of what had been done to my uncle. Ilve was waiting outside with the packs. I was to walk with her to a house that had lost its roof in the rains. Presently there was a man at the door turning the mouthpiece of a horn in his fingers.

“You again,” I said.

“Did you find out what you wanted to know?”

“Yes. Four men can get together in the morning, eat a good breakfast and come out quite pleased with themselves to put their hands on a girl. And if the people on her road go hungry, there are men who will grow rich off it.”

He sat in the place where Ilve had pushed the bed back against the wall. “That's a fair account of those men. What would your uncle think if he heard you tell his story and those four were the only people in it?”

I had been kneeling some time. My knees hurt. I got up and sat by him.

“The last two days I thought I was getting him to help,” I said. “When we reached the ducks, I could have left him under a tree and gone for her. He knew that. Why did he insist I take him the whole way?”

“What did he ask when he got here?”

“Whether I wanted the work.”

“Who could have answered him from the woods?”

I put my head in my hands. Veka had put her palm flat against a beautiful door before she came out to see about another woman's child. Ilve had taken a cart she used for apples and carried the man she loved home to die in it. There was something I wanted to ask and it took me a moment before I could trust my voice.

“When you saw me on my road,” I said, “was she there?”

“For a good long way.”

I sat with him a little while longer. From outside came the small everyday sound of somebody getting impatient with a buckle. I kissed two fingers and put them to the place on the bed where my uncle's hand had lain. Then I went out to Ilve.

“Will we pass my mother's on this road?” I asked her.

“If we start today.”

I took the heavier pack. We had a roof to see to, and I knew a place to get good tiles.
