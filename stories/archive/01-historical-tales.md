# Tales from Svellheim’s History

At Lake Urdvatn the War of the Dead is remembered by the names of two women who lived on opposite shores. In the river country the account begins with the promise of a boat. Here are the longer tellings, from the refuge at Reedbank to the living memory of the third winter.

## The War of the Dead

### The quarrel at Urdvatn

The oldest quarrel remembered of the First War began over a boat on Urdvatn, the deep lake below the mountains of Midgard's Shield. By then there were families throughout Svellheim who could name someone who had died and come home. The Barrowbound, as these people were called, spoke of meeting Hel, queen of the dead, and of a promise each had made her for the privilege of coming home. What was new on the eastern shore was a house where several of the Barrowbound could live together. Their neighbors also called them the Returned. The fishing households of Reedbank had given them the use of a net hall above the landing. By autumn there was a second door in its gable, a little garden behind it, and a path worn down to the water.

Across the lake stood Cairn Ness. Its households kept the burial ground on the headland, where even the drowned whose names were unknown received a grave and a coin for Hel. That summer they buried a boatwright named Aldis. Her daughter laid the coin beneath her tongue. Nine mornings later Aldis came to Reedbank with clay in the seams of her hands. She asked for an adze, the tool she used to shape a plank. Before she died she had taken payment for a boat and spent it feeding her family. She said Hel had let her come back to finish it.

The woman who gave her the tool was Thora of Reedbank. Thora had grown up hearing how Frigg, the goddess of the hearth, dug the first children of the fjords from cold clay and warmed them. In her household that story carried an obligation. If someone came in cold, you made room at the fire. Thora washed the grave clay from her friend’s hands. When asked before witnesses whether she meant to give shelter to every dead soul who crossed the lake, she answered that she had given a bed to a boatwright she had known for thirty years. A stranger asking at the same door would have got the same bed. Through the open gable the witnesses could hear Aldis fitting a plank.

It was Aldis's daughter who spoke for Cairn Ness. Rann had kept her mother company through the last bad nights. She had washed her, dressed her, and taken leave of her by name. These were holy offices in Cairn Ness. The dead went as guests to Hel, and their kin entrusted them to her keeping. To invite them to live under another roof seemed to Rann as dreadful as entering the Hostess's hall and calling her guests away from table. Worse, she knew men who would gladly pay a visit to the net hall if they thought a debt could follow a woman through the grave. Her mother had already given those men the strength of both her hands once.

In autumn, when the fishers hauled their boats ashore, Rann crossed the lake with enough silver to repay her mother’s customer. She laid it on the unfinished seat of the boat. With the debt paid, she hoped her mother could rest. She asked Thora to close the hall to the Returned before winter. It was Aldis who put down her adze and answered. Rann could settle with the customer. She could never release her mother from a promise Aldis had chosen to make to Hel. She asked her daughter to take the silver home.

All winter Aldis’s unfinished boat lay on the wooden blocks that kept its hull off the frozen ground. Reedbank kept the net hall open. Cairn Ness maintained the landing lamps, as it had always done, so that anyone out on the lake could find the western shore. For one more season people who would scarcely speak across a table still lit the way home for one another.

### The oaths of the two shores

In the spring a man named Vadi came down to Urdvatn. His brothers had buried him the autumn before at Cairn Ness. He told them he had promised Hel to find his foster-son, sold into service far from home. They offered to fetch the boy themselves. Vadi thanked them and asked which way the road ran. After he had gone, his brothers stood before the western assembly and asked whether the burial promises made at Cairn Ness meant anything if Reedbank could send a boat to fetch their brother away. The assembly forbade the passage of the Returned from the Ness landing. Reedbank sent a boat for Vadi.

Long before there was a quarrel about the dead, an avalanche had buried a party of miners on the high approaches to the lake. They were Deepforged. Their families had come up from homes deep inside the mountains to look for them. For eleven days the men and women of Cairn Ness had dug beside the miners. They had brought out the last three bodies after even the miners' kin had given up hope of finding them. At their burial the head of the miners’ settlement had laid his palm on the stone over the burial-house door. “While one stone stands upon another here, call us, and we shall stand beside the hands that laid our children down.” Now the Ness sent the miners a chip from that stone to call in the promise. The master's granddaughter came down with forty of her household. She had a brother's son among the Returned at Reedbank. Before she took her place on the landing she sent across the lake to tell him herself why she had come.

Reedbank too had a promise it could call. In a year of summer floods its fishers had taken the boats from their own beach and hauled them overland to the upper reaches of the Ormá, the river that carried the inland harvest to the southern sea. There the Ormsbani, the people who traced their descent to a river dragon, kept the crossings. Above each ford stood a timber hall where a traveler could find a guide and hear the keeper give his word that he would see them safely over. When the water reached the roof beams of one such hall, the fishers took off everyone who had gathered there, down to the cattle and the old blind hound that slept beside the door. The ford-keeper had stood dripping before witnesses and sworn by Tyr, the god of oaths, “When you put out for one of yours, an oar of ours shall go with you.” The fishers had counted Vadi their guest from the hour he asked them for passage. The new keeper of the ford might have disputed that point. Instead he took down his father's oar. Three crews went with him.

Kinship brought people to both landings. Among the human families of the fjords, who called themselves Hearthborn, many believed with Thora that Frigg required a host to take in anyone in need. They called that protection guest-right. Other Hearthborn, including Rann's own cousins, had carried their parents to the Ness for burial. They took their places with the Deepforged. Among the Barrowbound, Vadi wanted passage and a day's provisions. Others remembered enough of their own burials to fear what an armed boat at the steps would do to the people who had loved them. Two of the Returned crossed ahead of the crews to ask Rann for a hearing. She heard them in her house. Outside, the landing filled.

### The first arrow

On a dark evening the way into Cairn Ness could be judged by two lamps. Bring the upper light into line with the lower and there was deep water almost to the steps. Leif had charge of them. In a high wind he used to row out with his elder brothers so he could see for himself whether the wicks were throwing enough light. The people who fished the farther shore knew the boy well enough to shout a complaint across the water if one lamp burned low.

It was daylight when the boats came for Vadi. The first boat ran aground, and he had gone knee-deep to meet it when a woman from the mining hold caught hold of his sleeve. The keeper of the ford leaned over the gunwale and took his wrist. Everyone close enough to see whose hand had hold of whom was looking down. An arrow struck Leif on the steps. In all the years afterward, when people on either shore could show their children the very place the archer must have stood, nobody could tell Rann who had shot him.

She had him carried to her bed on the wicker frame the fishers used for their catch. His brothers came in wet to the waist. One had gone into the lake after the arrow, thinking he might know it by the feathers. Toward evening Leif tried twice to get out of bed. Rann understood him the second time. She sent his eldest brother out to fill the lamps, and when the boy could see the upper light through her window he lay quiet. His brother was still out on the steps when he died.

The brothers had grown up at those oars. They knew a boat by a crooked plank or the way she sat in the water, and they knew which shed now held the boat that had come for Vadi. On the third night they rowed straight to it. By the time the roof caught, a young man inside had burned his hands trying to lift the door. He had brought his father's fishing gear on the journey from the Ormá and had stayed behind to watch it. His people found the iron hooks where he had laid them in a row beside his bed.

That summer his kinsmen took the Ness boats as payment for his death. At haymaking the fishers went to get them back. They brought cousins from well up in the hills, men who came because a family without a boat on Urdvatn had little chance of eating through the winter. Several of those cousins were buried by the lake. In their own villages there had never been a Barrowbound. What their widows remembered of Reedbank was the journey there to claim a body.

### The war beyond Urdvatn

In autumn the river Ormá usually ran low enough for the grain carts to cross. A keeper who knew the gravel bottom walked beside the leading ox. His younger brother took the far bank, where the beasts had to pull hardest and a wheel could sink to the hub. Between them the brothers had got the harvest across for years. When the western king demanded the men who had taken the fishing boats, it was to this crossing that his messenger came. The keeper gave his answer where anyone waiting with a cart could hear. He would bring the accused to Urdvatn and stand beside them while the fishers spoke. Would the king give his word that they might come home afterward?

The men who came back wore the king's colors and carried provisions to stay the winter. That evening they claimed the crossing in his name. The keeper's brother stood in the doorway of the timber hall and a guard struck him down there. His body lay half inside the house. This detail survives in every telling along the Ormá, for the neighboring halls had given their word to come to one another's doors if ever armed men tried to enter by force. Each owed nine defenders. When people on the river spoke of their sister halls, it was this promise of help that made them sisters.

Word went downriver with the empty grain carts. At each hall nine people took leave of their families knowing precisely how far their oath required them to go. They drove the guards from the crossing. Then came the morning when the oxen were yoked again, and the keeper had to walk down to the far bank to do his dead brother's work. The nine from the nearest hall stayed to help him. At supper they asked who would answer to the family for the killing. By the week's end men who had been free to go home were following the retreating guards into the western king's country to exact the price themselves.

A king could scarcely tell the farms now burning along his own border that his guard had begun the trouble and the matter belonged to two brothers at a ford. He summoned every household that held land of him. Across the Shield, messengers were already climbing to the courts of kings who had sworn to defend the river settlements. By spring a farmer days from either riverbank could lose the strip his father had plowed if he failed to answer the summons. He went to his lord's gate with seed still in the sack at home.

One family in the mountains kept a count of the war in visits. Two cousins earned their living guiding pack animals over the Shield. In summer their routes met at a shelf of grass where the beasts could graze and the cousins could sit with their backs to the same warm rock. Both were Cragbound, descendants of the giants who had first found a way through those heights. One carried grain west because the price his grandfather had agreed to pay for grazing in the valleys was pack service in time of war. The other had carried a wounded western captain down off the mountain. His people had gone to war against the eastern soldiers who broke a guide's arm for giving the captain shelter. At their first meeting the cousins discovered, with some relief, that both were feeding the same army. The next year the eastern king took the grazing lands. The grain went east. The cousins still met for their meal. In the third summer a stranger came up with the pack string. It is from the cousin who waited on the rock that the family reckoned how long the war had lasted.

That year the river Askfoss watered the forest clearings of the Greenweald as it always had. The weather was excellent. Along the river, whole fields went unsown. The Tonttu who farmed the clearings there had taken children into their homes from the river country. By the end of winter the children were eating bread made with barley put aside for sowing. Their hosts hardly grudged it. There was an old friendship between the woodland villages and the Ashmarked families who hunted the open country, and presently the hunters came in pulling sledges heavy with meat. There would be barley enough left to sow after all. Then a company marching to the ford took the grain. Seeing the laden sledges in the yards, the soldiers took those too. A year earlier it would have puzzled the children to see a hunter bringing a spear to stand watch over a woman planting barley. By the following spring they knew enough to bring both of them supper in the field.

Some of those children reached the coast, where the Veilfolk built their houses into the slopes of the hills. A fugitive could ask a night of peace under one of those roofs, even with an enemy sitting at the fire. The host was obliged to sit up between them if that was what it took to get them both through to morning. One lord from the fjords, a Hearthborn man pursuing three fugitives, grew tired of waiting outside such a house. He gave the order to pull the roof down. People in the neighboring halls heard the timbers go. Neighbors came running to lift the timbers. They dug a child out together. Years afterward families along that coast could still name the child and tell you which of their own people had been there.

Farther east, the crews who went to sea had always blessed the Lumenkin for their headland fires. The families who kept them honored Hallinskidi, the god who had once held a passage open for their ancestors. They burned good oil on the darkest nights for sailors whose faces they would never see. Then a fleet came in by those lights and carried away a harbor's store of winter fish. The fishers knew what putting out a beacon might cost a crew coming home late. Some of their own people were still at sea. They went out to put the fires out all the same, and the watch families met them on the headland. By then there were empty berths on boats as far west as the Singing Islands. A king who needed a crew to move his grain could take the grown children of an island household as readily as he took its catch.

One of the last places a man could lay down his arms was in the volcanic north. There warm springs steamed in the open air, and the Cinderbound families who lived below them would undertake to interpret between enemies. For two seasons their interpreters traveled south and came back with the terms on which this prisoner or that captive crew might come home. Then armed men followed an envoy into the bathing water. The households sworn to keep the peace there had to fight in it. Afterward, when they opened the purses of the dead to learn where to send word, they found salt sewn into the lining in the fashion of the southern coast. Somewhere on that coast there were people still looking for a sail.

On the plateau east of the mountains there is a stretch of open country called the Bone-Fields. Among the great ribs of creatures older than the kingdoms lie the bones of giants who marched there in the First War. The songs of that muster name the oath that brought each company onto the field. For some companies the singer gives only the outward journey. No one came home to tell the rest.

### The bread of Vargeld

While the kings were fighting over the Ormá, a man named Vargeld kept a mill in the foothills of the Shield. When the king called his elder sons away to fight he promised to keep a loaf for each of them. Through one harvest and a second he set two good loaves on the sill every baking day. The boys never came home. By then there were so many children at the mill door that he would take the loaves down while they were still warm and break them up. In villages that afterward tore his likeness from their walls, old people could remember the particular pleasure of getting a piece of the heel with his thumbprint baked into it.

His youngest son came home from the millpond one morning with a goblin girl at his heels. She had come up through the old workings under the mountain. Her people had meal enough for three days. One king had shut the road by which they traded and the other had taken the mules. Vargeld went below with what he could spare. In return the goblin households took him through galleries that passed clear beneath the contested ford. Before the year was out he was feeding villages on both sides of the river. When a captain came to seize his wagons, the villagers themselves drove him away. There had been kings in the valley for generations who had never been loved as well as Vargeld was that summer.

In the deeps he found a chamber where a promise spoken into the rock came back in the voice of the person who had received it. The miners kept away from that place. Vargeld went there to hear his boys. Night after night he stood with his forehead against the stone and promised them their bread. One night, after the two beloved voices had answered, he heard a third. It was the captain who had taken them, begging him for pardon. When Vargeld visited the captain in his house next morning the man was still on his knees. While Vargeld listened below the mountain, the captain had heard those same boys calling for their father in his own house.

Once Vargeld understood that he could make someone hear the dead and feel his grief, he began using the chamber to punish people. The first man he brought to his knees had sold flour meant for a starving village. Few begrudged him his punishment. When the wagons rolled again, each driver swore at the mill door to deliver the sacks entrusted to him. In time it was enough to question an order for Vargeld to send the dead boys into a driver's dreams. The neighbors learned to hear a man crying out in the night and go on eating the bread he had brought them. His youngest son left the mill during those years. Nobody dared ask his father where he had gone.

It was then that the deep gates opened. Goblin captains came up out of the workings with their whole companies. Orcs who had taken service underground through the hungry years came with them, bearing the ridged skin of the Ashmarked hunters who were their kin. Behind them came pale hounds bred below the reach of daylight. Vargeld gave his captains the storehouses of the lords who had starved them. Presently he gave them the villages as well. At one granary a captain made the mothers kneel and swear away their eldest children before he would unlock the door. People who had marched out years before to settle the rights of the Returned came home to find their own children gone. The name they gave the miller was **Vargeld Hollowking**. By the time it was safe to say it aloud in the valley, his hounds were drinking at Urdvatn.

### What the living could release

At Reedbank there was ground grain enough for perhaps a week. Across the lake the burial headland had fallen, and the people of Cairn Ness were coming down to the water with bundles balanced on their heads. Rann stood on the lowest step with the key of the burial house in her hand. Thora put out for her. Beside Thora sat the keeper of the Ormá ford, pulling on his father's oar. By dusk the keeper had ferried across three of the guards who had been in his hall the evening his brother died. He knew one by the coat his brother's wife had described to him.

After supper he took the coat's owner to meet the widow. What they said in that room belonged to the two of them. In the morning she came down to the boats and asked the nine defenders from each of the river halls to gather where she could see them. “You came when we called,” she said. “I have had the strength of your arms for my husband's sake. I give it back to you. My little girl has begun to think nine men must die before she may speak of her father. I should like her to know he could sing.”

Others had to do for their own houses what she had done for hers. Some were a long time consenting. At the water's edge Rann returned the chip of stone that had called the Deepforged miners to defend her shore. She asked them now to stand beside her while she buried the dead of both landings. Reedbank’s fishers told the river crews that they had done everything their fathers had promised. The oar could go home. No one owed Reedbank another fight. The captains heard each release in the presence of the people who had heard the summons. Claims for cattle, boats and burnt houses would be heard when the families could go home. The kings agreed to send their prisoners to the lake for exchange. The first to arrive were two Ness fishers so long presumed dead that Rann had tended their graves herself.

### The last turning of the mill

The captains who set out together from Urdvatn had waited nearly too long. Vargeld held the crossings and the grain. His officers had begun taking children underground as hostages. Any household that resisted him might never see its children again. Among those sent to bring the next company of children through the galleries was the goblin woman who had once followed a boy home to his father's mill. Her own son was among the hostages. She led the children instead to a deep hold whose masons she had known in the years of honest trade. From there word went to the lake. For the first time the people fighting Vargeld knew where their children were and which road could bring them out alive.

At the harvest ford the western king sent his guards into the water beside the Ormsbani whose hall they had once occupied. They held Vargeld's captains there through a day and a night, while the miners and the goblin guides led family after family out beneath the mill. The last guide to emerge heard the wheel still turning. She went back for the miller. In the chamber below she found Vargeld listening to the stone. She told him she remembered the bread he had brought her mother. For a moment he knew her. Then from the rock came the voices of two boys asking their father to stay.

The river broke into the workings before she could bring him out. Above ground the wheel stopped with one bucket still spilling water into the next. His captains felt his hold go out of them. Some surrendered and gave up their keys. Others fled underground. The allies pulled down the mill and broke open the granaries. The deep chamber lay under black water, and those who had heard Vargeld in their sleep slept through the night. It seemed reasonable to believe him dead.

### The second door at Reedbank

When the people of Cairn Ness could go home, Rann asked her mother to row her over. Aldis took her in the boat she had finished for the man who paid her before she died. He lent it readily. Everybody on the lake knew who had fitted her planks. At the western landing Rann climbed out and held the boat’s line until her mother was steady on the steps. It was the first time since the burial that she had put out a hand to help her ashore.

The settlement at Reedbank endured. Its people could keep a house, earn a wage and put their names to what they owed on the same terms as anyone else at the lake. A creditor who came with a claim from before someone's burial had to ask the Returned in person whether they would take it up. A family could invite its dead home. The choice to cross the water belonged to the one who had come back. Several stayed on the eastern shore, where they had made friends and found work. Reedbank kept the little garden, and left the second door in the gable so that someone coming to ask for shelter could step inside without waking the whole house. Of what passed between Aldis and Hel after the boat was finished, even Rann would give no account.

The name that endured was the **War of the Dead**. In the oldest lakeside verses the dead are Aldis and Vadi and the others who had come asking a place among their kin. On the coast the lament of that name was sung as boats came in after a levy. A singer stood on the quay and called each missing sailor by name so that the family should hear it from a living voice. Long after the kingdoms had laid down their arms, a mother on the Weeping Coast could know every word of that lament and have to ask where Urdvatn lay.

## The Years Between the Wars

### A place by the spring

One evening after the War of the Dead, Yrma found a boy shivering on the steps beside the flame spring. His mother was trying to wring the water out of his coat while he was still wearing it. Yrma kept the Ember that rose from a cleft there. She knew which paving stones were warm enough to dry wool, and she could see an empty bench right beside them. She asked the mother why she hadn't taken it.

The mother had already asked. The carpenter who owned the bench wanted a penny. The town had ordered benches when families displaced by the war began arriving in the fjords. He had cut the oak, built the benches and carried them up the hill. Nobody had paid him. He had taken to renting the seats, and there was hardly a patch of warm ground left between them.

Yrma called the carpenter over. He helped her shift one of the benches back a few feet. The mother took off the boy's coat, spread it on the warm stone and sat with him wrapped in her own. At the next town meeting Yrma described what she had found. The carpenter told the households what they owed him. They agreed to pay him out of the town chest and to keep the seats free.

Until then Yrma's work had been to look after the Ember, clear fallen stone from the cleft and keep people from getting burned. Now the town also put her in charge of access to the spring. If someone tried to collect a fee or reserve the warm paving for a wealthy household, she could ask the watch to move them along. Other spring towns adopted the same rule. A Keeper could clear the steps even when the people blocking them were the ruler's own servants.

### Who kept the roadside fires

In the hills inland from the fjords, a woman named Sela cared for one of the small Embers that burned in roadside shrines. Her neighbors brought her food. She kept the roof tight, the windscreen in good repair and a dry place ready for anyone caught on the road at night. Some stretches of the old road had already disappeared under grass. Sela could tell a visitor how to get to the next farm. Who had built the shrine, or where the road originally went, was as much a mystery to her as it is to travelers today.

The summer Sela broke her hip, rain ran under the shrine door and pooled around the Ember. The farmers who came to help knew there was a drain somewhere under the paving. None could find it. In the end they carried Sela up the hill on a door. From where she lay she could see a tuft of grass growing against the wall. She had them lift the stone beside it, and the water ran out.

At harvest the farms agreed to support a second person at the shrine. Sela could teach her replacement and have a night at home when she wanted one. Along the busier roads other settlements made arrangements of their own. Some left a shrine in the care of one family. Others took turns feeding a Tender and an apprentice. A Keeper tended the Ember at a flame spring in town. A Tender lived near a roadside shrine and cared for the smaller Ember there. Each answered to the people who had undertaken to keep that particular fire going.

A traveler who left a weapon at the shrine door could stay there in peace until morning. If two men arrived with a grievance between them, the Tender could ask their neighbors to come and hear it when daylight returned. Until then both men were entitled to dry their clothes and sleep. Tenders who worshipped Frigg understood this as part of the hospitality owed to the goddess of the hearth. A traveler could follow another god and still expect a place by the fire.

### How a city came to hold the keys

The farms supplied ground barley for travelers as well as food for the Tender. After a bad harvest, the nearest spring city sometimes supplied a shrine until the next crop came in. The Tender lent the city's carter the key to the grain store so he could unload even if she was out helping someone on the road. When local deliveries resumed, he gave it back. For years the arrangement worked. It allowed a farm to save enough barley to sow in spring and kept a hungry traveler from becoming one more mouth that family had to feed.

Beneath the Shield, Vargeld had survived the flood that destroyed his mill. By the time the children rescued from his galleries had grandchildren of their own, his captains once again commanded an army underground. He wanted the mountain roads clear for their return. The grain wagons gave him a way to draw the towns' defenders down to the coast.

His agents bought up barley in the hills before the shrine carters arrived. Elsewhere they paid farmers to sign for deliveries they had never made. Each shortage sent another Tender to the fjords asking for help. Before long the coastal city was feeding most of the inland road. Families on the quays were eating thin porridge while sacks of good barley went past their doors. Their ruler, Lady Inga, owed them an account of where it had gone.

A grain merchant in Vargeld's pay proposed that the city retain the store keys until the inland settlements repaid it. Inga agreed. She also wanted a record of who was drawing on the food her city supplied. The order she sent put the city guards in charge of the shrine doors. Until then, the city had controlled who ate from its grain store. Now its guards could decide who got near the fire. Her Keeper read the order and asked what a guard should do about someone who had brought supper and needed only a dry place to sit. Inga believed the guards could settle such matters for themselves. She sent the order as written.

### The potter at the door

At Sela's old shrine a potter and his daughter arrived in the rain. They had eaten before they set out. The guard asked to see the stamped token the city issued to people who had contributed supplies. The potter had roofed half the shrine with tiles from his own kiln. He showed the guard the maker's mark on the lowest row, well within reach of the door. He had never been given a token for the work.

While her father argued, the girl went round the back and called for her uncle. He was the Tender. He let them in, found his niece a dry blanket and went back to clearing the wet paving by the door. The next morning the guard arrested him for admitting travelers without permission.

The inland town whose farmers had long supported the shrine sent an envoy to demand a public hearing. Inga's guards had orders to take the Tender to the city for trial. The envoy had just unbuckled his sword to enter the shrine when one of Vargeld's men stepped out from among the grain wagons and killed him. A carter recognized the killer. It was his own son. He set off for the coast to tell Inga what he had seen.

The news outran him. In the hills people heard that an envoy who had laid down his sword had been killed in front of city guards. The inland town sent armed households to take back the shrines. Inga heard that her guards were being driven off a road her people had fed all winter. She sent ships to seize the inland towns' grain at the river mouths. By the time the carter reached her, the mountain towns had already sent their defenders downriver to recover the harvest. Vargeld brought his companies up through the passes behind them. The Second War had begun.

## The Second War

### The army beneath the Shield

For three generations the people of Urdvatn buried their dead in peace. Boys who had seen the mill fall grew old telling their grandchildren about the day Vargeld drowned. Under the mountains the Hollowking lived to hear the story from the grandchildren themselves. His agents came home by the same passes as honest carters. They brought him the names of the houses that had helped pull down his mill, the strength of the kingdoms those houses now served, and news of each fresh quarrel over a road or a spring.

For all those years he had been feeding an army. In the deep country whole goblin towns worked his forges. Orc warlords who swore to bring him their sons were given land enough for their herds in the sheltered valleys beyond the high passes. Pale hounds bred in the workings grew so numerous that the army took its own flocks when it marched. Some families below had served him willingly since the days when he brought them bread. Others had lived and died in his service without once being permitted to see the sky. By the time Lady Inga seized the grain ships, he could put forty thousand fighters into the field. The kings who had emptied their mountain garrisons to quarrel at the river mouths had never seen so great an army gathered in one place.

At the harvest ford on the Ormá the river lords drew up eight thousand to meet him. They expected the enemy to come down the high road, where their fathers had fought. At first light they could see those companies plainly. Then the farmers on the far bank began running toward the river. A second army had come out of the deep workings behind their villages. By midday Vargeld held both banks. The sister halls whose nine defenders had once answered for a single doorway had sent nearly every adult who could bear arms. Fewer than three thousand came home. For a generation afterward a person could walk the length of the upper river and be told at every landing where someone had last seen a father or a wife.

Vargeld went to Reedbank himself. He had the old net hall pulled down and made his camp on the bare ground. He ordered the names cut from the burial stones at Cairn Ness. The workers left the backs of the stones untouched. When their children came home after the war, their mothers could still show them whose grave was whose by a long crack or the hollow left by a mason's thumb. Elsewhere his captains had broader orders. They took the spring cities intact wherever they could and allowed warmth to families that sent workers to the army. In the towns that resisted, they burned the roofs and carried off the winter stores.

By the second summer he held the roads on both sides of Midgard's Shield. Whole districts of the Greenweald were stripped to build wagons and siege works. The deeper forest survived, and for years its people ate from gardens planted by refugees in those clearings. On the coast, enemy columns reached the salt houses while the herring still hung in the smoke. Out on the Singing Islands, beyond the reach of his infantry, the grain stopped coming. The islanders ate their seed barley and took their fishing boats to war. In the volcanic north the warm pools filled with wounded men and women brought over the ridges on doors. There were children on those terraces who remembered the war chiefly as the years they had to go down to the washhouse to see their mothers, because the healers scarcely came home.

### The oath at Ormsund

The rulers who still had armies met at Ormsund, where the Ormá reaches the southern sea. Their ships filled the harbor. Inland delegates came with mud to the waist from walking around occupied towns. Lady Inga had the seals broken on the grain she had seized and gave it back to its owners in sight of them all. She also put her ships, including the vessel that had brought her there, at the disposal of the river crews. An inland widow asked when her two captured sons would be released. Inga sent for them during the hearing. The assembly waited until the three of them were together before it took up the matter of command.

The surviving Ormsbani ford-keepers brought an oar worn flat on one side. One of their ancestors had promised the fishers of Reedbank that an oar of his house would go with them whenever they put out to recover one of their own. Now Reedbank lay in enemy hands. Its surviving families asked for their town back. The river halls renewed the promise. A Lumenkin captain from the east asked who would keep it for her rowers if they died so far from home. It was a woman from Reedbank who answered. Give her their names and a description of their boats, she said, and she would go on looking as long as there was anyone left to ask.

The oath the assembly finally swore bound every company to stay in the field until the last occupied homeland was free. The allied granaries would feed a soldier's household while that soldier served. Each contingent was to keep a roll of its missing and share it at every exchange of prisoners. The kings had to surrender their right to call their forces home when their own walls were safe. For three days one of the western kings refused. His daughter commanded what remained of his cavalry. Her husband and children were already safe on the coast. When she stood before her father's company and asked who would ride on with her to free the inland towns, nearly every rider stood. On the fourth morning her father swore.

All ten peoples sent companies. The Returned from Urdvatn served under their own elected captains, with the same share of food for the kin they left behind. Three generations after their grandparents fought over giving the Returned a bed, delegates from those same shores helped choose a Barrowbound commander for the allied army. Her name was Asa. In her first life she had commanded barges on the Ormá. Since her return she had spent thirty years finding people sold or carried away during the wars of the river lords. There were captains from both shores who had been children in the bottom of her boat.

### The road to Titan's Stair

It took two campaigning seasons to reach the mountains. Asa brought the coastal armies up the Ormá while a second host came down from the north. The Tonttu opened their woodland settlements to the wounded, and Ashmarked families drove their breeding herds into the forest to feed them. At the western approaches, Cragbound guides led Deepforged companies home to holds from which their families had been driven. Several found their old doors standing open. They could have been underground with their children by evening. They left guards at the entrances and marched on, as they had sworn at Ormsund.

Vargeld recalled his armies to Titan's Stair. The giants had cut the pass through the Shield in steps broad enough for a company to stand on. West of the crest lay the shelters of Stairwatch. Eastward the mountain opened onto the high plateau. Beneath the Stair ran the galleries through which Vargeld had brought up his first armies. As long as he held that crossing he could withdraw below, feed his surviving hosts, and invade again when the kings went home. Asa called in the island crews and the last companies defending the northern springs. By autumn nearly thirty thousand of the alliance were gathered on the two approaches. Across the crest the Hollowking had drawn up the greater part of his remaining army. A man at Stairwatch could hear their multitude coughing in the cold before he could see a single face.

### The battle of Titan's Stair

The first day belonged to Vargeld. His companies came down the great steps shoulder to shoulder and drove the western host back through Stairwatch. The shelters changed hands twice. By dark the roof was gone from the broth house and the dead lay deep enough across its door that the wounded had to be carried out through a window. To the east the army of the plateau had been fighting uphill since morning. From the highest step it had taken, its captain could see the smoke at Stairwatch. Between the two hosts the Hollowking still held the crest.

That night Asa walked among the island crews. They had drawn their boats up at Ormsund to carry food and wounded for the army. Now they were the last large body of fresh fighters on the western side. Their captain asked her one thing. If they went up in the morning, who would carry down the wounded? The answer came from the Veilfolk families in the baggage camp. They had brought wool blankets and the long poles of their shelters. They spent the night making litters. The Cinderbound healers sent word from the eastern slope that they would receive anyone who could be brought over the crest. Neither promise depended on which side the wounded had fought for.

On the second morning the island companies went up through Stairwatch beside the Hearthborn called up from the fjord settlements. On the east the Ormsbani and the Ashmarked climbed together. Some of the orcs facing them bore the names of families still living along the Greenweald. A few came over when they heard their kin calling. Most kept faith with the captains beside whom they had grown up. By afternoon the armies had drawn so close that people fighting on the two faces of the mountain could hear their own language spoken beyond the crest.

Vargeld opened the deep gates and called up his last reserves. The Deepforged waiting below the west gate recognized the sound of its counterweight. Their parents had worked those gates before he took them. They went in with Cragbound volunteers to keep the passage open for the captive laborers coming out. Then they brought down the worn vault behind the last of them. The gate and the companies still coming up were buried together. Of the volunteers who went below, eleven came out. The families of the deep holds had saved places at their tables for many who would never see those homes again.

Above them the eastern army gained the crest. Asa led the western companies up the last flight herself. The Hollowking stood there among his household guard. Those who came within reach of his voice heard the dead calling them away. Some dropped where they stood. Asa heard the names of children she had failed to bring home. The woman beside her, an island rower Asa had once carried out of captivity, took her hand and went on up with her. Behind them the western ranks kept coming. Across the top step the two allied armies met.

The last of Vargeld's guard fell around him. An Ormsbani spear brought him to his knees, and blows from both hosts killed him on the open stone. No house could afterward claim the deed for its own. This time thousands saw him fall. The companies cut off on the plateau surrendered or were slain. Those nearest the surviving eastern galleries fled below. Through the following winter the alliance kept watch at the mouths, freed the occupied spring towns, and drove the last armed bands back into the deep places. When the final prisoners came up, Asa sent word to Ormsund that the armies could go home.

### What came home

More than eight thousand of the alliance died at the Stair or of their wounds that autumn. For weeks the people who had made litters went up again with wool and cord to bring down the bodies. At Ormsund a woman from Reedbank sat with the company rolls as she had promised. Beside some names she could give a place of burial. Beside others she could put only the name of the person who had seen them last. She made a second copy for the eastern coast. The islanders received theirs by boat.

Some old kingdoms still had heirs and sound walls. Others had lost their ruling houses, their harvests, and most of the people who had known where one holding ended and the next began. Spring cities opened whole quarters to families whose villages had burned. A king who wanted rents again often had to borrow seed from the very households he meant to tax. In several valleys the first authority to come home was the company that had sworn to feed its soldiers' families. It still held the grain, and it knew the names of the widows.

The hardest promise to keep was the one about food. When the armies disbanded, there were thousands of households with a claim on grain and scarcely an ox left on the upper Ormá. At the first sowing the island crews returned with fish to feed the work parties. River folk plowed land for families whose dead had carried their children off the Stair. Tonttu growers brought seed from the forest clearings. Some meant to stay one season and ended by marrying there. Their children could visit kin in three old kingdoms without leaving a road their parents had helped reopen.

At Reedbank the survivors laid out the net hall on its old foundations. From Cairn Ness came a boatload of roof timber. The first completed room housed the lists of people still missing from the eastern armies while copies went out to distant households. Only when the rolls had been sent on did the fishers take the nets back inside. A stranger who came to inquire about a name could still ask for a night's lodging there. In the end they needed both doors again.

## The Age of High Kings

### The kingdoms of the peace

The first lands to prosper after the Second War were those along the Ormá. The river joined the grain country to the sea, and much of its good soil could be worked again as soon as the survivors found seed and beasts to draw a plow. The kings who came back to those valleys found people farming on both banks whose parents had fought at Titan's Stair. Some had come down from the mountains. Others had come inland with the relief fleets. An attempt to restore the boundaries of the old ancestral kingdoms would have put half the harvest in one kingdom and the families who had sown it in another. The survivors kept their farms. The rulers who recovered their seats had to accept them as their people.

Out of the peace grew kingdoms that followed the lay of the land. Along the Ormá, families paid a share of their harvest to maintain fords, protect the passage of herds and keep grain moving in a lean year. The river lords who could undertake that work drew whole valleys under their rule. In the Splintered Fjords, harbors furnished ships to a common fleet. A strong harbor king could protect the fishing grounds and the ships on the southern run, and collect duties from trade far beyond the reach of his own oars. The lords of Midgard's Shield held the passes between them. Their stone holds sheltered the mountain peoples, and from their heights they could open or close the roads by which the inland kingdoms reached the coast. A sovereign on the Shield might command fewer people than a river king and still go into council knowing that neither of his neighbors could afford to lose his friendship.

These kingdoms owed much of their strength to the mixing of peoples after the war. A coastal king could call on the descendants of the Lumenkin mariners who had brought the eastern ships to Ormsund. In the heights, Cragbound guides and Deepforged builders kept open roads that neither people could have maintained alone. Kinship ran across the new borders. In good years that gave merchants a welcome in distant halls. When rulers fell out, there were families throughout Svellheim who owed service on one side of a pass and had children living on the other.

### The closing of the Shield

By Harald's time, possession of the grain roads had brought the kingdoms to the edge of another general war. The quarrel began when the rulers on the Shield demanded a share of the harbor duties to meet the cost of keeping the passes open. The coastal kings refused. They already bore the cost of the fleets. The mountain lords closed the principal crossings to their caravans, and the river kings sent troops to escort the next harvest through. Each ruler had an old agreement to justify what he did. Each had allies bound to come if he were attacked.

Closing the passes cut off the farmers from the people who bought their grain. The closures lasted through a winter. Wool and hides accumulated behind the mountain walls. In the ports, foreign ships left with their holds half empty. Inland, grain spoiled in barns while families on the coast went hungry. By spring the river host had gathered on the eastern approaches to Titan's Stair. A coastal army was coming up from Stairwatch in the west. The mountain kings held the crest. From all three camps men could see the burial grounds of the Second War. This time the armies climbing toward one another were the heirs of the people buried there.

Harald was then king of Hrafnborg, the great harbor of the southwestern fjords. He had led the coastal army inland. His allies expected him to claim the western gate before the river kings could take the eastern one. He reached Stairwatch first. There he received an offer from the mountain kings. They would open the western gate to his grain trains if he left them free to deal with the army in the east. For Hrafnborg alone the bargain was excellent. Its people could eat, its ships could sail, and its king could go home with his army whole.

Harald declined. He sent the offer on to the river camp with his own seal beside the seals of the mountain kings so all three armies would know precisely what had passed between them. Then he recalled his companies from the foot of the Stair. He bound himself before the assembled coastal lords to put the duties of Hrafnborg toward the upkeep of both approaches if the road were opened to every kingdom. The money had furnished his ships and paid his household men. He was offering income over which his family had held sole authority for generations.

The mountain kings opened both gates for the passage of food. The river kings sent the first grain through under the escort of mountain households and withdrew their army from the eastern approach. Harald held his host at Stairwatch until the last wagons had come down, then sent his men home to bring in the hay. The three armies dispersed. What remained to be decided was who would pay for the road the next year, and whose word would hold when another king stood at the gate.

### The settlement at Thingvellir

That summer Harald brought the rulers to the assembly ground at Thingvellir. Soldiers had got the grain through once. The farmers needed to know they could get next year’s crop through without an army. He had spent the winter among the coastal kings and knew how readily they would promise ships in a season when the ships were already at sea. He asked each ruler to declare in front of the others exactly what his kingdom could furnish and what it needed in return. The river kingdoms undertook to keep the grain landings and ferries open. The mountain crowns agreed upon a toll sufficient to maintain the roads and surrendered the right to close a pass over a private quarrel. The ports pledged a share of their duties to the inland roads and committed ships to the common defense. Men who had expected to face each other on Titan's Stair went home responsible for keeping the same road open from end to end.

Their greater difficulty was the next war. Few rulers would entrust their kingdom to the goodwill of a neighbor's heir. Few would let another king hear cases between their own people in their own hall. What they could agree to was a public hearing when a grievance crossed a border. Both peoples would send witnesses. Each ruler would answer at home for the judgment given in common. A killing could be settled by **wergild**, compensation agreed with the dead person's kin. From then on a family could pursue its claim into another kingdom without calling its armed kindred after it.

This was the beginning of the **Danelaw**, the common law of Svellheim. Every summer the kingdoms sent their rulers, lawspeakers and households to the **Alþing**, the great assembly at Thingvellir. Their local assemblies continued to hear the ordinary business of their own lands. At the great assembly even a small settlement could be heard on a matter that touched the realm. The islanders, whose ships and harvests had so often been pledged by greater powers, came to speak for themselves.

The rulers acclaimed Harald High King and entrusted him with command of the common defense. He could call the hosts together against an invader. He also had to answer before the assembly for the tolls that fed those hosts, including the duties paid into his own harbor. That settlement earned him the name **Unifier**. He had led one of three armies to the Shield. He came away able to call upon all three, and the people who lived between them could sow another year's crop in peace.

### The Silver Era

The roads and great circular forts called Trelleborgs date from the years of common rule. A grain convoy could go from the upper Ormá to the coastal markets under the same protection all the way. The forts gave travelers shelter and their escorts fresh horses. Ships sailing south could count on finding cargo for the return season. Worked silver came back into the fjords and passed inland to people who had scarcely seen it since the wars. Those years are remembered as the Silver Era. Many of the families who prospered in them had good reason to remember the order Harald made. They could measure its worth in land plowed, children raised and voyages safely completed.

In Hrafnborg the skalds still sing of the armies at the Stair. They give Harald a speech on the mountain and have the kings lift him on their shields. The account kept by the harbor gives the city's part in a few lines of figures recording what it owed for the inland roads. Harald pledged that revenue when his own people badly needed it. The king who now hears the fuel petitions in Hrafnborg knows what that promise cost, and how much harder it would have been to feed the city if the harvest had stayed on the wrong side of the mountains.

In the last summers of the Silver Era, barley sometimes stood green when the first hard frost came. The ships still sailed. A family that lost its crop could buy grain from another valley, or borrow against next year's harvest. People had lived through poor summers before. In the ports they laid in stores for the winter and made agreements for voyages in the spring.

## Fimbulwinter

### The Long Night

On the longest night of the year, the moon, Máni, turned blood-red and vanished. People call that solstice the Long Night. It marks the end of the Silver Era and the beginning of Fimbulwinter, the winter that has lasted three years. For three days people knelt at their cold hearths trying to strike a light. Nothing caught. People remember those days as the First Silence. When flame returned, glaciers advanced and the southern shipping lanes froze. The dead rose in numbers no living person recalled.

Ask someone who lived through those three days when the trouble began. One remembers kneeling for hours beside a cold hearth, nursing a bit of tinder that would once have caught on the first try. Another remembers the last summer her father's barley ripened and dates the trouble years earlier. Both can tell you what their hands were doing. Neither can tell you with certainty what befell the world.

### Three years into the deep winter

In the first year after the Long Night, livestock died and famine spread. Some halls shut out people they believed they could no longer feed. The second year brought the War of Fire. Raids for fuel reached the Greenweald, and burned forest became the Ashen Woods.

A generation earlier, a load of wood had been one of several things moving out of the Greenweald. After the War of Fire, charcoal became one of the few goods people could reliably draw from what was left. At Kolhavn, the market where the charcoal comes down to the water, the families who control the trade employ one agent to set the price. They are known as the Ashwright Kins. People who once earned a living from standing timber now have to buy the remains of those trees to heat their homes.

By the third year, snow and shortages have made large campaigns difficult. Much of the work of surviving falls to neighboring halls, harbor towns, and the few roads still in use. In Hrafnborg, the skalds still sing of Harald the Unifier. The man they sing about spends his days trying to hold the city together and get as many people through the winter as he can. At the gates, the city guards known as Chainwardens enter newcomers on the lists that determine how much fuel each household receives. Inland, charcoal camps gather at Kolhavn, and traders float bundles down blackened water. Out on the volcanic island of Eldøy the ground is still warm enough to grow vegetables. A boat from there can bring greens to a harbor where no one has seen a grain ship from the south in three years.
