# Along the Winter Roads

## The pilots of the southern sea

The river Ormá once carried grain from the farms of Svellheim to ships waiting on its southern coast. There are pilots alive who could still take one of those ships south by the stars. They know how much water to allow under the keel at the river mouth, and which headland to look for when they turn for home. It has been three years since the sea lanes froze. A child learning those stars tonight might reasonably ask why anyone bothers.

It took generations to get grain from those farms to the sea. People fought over who might cross the mountain passes, buried their dead beside the road, and made peace with families they had every reason to hate. By the time a pilot took the helm, few remembered what the voyage had cost.

## When the ground should open

On the first day of Harpa, the month when planting used to begin, some farmers still go out and turn the soil with the heel of a boot. A thumb's depth beneath the surface it is frozen hard. There are people who could sow a straight row by the feel of the seed leaving their hands. The youngest of their children have never seen a field plowed.

Three years have passed since the winter solstice when the moon vanished and every hearth went cold. Fire came back after three days. Summer has yet to return. Fimbulwinter is the name people give to this long cold. In Sólmánuður, the month of longest light, farmers once left the fields to a daughter or a neighbor to attend the great summer assembly. There they could hear their king answer for a toll, settle a debt or see a sister who had married across the mountains.

Families who used to meet there still ask after one another. A roadside shrine may be the only place where people traveling in opposite directions can stop and talk. Its Tender keeps a small Ember burning under cover. Word that a sister is safe may come home with a carter who met her there, or on the last fishing boat able to round a headland before the ice closes in.

## Supper at the smith’s

Before a smith takes a stranded traveler home, she has to reckon what she can spare. There is wood enough to cook another bowl of supper. People call that **Heat** when they speak of the things that keep a household alive. The smith also has a bar of good iron she could trade for a sack of barley. A merchant who calls that **Weight** can tell its worth by putting it in the scales. For a bed she must go next door. Her neighbor has heard her promise before, and knows she will make good the favor. That is what people mean by a household’s **Word**. That evening the traveler eats at one house and sleeps at the other.

## A place on the fuel rolls

A household promised shelter by King Harald can still spend a cold morning outside his gate. The guards who lower the harbor chain and keep watch on the road are called the Chainwardens. They also keep the fuel rolls, the lists used to divide the available wood among households. The clerk at the gate needs to know where the newcomers will sleep and how much wood to enter against that door. If five people sleep there and four go on the list, the woman who offered them her floor must make up the missing share of wood herself.

If the clerk leaves a guest off the list, she can take the matter to the Thing, Hrafnborg's public assembly. The Law-Speaker recites the city’s law and hears disputes in a public hall. There she can ask the people who heard Harald give his word to stand up and say what he promised. Sometimes getting enough wood to last the week depends on a neighbor willing to lose a day’s work to testify.

## Under the market awnings

Under the awnings of the Long-Market, a woman buying lamp oil can pay with a piece clipped from a silver arm ring. The stallholder weighs the scrap, called hacksilver, in the same pan that takes a fisher's payment in salt. If she needs the oil now and can only pay when the boats come in, both look about for a neighbor who will remember the price. Along the next row there are nails for mending boats, rope, cured fish, and winter coats with good sleeves left in them. The busiest clerk issues the slips that entitle a household to collect its allowance of fuel.

## A passage along the Weeping Coast

A clerk at **Grátholm** once met ships coming home from southern markets and assessed the duty on their cargo. The customs house still does business near the mouth of the Grátelva. These days a load may come in on someone's back. Offshore lies the **Mast-Forest**, where wreck follows wreck so closely that at low tide a salvager can walk from one deck to another. The **shipwrecks** run far out along the **Weeping Coast**. Salvagers go out on the ebb to cut free what they can, sometimes working on a ship they once helped bring into port. Enough sound timber from a single hull can roof a house. At **Brimstead**, on the chalk cliffs, worshippers of Frigg keep the lighthouse and weave the names of the drowned into a tapestry. There is work enough for the weavers long after the last timber comes ashore.

West of Grátholm the people of **Strandvik** cross rope bridges to reach the seabird colonies on the cliffs. A fisher looking up on the way out can see the shrine to Njoror, lord of the sea, standing among the gathering birds. A lighthouse still burns on **Siren’s Rock**, a pillar of rock offshore, though no one has seen its keeper in years. Some pilots have followed its light safely home. Others have had to turn hard away when they heard breakers dead ahead. At **Ormsund**, where the broad Ormá meets the sea, sailors once made room at the quays for the harvest coming downriver. The granaries stand empty now. When the lower river opens, boats can still carry news between the coast and the farms where so many harbor families have kin.

## The lighthouse weavers

On the chalk cliffs of the southern coast at Brimstead, the lighthouse keepers weave the names of drowned sailors into a tapestry that hangs in the tower. When a boat is lost, somebody has to go up to the tower and tell the weavers whom to put in it. Sometimes there are two men of the same name aboard. Sometimes the only person who knows the name of a foreign hand is the captain who drowned with him. A weaver will walk a long way to speak to a surviving crewmate before she sets that name in wool. The work belongs to Frigg, whom people here call the Keeper of Names.

A few people in Svellheim say they have met a god and can tell you what was said between them. Many more have a story about the stranger who knocked on a particular night, or the light that showed them the way off a mountain. A family may believe such an account for generations while the family next door tells it differently. The gods seldom take the field for a king or send a whole country fair weather. What one person believes a god asked of her can nevertheless keep a lighthouse burning for a hundred years.

## News from the Stair

When the allied armies defeated Vargeld Hollowking at the mountain pass called Titan’s Stair, thousands of their dead lay far from home. A list could tell a household that a son had died. It took somebody who had marched beside him to remember the song he sang so badly that his whole company finally taught him another. Such memories traveled with the skalds, the poets who carried news and the names of the dead from hall to hall. Bragi is their god. Under his protection a messenger can deliver news that will make a hall turn against him. To harm him for bringing it is a grave offense.

## The last sheaf

The stack in Freyr’s name is the grain the reapers cut and gathered before the autumn rains. In the years when the upper Ormá fed kingdoms, the last sheaf belonged to Freyr. Reapers left their weapons outside the field. A man helping a neighbor who had once borne arms against his family could spend the day beside him with some confidence that both would go home alive. The god himself once owned a sword that fought on its own and gave it away for love.

Honey poured upon a standing stone is another gift to Freyr. The stag is sacred to him. In Fimbulwinter a household that keeps a little seed through the hungry months has little enough left to offer. The day for bringing in the last sheaf still comes round on the calendar. There are villages where the old reapers meet on that day even though nobody has swung a sickle.

## The language of the quay

A child who grows up on the quay at Hrafnborg learns the local dialect, Fjordmål, from the crews unloading boats. When a crew comes in from the far coast, the same hands bargain in **Svellspraak**, the common speech used for trade, schooling and public records. Most children learn both. The word *mål* means speech. The dialects are close enough that an islander and a mountain guide can follow each other, but the first few words may tell each where the other grew up. Two Deepforged raised on opposite sides of the Shield might first speak to each other in Svellspraak.

## What the river families remember

A grandmother on the Ormá can remember which field her family cut last in the Silver Era. She can name the winter they ate the seed grain. For the Second War she has what her own grandmother gave her, and what the skalds and the records of the halls have preserved. Dates grow scarce as one goes back. At Reedbank the question that survived the centuries was whether a dead stranger might come in and sit by the fire.
