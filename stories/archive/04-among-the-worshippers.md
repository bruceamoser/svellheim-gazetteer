# Among the Worshippers

In Svellheim a god’s name may be heard over the breaking of a blade or the cutting of the last sheaf. The accounts collected here follow such observances into working houses, harbor shrines and the places where the dead are remembered.

## Grimnir, the All-Father

Grimnir and his brothers made the earth from the body of Ymir, the first giant. When Grimnir wanted wisdom he gave an eye for it. To learn the runes he hung himself upon a tree. These are signs a carver can cut into wood or stone to give a word lasting power. Those who study such inscriptions remember what their god paid to read them. A pupil who claims to have read a mark that defeated her teacher should be ready to say where she learned the reading and what it cost her. A drop of blood pressed into carved wood is a customary offering.

Kings ask Grimnir for the knowledge that might save an army. Some take considerable trouble to ask in private. He is called the Masked One, and the stories give little comfort to a ruler who thinks he can outwit him. When ravens settle on the roof of a hall where a war council is sitting, people take the birds for a sign that Grimnir is listening. An old soldier might well ask what the king has promised and who will have to pay for it.

## Thunar, the Thunderer

Thunar's temple at Hrafnborg takes people in when the weather brings roofs down. Its posts are bound with iron. On a bad night a fisher can find herself bracing the door beside a man who worked for her before his boat and house were lost. Both have the same claim on Thunar, and both are expected to get up when the next knock comes. To refuse a neighbor help during a storm is an offense against him.

A nail driven into a threshold post is a common offering. Farther along the coast at Thornwall, those called out to defend the settlements gather at his temple. Many of them learned what his protection meant years before they were old enough to carry a shield, standing barefoot in a crowded hall while someone else held the door.

## Tyr, the Hand Given

When the Aesir bound the great beasts beneath Yggdrasil, the tree whose roots reach between worlds, Tyr lost a hand. Those who come before the Thing, the public assembly, remember that the god of law bore the cost of his own undertaking. No one may swear another person's oath for them. Among the Ormsbani of the upper Ormá, a young heir spends an evening hearing the duties of the person who keeps a river crossing safe for cattle and travelers. The scale the heir wears comes from the last keeper’s armor. Handing it back at the end of the hearing means declining the office. Only the heir can say the words that bind a life to the crossing.

One offering to Tyr is a weapon broken when enemies agree to peace. A blade that has come down through a family may be worth a season's livelihood. Anyone can hear the cost of the agreement when the iron breaks. It is customary to give the people who must live with the verdict a little silence afterward.

## Hallinskidi, the Watchman

Along the eastern headlands people once gave lamp oil to Hallinskidi on nights when every local boat was safely home. There might still be a foreign crew offshore. The Lumenkin say the Watchman held open a way between worlds while the last of their people helped his brother across. He waited although the shaft of his spear was already bending. A light left burning for an unknown pilot is an old way of thanking him.

The warning horn is Hallinskidi’s sign, familiar to anyone who has heard a harbor watch call sleepers from their beds. At a city gate his worship can put a guard in an uncomfortable position. The watch must challenge a stranger, and the Watchman forbids selling safe passage. A gift of silver from a frightened traveler can disgrace a guard whose whole duty that night was to see the traveler safely in.

## Frigg, the Hearth-Mother

In the fjords the first human child came out of the shore clay wet and cold. Frigg gave her a pot of fire to hold and went back to dig for the others. Families still send a child with a covered pot of coals to the next house as winter comes in. There the child eats while the households work out who has food to spare and who has room for a guest. The girl in the old story was cold enough to need both hands for herself. She held on to the pot all the same, until the last of the children had been dug free.

Frigg keeps the names of the dead as well as the doors of the living. Bread left beside the hearth or a lock of hair woven into cloth belongs to her. The weavers at Brimstead can tell a bereaved traveler where a particular name lies in their tapestry. Sometimes that is all the traveler has come to the coast to learn.

## Forseti, the Arbiter

People who have agreed to meet an enemy in peace ask Forseti, the god of truces, to hear them. The songs call him Balder’s son and describe a court in which either party can speak without fear of bloodshed. To draw steel in a sanctuary, or to break a truce one has persuaded others to accept, offends him. When former enemies share bread in his honor, someone has to be the first to put down the knife used to cut it.

The Cinderbound of the volcanic north tell a story about the first hearing in their valley. It concerns Una, the woman who called the fire giant Surt down from his crater. Her sister had died outside a locked gate. Una wanted the hinges melted so no one could close it again. When the giant’s footsteps set the paving stones aglow, Forseti had Una repeat her request in front of him. Surt took the gate and went home. Everyone in the valley owed their life to Una being allowed to finish speaking. In the old villages a stranger whose future depended on an agreement could ask to hear it in her own tongue. The household might have to feed her for days while someone went over the ridge for an interpreter.

## Mardöll, the Lady of the Field

The field in Mardöll’s title is also a battlefield. Families whose kin have died in battle believe she receives half of those who fall. She also hears lovers' vows, and it offends her to shame someone for keeping one. A family mourning a soldier has no business turning away the person that soldier loved. In some households there is a place at the burial meal for someone who has never before been invited through the door.

Jewelry buried in the earth is given to Mardöll. A person with nothing so costly may set out cream for the cats that gather on her threshold. Her worshippers tell of gold that stays warm in a closed hand long after the hand itself has grown cold.

## Njoror, the Sea-King

The cliff shrine at Strandvik stands high enough for a departing crew to see it after their families on shore have dwindled out of sight. Sailors pray to Njoror for wind on the outward passage and a harbor free of ice when they come home. For generations a household on this coast might have much of what it owned aboard one boat. An offering of gold is cast where the water is too deep to recover it. In a town living on its last few voyages, even a small piece takes some parting with.

Crews sworn to him let an albatross feed in peace, and many a child has been sharply hushed for whistling aboard a boat. Gulls coming far inland are counted among his signs. These days a sailor who sees them over a frozen field may walk down to the harbor to see whether the ice has opened.

## Skadi, the Snow-Shoe Goddess

Skadi was born among the giants and married into the gods. Across Midgard's Shield the Cragbound claim her for kin. They say she taught the children of their foremother Runa to turn and look back on a climb so that they could find the way home to their mother. Guides taking out a search party leave frozen meat for Skadi on the heights. Her wolves may take it freely.

The Ashmarked remember the same goddess coming on skis to a hunting band dying beside a fallen ash. They know a harsher side of her worship too. Skadi is the Lady of Vengeance. A death for which the killer has never answered is what her followers mean by an unpaid blood-debt. An assembly can settle what one family owes another for a death. Someone who has sworn by Skadi may still have to go up the mountain and reckon with the promise made there.

## Idunn, Keeper of Apples

Idunn keeps the fruit that preserves the youth of the gods. Along the Askfoss the Tonttu tell a homelier part of her story. She held an apple down among the roots at a warm spring and drew up a child who had fastened his teeth in it. The last child to come out took the pips and planted them. In the old Greenweald several villages claimed the first trees, and a guest could spend a pleasant summer trying the apples from all of them.

After the woods burned, survivors carried cuttings out wrapped in wet cloth. Planting seed in ash is an offering to Idunn. Her followers spare green wood from the fire and count harm done to a child a sacrilege. In the charcoal settlements a tree kept alive behind a house sometimes bears the name of the village from which it was carried.

## Ullr, the Glory-Hunter

When Grimnir leaves the citadel of the gods, Ullr rules in his place. Hunters know him through the bow, and people called to single combat ask him to witness the terms. A ring hung on a yew branch is his customary offering. A challenger who has named his ground and weapon before witnesses will have a hard time persuading them that the god approves a change once he has seen his opponent.

Ullr's stories are especially dear to archers who can tell you what the wind was doing at the instant of a shot. An arrow that holds true against the wind may earn him a ring. The hunter still has to follow the animal, dress it and bring the meat back to camp before there is anything to boast about at supper.

## The Shield-Maiden and the Bear-Sark

Thornwall's ruling house claims descent from the **Shield-Maiden**. In the settlements that muster there her most devoted followers include people too old to answer the call to arms. They remember having to hold a doorway until the last wounded person was carried out. That is the deed for which they invoke her before their sons and daughters leave.

Soldiers facing a charge pray to the **Bear-Sark**, a mortal remembered for the fury that came over him in battle. Those who ask for his courage know how hard it can be to lay it aside afterward. There is pride in having fought beside such a companion. There can also be fear in approaching afterward. Families who honor the Bear-Sark have reason to care who will know their child well enough to bring them home once the fighting stops.

## The Law-Speaker and the Staff-Carrier

Lawspeakers learn judgments by heart so a case can be settled even after its witnesses have died. When they ask the **Law-Speaker** for help they are addressing one of their own, a mortal remembered for doing that work faithfully. An apprentice rehearsing a judgment has to remember whose land was pledged as carefully as the price. A single omitted name may leave a widow depending on the memory of the man who owes her cattle.

The **Staff-Carrier**, called the **Volva**, is honored by seers and by those who have found themselves unwelcome in their own halls. To receive a wandering prophet can cost a host some standing with the neighbors. A place is sometimes made for her by someone who remembers arriving at a door under much the same circumstances.

## Volund the Smith

Smiths honor **Volund** for the craft it takes to make a piece work as its maker intended. He is also remembered for traps and for vengeance. A patron ordering a lock and a prisoner studying one may both have good reason to speak his name. A smith who has worked for a cruel lord is likely to understand both prayers.

## The Mariner and the Horse-Lord

The **Mariner** belongs to those who sailed beyond familiar headlands and still found a way home by the stars. A pilot teaching the southern run used to have a pupil find the harbor twice, once under sail and again on a clear night by naming the stars above the entrance. The route is closed now. People old enough to have sailed it still know where to look.

The **Horse-Lord** is a companion to travelers and the animals that bear them. In a crowded road shelter it can take a determined person to leave the warm room and see to a wet horse. In the households that honor him, someone always asks who took that last watch, especially if the rider has already drunk to a safe arrival.

## Hel, Queen of the Dead

At Cairn Ness, where families on the western shore of Lake Urdvatn bury their dead, even a drowned stranger received a coin beneath the tongue. Hel, called the Hostess, would have her due, and the people who washed the body wanted the stranger received decently in her house. The older temple verses assign to Hel those judged unworthy of a place elsewhere. At this lake, however, a daughter once buried her mother with a coin and met her alive on the far shore nine days later. Families there are careful about telling a mourner what judgment Hel has given.

Among the Barrowbound are people who remember Hel addressing them by their own names. They speak of a choice between suffering in her house and returning to the living under a promise. No two of them can make that promise for each other. On what happens when it is kept, the Returned have as many questions as their kin.

## Surt, Lord of Muspelheim

In the volcanic north a red glow on the underside of a cloud can mean that the great crater called Surt's Forge is active. People watch until they know which way the ash is blowing. Surt rules Muspelheim, the realm of fire, and the old songs name him as the power whose fire will consume the world. There are those who desire such an ending and call to him. No offering to Surt is held safe.

In the spring country families tell of Una asking him for enough fire to melt a gate hinge. He came down to attend to it himself. Cinderbound interpreters remember that Forseti, the god of truces, made the giant hear Una’s request again before he went any farther. Surt could have set the whole settlement alight. He carried off the gate. A traveler who hears Surt's name at their mourning table would do well to wait for the rest of the story before deciding what gods the household serves.

## Ymir, the First Giant

The oldest claim the giants make upon Svellheim can be understood simply by looking up at the Shield. The mountains were once Ymir's bones. Grimnir and his brothers killed him and made the mortal world from his body. Cragbound mourners who call the names of their dead into a gully believe their eldest kinsman carries the sound back to them.

There is a harsher devotion to Ymir among those who would take the first cold of the world into themselves. Those who undertake it give up lighting a fire of their own. A family in the fjords might hear with respect the giant account of creation and still go in haste to a neighbor whose chimney had stood smokeless since a burial. In the third winter a person can be lost quite near home.
