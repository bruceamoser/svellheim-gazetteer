# Hearth and Homeland

After the Great Wars the peoples of the old homelands rebuilt their halls together. In the Age of Embers their descendants share streets, shrines, and assemblies, and speak to neighbors in the common tongue, Svellspraak. The older tongues can still be heard wherever a household tells these stories.

## Hearthborn

### The inner shores of the Splintered Fjords

On a fine morning a crew could push off after breakfast and reach a cousin's beach in time to help haul the nets. The oldest remembered Hearthborn villages lay along the sheltered inner shores from the narrow inlet at present-day Ketilsfjord, past the harbor where Hrafnborg now stands, to the mouth of the Saltá near Saltvik. On that stretch of coast, the cliffs broke often enough to leave a landing beach, a fresh stream, and ground above the high tide for a hall and its stores. Short voyages between coves let households share labor and supplies. When a gale shut a family in for days, hearth smoke across the inlet was sometimes the only word they had of their kin.

A village began with a few timber houses roofed with turf. Doors opened away from the water so that a gale coming up the fjord would miss them. At the head of the beach stood boat sheds and racks where nets could be hung for mending. Storehouses sat on raised stone footings uphill of the houses, with room for dried fish, grain, and spare oars. Where the slope opened into a wider shelf, families kept sheep and grew barley. From the Saltá end of the fjords came salt for curing fish. People who arrived with a load could count on finding work wherever boats had brought in a catch.

As settlements grew, several households kept a larger hall together. Crews met there to assign landing places, mend gear out of the weather, and decide what could be taken from the common stores after a run of bad fishing. A place by the fire gave a traveler shelter. If someone pledged a place in the boat crew or a share of the winter stores, the people at the hearth could later be asked to remember exactly what had been offered.

Hrafnborg grew around its flame spring on this coast. Hearthborn families remember the harbor as part of their old country. The port grew into a city shared by many peoples.

### Frigg's portion

At the beginning of the cold season, a child in the inner fjords used to be given a little pot of coals to carry to the next house. There the child ate supper while the two households reckoned what each could spare for the winter. A widow might have room under her roof for three and food for scarcely one. Her brother might have a loft full of meal and six children underfoot. By the time the pot went home there would be grain drying by her fire and a place made up there for his eldest. Frigg had trusted a child with fire, and it was considered a poor house in which the child who carried the pot was sent away when the promises were made.

To the families of the fjords, Frigg’s gift of fire carried a duty. Anyone who kept a hearth had warmth to offer the next person who came in cold. A stranger coming off the ice could claim a place to thaw by the fire before anyone asked who would feed another mouth. What the host promised after that could be heard before Forseti, the arbiter of the gods. In Hrafnborg a family that takes someone in must still answer to the ration clerk. Among the questions asked at a winter supper now is whose name shall go down for the extra measure of fuel.

## Deepforged

### Caves and mines beneath the mountains

Deep beneath Midgard's Shield, a child going from her parents' house to her grandmother's could make the whole journey by lamplight. One reach of the passage might be smooth where water had worn its way through the rock. In the next she could lay her hand over the chisel marks of relatives who had cut that stretch within living memory. These were the first Deepforged homes, among the caves and mines. Generations later their descendants came up through the mountains and made homes among the other peoples of Svellheim. A clan that built a bridge above ground still expected one of its own to take responsibility for the stonework long after the builders died. High-Hall, the sealed Deepforged observatory, is a surviving landmark of their work above ground.

### The answer from the stone

In some deep holds the first sound a newborn heard after its own crying was a rapping of knuckles on the wall. One by one the neighbors answered along the gallery, until a mother lying in the dark could tell how far the news had gone. The old people said the mountain was still full of voices that might one day be given flesh. A mason who heard knocking behind a fall was bound to answer, even when the gallery beyond belonged to a clan with which his own had quarreled.

In the underground settlements, bridge builders made their promises in Dvergsmål, the Deepforged language. They swore by Tyr, who required every person named in an oath to speak for themselves. At the dedication of a bridge the maker laid a painted hand upon the stone and named the person who had agreed to inspect it after her death. Maker and heir each spoke for themselves, as Tyr requires. Where such marks survive, a stranger who sees a crack opening can find the household that will hear him. A traveler taken below for the first time is taught the answering knock before he is led out of sight of daylight. Deepforged who build on the surface still speak of the mountain's unfinished household. When a builder dies they paint a hand beside hers on the bridge. So long as the span stands, some living hand must answer for it.

## Ashmarked

### The margins of the Greenweald

An Ashmarked hunter coming home across the open Interior could once see the tall trees of the Greenweald miles before she reached the campfires under them. Along that edge her people could hunt the open country and still bring their youngest through a winter under shelter. The War of Fire made the winter hunt harder to sustain. Some families stayed near the burned country, while others carried their traditions to the road settlements and towns that fed them.

### Those who watch the trail

When the meat came home to the Greenweald camps, an old woman or a child went out with the knife for the first cut. That portion belonged to the people who had kept the fires and watched the trail. The hunter who had carried Skadi's coal had needed someone to come home to. In Askmál, the old speech of the Ashmarked, the song used to teach children the cuts of a carcass names the people expecting each piece. Even the lean strip along the backbone has a verse.

Hunters left frozen meat for Skadi on a height and gave her wolves room at a kill. To bear fire in the blood was, in their telling, to be fit to go out where another body would fail. When a stranger stumbled into camp with news of a party missing on the open ground, the searchers took that person along to show them the last tracks. On their return the guide ate with the hunters and was owed a share for the journey home. Some families still make that division on the floor of a town kitchen. Long after they have lost their hunting grounds an elder can take up the knife and find that every child at the table knows the song.

## Cragbound

### Settlements of the high ranges

From the valley a Cragbound village could disappear altogether against the rock. It was the hay cut in summer, drying in bright strips below the skyline, that told a traveler how many households lived up there. The largest settlements stood on broad shelves below the ridges, close to reliable water and enough grass for summer grazing. Stone houses sat shoulder to shoulder behind low windbreaks. The walls were thick, the lintels high enough for a Cragbound adult to pass upright, and the roofs sloped so that sliding snow fell clear of the lanes. In steeper country, households built short steps between terraces and kept ropes at exposed turns. Stone shelters farther out along the paths gave herders and travelers somewhere to wait when they could see neither the next cairn nor the way home.

Summer was spent high on pasture and lower down cutting hay for the enclosed pens. Families dried meat, made hard cheese, and brought fuel up from below the tree line. Settlements with little growing ground traded wool, hides, and pack service for grain and timber. Storage rooms were set into the slope behind the houses. In a long storm, nearby households could share stores without crossing the most exposed ground. The larger settlements kept a common hall where families met to assign work on paths, hear claims to grazing, and agree when a crossing had become too dangerous to use.

Around Titan's Stair, families remember paths that predate the great steps. A traveler might stop at Stairwatch, just west of the pass, before continuing into the range. Its stone shelters give an idea of the outlying places that made regular travel between the old settlements possible. Some of the high paths also reached entrances to the Deepforged holds. Those mouths of the mountain offered places where Cragbound guides and Deepforged travelers could meet, exchange goods, and pass on word of conditions above or below.

### The names called across the cleft

A guide of the Shield learned every high path twice. On the outward climb her teacher would stop her and have her turn until she could pick out, far below, the rock where they had eaten or the cleft where they had left their packs. That turn on the path was the lesson Skadi gave Runa’s children when she led them away from their mother. They would need to recognize the way back if they wanted to see her again. When a guide took on a pupil from outside the range, that pupil learned the homeward view as carefully as any daughter of the house.

At a death the family spoke the names of the departed into a gully and listened as the mountain gave them back. Ymir's own bones bore those voices, and to the children of Runa the high ranges remained the part of their eldest kinsman entrusted to their care. Skadi received meat on the heights when a search party went out. Children learned early how to call for help in Jötuntunga, the language of their giant kin. Everyone who heard that call was obliged to answer. Even a traveler who knew only the cry could bring a whole household out on the rope. A guide who answered it might ask a steep price for taking the traveler farther once the weather cleared.

## Tonttu

### Settlements of the old Greenweald

A deck of split logs once led a visitor dry-shod through the wet woods of the Askfoss, the river that watered the old Greenweald. Where the walk widened enough for two laden people to pass, the first Tonttu houses were close. A village might occupy several clearings joined by footpaths and raised walks over soft ground. Houses stood on stone footings or short posts, with steep shingled roofs and shutters fitted close for winter. The wettest paths were laid with split logs that could be lifted and replaced when the river rose. Above the flood line stood a common hall, dry storehouses, and a covered place to work through the long wet months.

Spring water made year-round settlement possible at some sites. Where a warm outflow crossed gently sloping ground, households tended small beds for greens and medicinal plants. In the wider clearings they grew hardy crops, and in season they fished, hunted, and gathered berries and fungi. Dry stores held seed as well as food. Larger villages could lend a household enough grain to sow again or supply cuttings to a settlement that had lost its beds to frost. Along the outer paths, guides met traders bringing salt and iron in exchange for smoked fish, medicines, and worked wood.

Access to water and gathering grounds was settled close to home. Families met to decide where a new house could stand, whose bank could be fished after a flood, and what timber could be cut when a household needed to rebuild. Springs shared by several villages brought their neighbors together. At some of those meetings the first item of business was to walk the stream and see what the last season had done to it.

Fuel raids reached the Greenweald during the War of Fire. Where the canopy burned, watercourses filled with ash and fallen timber, and familiar paths became hard to follow. Some families moved to the woodland margins or found homes farther downriver. Those who stayed carried seed and cuttings to surviving groves. Near Askveld, accounts of a grove called the Green Heart still circulate alongside memories of the settlements that once stood throughout the wood.

### The spring and its children

The people of the Askfoss called the spirit of their village spring a relative. They believed their first children owed their lives to a spring spirit who heard their voices underground. Living beside her meant taking care of her water. One household looked to the moss that held the bank together. Another was answerable for keeping the channel clear below it. The spring spirit received a little piece of weaving when a child was born, and midsummer callers upon Idunn brought pips from fruit that had lasted through the winter. A child could grow up making both offerings and know which one to take to the spring and which to the orchard. The spirit had once made clothes for children she had never seen, and the apple trees the last child planted had kept the bank from washing away.

A visitor was given a dipper at the drinking stone. Someone would walk downstream with him before he gathered a single handful of moss. Pulling moss from the wrong bank could loosen the soil and spoil the water for every house below. When the Greenweald burned, families carried cuttings down the Askfoss wrapped in pieces of wet cloth. A child growing up among the charcoal yards might learn the name of a lost village in Tonttumál when her grandmother pointed out the tree she had carried from there.

## Lumenkin

### The early homeland

The first eastern headland a Lumenkin sailor sighted on the voyage home might still leave a day's rowing before supper in her own house. Even so, someone on that headland knew her sail. The earliest lasting villages lay in the coves below the northern ice sheet, the White Waste, and along the seaward slopes of the Frozen Steppe. The old coastal country ran south toward the mouth of the Hornvatn outlet, where Rindgate now stands. Families could work sheltered coves there and still reach the upland paths leading toward the Spire. The headlands held the signal fires that marked that journey. Most houses stood in the lee below them, where people could draw fresh water and bring a boat ashore.

The first villages grew above the reach of storm tides. Low stone walls broke the wind around courtyards, and timber-roofed houses opened inward onto shared workspaces. On the slopes above, walls held narrow terraces where families could grow barley and greens. The coves supplied fish and salt. Households dried the catch, hauled boats above the winter surf, and kept stores for the weeks when the upland paths closed. Beacon keepers watched arrivals at sea and passed word from one cove to the next.

Early records kept at the larger settlements give the days when a path opened, when the fishing boats returned, and when distant lights could be seen from a headland. Households brought those records together to agree when to sail or travel inland. Later Lumenkin scholars remember these gatherings as places where people learned to compare observations recorded under different skies and in different seasons. A headland tower might hold a beacon on its roof and several generations of weather and tide records in the rooms below.

The advance of the White Waste cut off the northern paths and left some of the old coves under ice. Other settlements endured farther south, and their descendants live among the mixed communities of the eastern coast and beyond.

### The watch on the headlands

In the days of sail the eastern coves gave Hallinskidi oil, burning it on the headlands through nights when there was no ship expected home. Crews from far ports steered by those lights. To demand silver of a captain who had seen the beacon was sacrilege. Their ancestors had reached Svellheim because a god held a passage open for strangers. Keeping a light for an unknown crew was how a coastal family could repay him. The Lumenkin counted the keeping of such shores among the reasons their people had come into Midgard.

Once a year the households on the coast kept a longer vigil. Each in turn named an absent kinsman in Ljósamál, the old language of their lost home, and those who knew him described the stars under which he might be sailing. A foreign pilot with another name for a familiar star could earn a seat among the readers of the sky. Sometimes a disagreement lasted long past dawn, and both names went into the headland book until another ship came in. Inland, families whose northern coves lie under the ice still set a lamp at the door on that night. In some houses the last name spoken is the name of the village itself.

## Veilfolk

### The hills between the Saltá and the Grátelva

Coming up from a fishing beach into Veilfolk country, a traveler might see sheep grazing on what turned out, a few steps farther on, to be the roof of the house where he hoped to spend the night. The earliest burrow-halls stood in the low coastal hills between the lower Saltá and the Grátelva, inland of present-day Thornwall, Strandvik, and Grátholm. Sheltered folds in the hills offered dry slopes above the streams, springs close enough to carry water from, and ground that could be worked from the door. Footpaths along the hills linked the old halls to fishing landings and to routes heading inland.

The first halls were cut into the side of a slope where the floor could drain toward daylight. Builders lined the walls with stone and laid earth and turf over a roof of stout timbers. On the open side stood a low, recessed door and small shutters. A stone-lined flue drew smoke up behind the hearth. Households enlarged their halls along the contour of the hill, adding rooms that could borrow light from the slope. In a larger settlement, several entrances opened onto a shared yard. One hall had room for visitors and assemblies, and each household had its own places to sleep and store food.

Families grew oats and roots on sheltered slopes and grazed sheep on the higher ground. They spread hay to dry in the yards and kept crocks of butter and root vegetables in the coldest chambers. The same paths that carried salt fish inland brought strangers to the halls. Guests who arrived wet could leave their boots by the entrance drain, dry their clothes by the fire, and share whatever supper the household had agreed to give them.

### Guests of the hill-wife

At the birth of a Veilfolk child someone stood between the lamp and the wall and held the baby up until the family saw its little shadow. A spoonful of milk was left overnight in a cup for the hill-wife. The milk recalled the shadow children she had sheltered. Until Frigg gave them bodies, the youngest could hold a cup but could never swallow a mouthful. Frigg received a length of the first yarn a child spun well enough to hold together. Weavers kept bits of it for mending a visitor's clothes. A stranger sent out warm enough to reach the next valley was said to go dressed from Frigg's loom.

The hill-wife had recognized each of her guests by the shadow it cast. Many burrow-halls accordingly gave a fugitive the right to withhold a name while accepting guest-peace, or _frith_. The host asked whether the traveler could keep the peace with everyone under the roof. An injured kinsman could require a hearing in the morning. Until then he owed the stranger the same shelter he would claim for his own child, and the host would sit up with them both if that was what a night of peace required. In the mixed coastal towns there are still inns where a person in trouble can ask for that single night. Their keepers reckon one such roof belongs wherever the hill-wife's children settle.

## Ormsbani

### The oath-halls of the upper Ormá

A drover looking down from the mountain approaches could tell where to take a herd across the river by the timber halls that stood above the shallows. The oldest remembered Ormsbani homeland lay along the upper Ormá, from the divide with the Djupá downstream to the country near the mouth of the Kolbekk. The river ran between cultivable terraces and low ridges where settlements could stand clear of the spring floods. People driving stock or carrying goods between the mountain approaches and the farms farther downriver converged on a handful of shallow crossings. The oldest halls stood above those fords.

The early oath-hall was a long timber building on a raised stone footing. The hearth ran down the middle, with room for witnesses on either side and a clear space by the door where a traveler could make a claim in sight of the assembly. Around the hall stood household yards, barns, and granaries. Landing stages and pens sat lower on the slope, close enough for a drover or ferryman to reach the water before daylight. In spring the households cleared debris from the crossing and marked the safe approach again.

Families in the valley raised stock and sowed grain on the terraces. The hall could pledge boats, fodder, or labor to a neighboring settlement and call on the households that had spoken for the promise. The duty to maintain a ford passed from one keeper to the next. If a flood shifted the channel, the people who had witnessed the new keeper’s promise could call on that person to find another safe crossing and mark it.

### The names borne at the ford

In the old oath-halls of the Ormá the name of a ford went with the duty to keep it safe. A possible successor wore a scale from the last keeper’s armor while the household recited those duties in Ormstunga, the old language of the river halls. The heir might go home that night still wearing it, or hand it back and leave the name for another. Tyr had heard Yrsa answer for children whose coming she could scarcely imagine. He had waited for her answer. Among the dragon knights it was held a grave impiety to hurry a young kinsman through those words while the whole hall looked on.

Once the heir accepted, even strangers had a claim on the new keeper’s time. A ferryman with a broken ankle could send to their door for a replacement. A traveler from another country whose packhorse had drowned could go before the assembled house and ask why the dangerous channel had been left unmarked. Even in poor years he had the right to finish speaking. The seven had drawn breath on dry land because a mortal woman had carried them there. To their heirs belonged the fords of Midgard and the burden of bringing others ashore. When the old houses scattered, some carried that charge to landings on rivers of which Yrsa had never heard.

## Barrowbound

### The early communities and Urdvatn

The first Barrowbound communities grew by receiving people who had returned in different parts of Svellheim. A Returned person might find a place again among their own kin. Others moved on when a home had been divided, a village refused them, or the promise they had made to Hel took them elsewhere. At first a community might be a single household that knew where to send someone for shelter, with a few names passed between settlements.

Reedbank, on Urdvatn at the foot of Midgard's Shield, had a net hall where fishers used to spread out their gear. Giving someone a bed there cost little enough at first. The wooden pegs that had held the nets would take a wet coat, and a guest who could mend a boat could earn a living on the lake. People who had died and come back began to seek the place out. The quarrel over their welcome is remembered in [the history of the First War](01-historical-tales.md#the-quarrel-at-urdvatn). Households settled on the eastern shore, near the old ring fortress. They built low timber houses above the landing, with a common room where newcomers could tell their stories and ask for word to be sent to their kin. Fishing, mending boats, and work for the farms and settlements around the lake supported the refuge. Some of the people who lived there were the living kin of the Returned. They helped carry messages outward and stood as witnesses when someone sought to recover a place in an old household.

The Barrowbound came from many homelands. Urdvatn became a home they made together, open to people whose first lives had begun anywhere. Some Barrowbound still speak of the lake as home. Others use the word for the farm, hall, or town that knew them before their death.

### A place among the living

At Urdvatn a newcomer was asked what name should be sent to the far shore and to whom. The name told the boat crews which household to search for. The promise, if the Returned chose to tell it, told them what help to bring. An oath to a child might call for somebody who knew where a foster family had gone in the famine. Those with no message yet to send still got a bed. The people of the refuge knew how long it could take a person to think of the living as people one might speak to again.

Some of the Returned set a coin aside for Hel and call her the Hostess. They have met her once. A woman who prayed to Frigg in her first life may go on spinning for her in the second. To her the two gods have claims as different as the grave and the house she has promised to reach. A Returned person may cross half the country because of a promise no one else heard. At Reedbank that is reason enough to offer a boat or find a guide. One of the Returned may offer to witness a hard promise, but leaves the words to the speaker. They extend the same courtesy to the living stranger who has come to the lake to claim a dead spouse, or to beg that spouse to come home. Each has a voice in what becomes of the household. Only the one who stood before Hel can answer for the bargain made there.

## Cinderbound

### The settlements below the Steam-Oasis

In the old country a Cinderbound gardener could walk down from a path crusted with frost and put a bare hand into warm soil. The first villages lay on the lower terraces south of the Steam-Oasis, where hot springs drain the volcanic ridges called the Throat of the World. The springs drew people through the ridges, and the lower terraces had sheltered ground where they could build and grow food. Families set their houses above the runoff channels, close enough to use the warm water and far enough from the vents that an ordinary change in the wind would leave the lanes clear.

The first villages had dark stone footings, low timber roofs, and covered yards where people could work out of the ash. Builders laid broad paving stones over shallow channels of warm water in the washhouses and workrooms. Beds of greens and roots grew in walled plots warmed by the outflow. The driest sheds held grain against the months when weather closed the paths. Fuel and timber came in from settlements farther south, and people in the spring country traded pots from their kilns and the use of the warm workrooms for goods they could store.

Paths from the houses led up to the springs, where the village offered every traveler a bath on one condition. Anyone carrying a feud into the valley must set it aside while using the water. When ash blocked a channel or a vent opened beside a house, neighbors helped move the people and dug a new course for the water.

### Una's hearing

At a birth below the Steam-Oasis the family used to shade the lamp and wait to see whether the child's eyes would shine like Una's. Among some families a little bronze key passed from house to house for the occasion. It was put away afterward with the names of the dead. To those families a mark meant that an ancestor had once gone barefoot over burning stones to bring somebody home. The name of the person who had been carried was kept, when it was known, just as carefully as the name of the bearer.

Una had asked Surt for fire to open a gate. Forseti made sure the giant heard exactly what she had asked before he could burn the whole valley. A Cinderbound hearing began by bringing in every person who would have to live with the agreement. Each had the right to hear the terms and answer for themselves. Even kin speaking for a sick brother had to carry his answer back. A stranger at the spring could require the hearing to be given in a tongue she understood. In winter this could mean housing her for days until someone came over the ridge who spoke it. Many Cinderbound learned the neighboring tongues young, and once the kingdoms opened their gates their interpreters went far from the volcanic north. They carried Forseti's worship with them. Surt's part in the tale was remembered too. In houses that kept a key in Una's memory, people spoke his name in mourning and took very great care what they asked of fire.
