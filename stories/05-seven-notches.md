# Seven Notches

## Chapter One · Salt Fish for Supper

Harald knew his foster brother had come by the supper laid for two. Nobody else in the king's acquaintance liked boiled salt fish enough to ask for it in the house of a man who could afford a roast. There was a puddle in the passage beneath a traveling coat. There was a clean pair of hands held out above the kitchen fire, with the left forefinger still crooked from an occasion involving two boys, a milk pail and a ram.

“Who let you in?” Harald said.

“I said I'd come for the silver you owed me.”

“Somebody should have directed you to the end of the line.”

Eirik got up. He had gone thick about the middle. There were white hairs in the red beard. Harald was struck by how nearly it had become the face of the man who used to put them both to bed. They held each other for a good while. Outside, Hrafnborg was settling down to rain. It was an evening almost precisely like the evenings the boys had spent in the house of Eirik's father, a keeper of the mountain road. Harald's own father sent him there every autumn to learn the country above the fjords. Everything in that house had smelled of drying hides. He had shared Eirik's narrow bed and never once been given an equal share of the covers.

The cook had taken the fish out of its salt early enough. Eirik always lifted the backbone with his fingers and ate the two sweet knobs of flesh from the cheeks first. Seeing him do it brought back to Harald the winter he was eleven and tried to run home. He had been at the road house a fortnight. At night he could hear wind wandering through the trees like the sound the tide made back home when there was still just time to run down and see your father come ashore. Nobody came ashore in the mountains.

He had taken a heel of bread and set off at dawn in his best calfskin shoes. He had understood that all water came down to the sea. It hadn't occurred to him a stream could go under a shelf of rock and come out some considerable distance below it. He sat on the bank above the drop, with one shoe gone and the little toes of his left foot turning the peculiar yellow of a candle in sunlight. His foster brother came around the pine above him with two pairs of socks tucked in his shirt.

“My father says he can see your arse from the house. That green cloak of yours shows for miles.”

“I want to go to the water.”

“I know. Can you show me what you mean to do when you get there? Because if it's ships you want to look at, this stream hasn't one for another three days.”

They ate the heel together. Eirik put a pair of warm socks on the yellow foot and the other pair over it. When they came into the kitchen Eirik's mother had a small pan on the coals. She had soaked fish from the fjord for supper because she thought the boy would like something familiar in this strange place. He had sat and cried over his plate with the water from four socks collecting under his stool. Eirik cracked the jaw open with a spoon and gave him the soft pieces out of the cheeks. When the old king came to fetch his boy in spring, the road keeper's wife packed him dried apples. She said she understood they could get their own fish where he was going.

Forty years later, across the table in Hrafnborg, Eirik put one of the cheeks on Harald's plate. Harald suddenly wanted very much to hear the old woman turn in her bed on the other side of a thin wall and call out to them to leave some for their father.

“How's your mother?”

“She's worn through the slippers you sent. I told her it was idle to go about complaining what bad slippers they were and wearing them every day for ten years.”

“And the boy?”

“Aron got married in the spring. There's a baby.”

Harald put the platter on the table before he had properly got hold of it and had to catch a fish with his sleeve. “I was at the boy's naming.”

“There was some objection to what you wanted to call him. He remembers you. He still has that wooden horse you made. Had to nail its legs to a board to get it to stand up.”

They spent half a jug of ale remembering what had happened the day they named the boy. Harald had suggested his own grandfather's name. All the way from the cradle there had come a sound like a goat working itself loose from a fence. Eirik had interpreted this as the child's considered opinion. Now that same boy could set a baby on his shoulder and walk the floor himself. Harald let Eirik tell him which tunes she liked. He wasn't looking forward to the moment when there would be nothing left on the platter. It was only when Eirik started cutting scraps of crust into little squares with the blade of his knife that Harald realized he was waiting for something.

“Have the roads come down?” he asked.

“Part of one. On the far side of the Stair. There's a wall holding up the bend above the drop. We had to go down on ropes last month and get a wagoner out of a beech tree. The tree caught him. It didn't catch his team.” He pushed the plate away. “I'm here for the harbor money, Hari. They sent me because they think you'll listen to me.”

So there it lay between them. The three kings in the mountains kept the roads by which the grain came out of the valleys to the sea. Every loaded ship that cleared Hrafnborg paid a duty to Harald. For years his father had said a better harbor at this end of the road did as much for the farmer at the other end as a better road, and all the kings who made their living on the salt coast agreed. He could hear his father's voice making that argument even now. The old man had had a singular gift for going on speaking after he was dead.

“Will you come down to the water with me before the kings meet?” Harald said.

“Yes. If you'll listen to me without making me get into a tree.”

By morning the rain had stopped. Gerda was on the harbor wall, dropping a stone tied to a cord down a hole where two courses of timber had come apart. She made Harald take off his cloak and lie with his ear to the planking. He could hear the suck of the water a man's length in from the sea.

“My grandchildren like to look through at the crabs,” she said. “I've taken them across my knee for it. Where's your cloak?”

“Eirik's got it.”

“Well, ask for it back before one of you catches cold. It's cold.”

She had kept the harbor accounts since the days when it was her job to send a red-faced young prince down to the quay to give back a duty charged twice. She had spent a year bringing seven shipmasters to agreement on the timber they needed for the wall. She had asked each master to pay the ordinary duty on one cargo a season early. The yard was expecting a raft of oak after the spring floods. It wanted proof that the city could pay for the timber on delivery. Gerda held the silver in the harbor chest against that day. Until their next cargoes sold, seven crews had less coin to divide, and only Gerda’s assurance that their king would spend the advance on the wall. She had a tally for the pledge, seven notches that had taken considerably longer to obtain than they had to cut.

The woman who owned the seventh ship was down in the gap now. She had a red wool scarf tied over her ears, slippers of plaited rope to grip the wet timber and a little awl in her hand to find the soft spots. Gerda lay on her stomach and handed her a dry length of rope to pin in next to each of the bad ones before the tide covered them. When the woman was out again she went straight to the drinking bucket and rinsed the salt from her eyes.

“Why are you doing that yourself, Runa?” Harald asked her. “There's men of mine down here to see to it.”

“Because when I said yes my crew took less home. I'd like to look the man in the face who tells me what I'm getting for their money.” She pulled a tuft of wood out of the awl like the last good meat out of a crab shell. “My boat is as old as I am. Some of the men have shares in her their mothers paid for. We can put her on a beach and mend her with our hands. But if the wall goes out in an east wind, it's houses we'll be trying to beach.”

“There's road money wanted inland,” Gerda said. “His brother's come down to ask for it.”

“Do you mean to pay it out of ours?”

Harald was well aware of the king of Hrafnborg standing there above her, whose answer she was entitled to. It took no effort at all to let her go on looking at Gerda.

“I gave you my word I'd keep account of every piece of silver,” Gerda said at last. “I can still give you that.”

Runa picked up the rope slippers. The cuts went so deep in the soles you could see the pattern of the boards she had been standing on. As she walked away in her wet stockings, Eirik watched the thread of water she left along the quay. “She could walk into our road house and speak in just those words,” he said. “All our money's in the road underneath where we put our feet. My father worked on that retaining wall the year you came to us. Somewhere behind it there's a hole with your good shoe in it yet.”

Eirik lay down on his belly and listened to the seawater. He got up wiping his ear. “I can find the straight timber above my house. How much is the haulage?”

For an absurd moment Harald was angry with him. He had wanted Eirik to look into that gap, sit down on the planks and admit that it was plainly impossible for the coast to pay toward a mountain road. Instead, his brother had set about finding trees for him.

At the kings' table that afternoon Harald spoke first. The ports had fleets, harbor chains, sea walls. Last year his own boatmen had fetched out the crews of two foundering grain ships. He asked his fellow kings what mountain lord had ever paid them a penny toward such expenses. One by one the kings agreed to keep the duties in their own ports. Eirik took their refusal home in writing. He left before Harald came down to breakfast. He had put the rest of the salt fish back in the small pantry, weighted with a plate to keep the flies off, as he did at home.

## Chapter Two · The Offer

The mountain kings closed the grain roads at harvest. At first Harald's city managed with barley brought round the headland in smaller ships. Then a gale brought down the rest of a granary roof that had long needed mending. Wet grain warmed in the heap. When Gerda put Harald's hand into it the heat was almost pleasant. He could feel the winter's bread spoiling under his palm.

Gerda had brought a woman up onto the granary floor with them. A light rain fell through the broken roof and gathered in drops on a single long gray hair on her chin. She had two baskets on a pole across her shoulder. Harald knew the smell when she took the cloth off. His nurse had always baked barley cakes that way, laid out on hot stones till they had gone brownish red underneath.

“I came to ask what you're counting for a loaf,” she said. “Gerda says the king wants an honest account of how many people he can feed.”

He picked up a cake. It broke between his fingers. There was a little bran in the middle still dry enough to scatter. He could see the husks and the last scrapings out of the bottom of the flour sack. He had asked how many cakes came out of a measure of barley, and the answer had looked well enough on paper.

“Go on, eat it. Don't be proud. The bit that fell down's clean. I swept this floor yesterday myself.”

He ate it. He had to drink from the dipper to get the last of it down. “How long have you been making them like this?”

“Since the little boats stopped coming round the point. My sister lays half of hers away till evening, though there's not much of it to put away. If you had asked for a dozen, you'd have been told we were still making a dozen.” She turned her hand and tipped the pieces out of his palm into the corner of the cloth. “I can put those in her soup. That counts for something as well.”

He went down to the wharf with her. The wind had picked up and he could feel the pole bite through his coat where he shouldered it. For an entire quay's length he had to walk bent a little sideways. She had let him take both baskets as a matter of course. She carried them straight enough to fool a man into thinking they weighed alike. When he handed it to her again, he understood that whatever account he ordered from these people next, they would do exactly what he asked and keep the other half of the truth for their own use.

At the end of Einmánuður, the last month before spring planting, a letter came from Eirik. The villagers near his gate had eaten the sweet turnips they'd put by to plant for seed. There was a sack of good grain standing under every holy image in the road house to keep the damp out of it. Nobody could pay the men who had grown it. Eirik had written down exactly what a sack fetched that day and how little a day of road work earned. Harald turned the letter over. On the back, scrawled in a much younger fist, was a line saying that his great-niece had her first tooth. He kept that part. He had the figures on the other side copied out for the kings.

In the river valleys the price of grain fell as it rose in Hrafnborg. Men had the food and could not sell it. In the spring the river kings called their armies to escort the wagons through. When the coastal kings sent an army of their own, the two forces agreed to wait outside the gates while both sides asked for passage. The river kings believed no city would buy a private way through and leave the others to fight. Harald marched inland at the head of it with a paper pinned inside his coat on which Gerda had written how many days his people could still be fed. He'd asked her to write it small. Her eyes were going, and the figures took up hardly less room than his hand.

There were nearly four thousand families' worth of men behind his banner on the road to Stairwatch. A whole company from the coast went past him bawling a song in which the mountain kings were to be dropped into the sea and made to tell the fishermen the price of wading out for them. Harald found himself watching for Eirik's little wooden house, though it was miles away on the other side of a ridge.

The western gate stood between two steps of a mountain pass called Titan's Stair. Giants had cut the flights, or so everyone said who had ever tried to get a laden mule up one of them. In the last great war the dead of two armies had lain together up there. A white blur of wood anemones marked the long field where they had been laid. Now when the air moved downhill the men below could smell other men's cook fires from the eastern approach.

The night they came to the gate he went out to see what the singing men had found to eat. Four of them had come up on a wagon from his home quay with a pot that had once been used to melt pitch for the seams of boats. Every spoonful of supper tasted of the shipyard. Runa had given them a dry place to sleep aboard her boat the night before they left. One of them, a sailmaker, had been given her own berth because he was the one whose wife was expecting a child before summer. He wanted the king to take a spoon and try whether you could eat this stuff. They had sent to the mountain people to borrow a pot, and the pot that came down had been full of hot peas with meat in them. They'd kept the peas to make a second supper of them. Taking a share of the first seemed only fair.

“Whose pot did they send?” Harald asked.

“Want a look at the bottom?” The sailmaker held it out for him. There was a little crooked tower cut in the handle, with a bird the size of the tower on its roof. As a boy he had cut the very same mark, a good deal more crudely, on the sheep hurdles that belonged to Eirik's house.

“What sort of fellows are up there?” the man asked. “You lived here. I heard someone say you did.”

“An old woman who certainly knows where her pot has gone,” Harald said. “A man sent to settle a price who couldn't leave a shore woman in wet stockings alone long enough to get it done. My nephew, who's learned what it costs to feed a child. I should have brought one of them to Hrafnborg when I put the matter to the kings.”

The men gave him their biggest spoon. He got the first lot of supper down. With it on his breath he went to see his men who had the sick horses and the ones who had made camp out of range of the water. Darkness came late in the pass. That upper camp went to bed with full daylight on their heads while down where the children were sleeping in the wagons there were stars.

Next morning Eirik came out of the gate by himself. He wore a helmet that had belonged to his father. There was a catch missing under one ear. Harald remembered the red place the old man used to get there when in armor and how his wife would wet the end of her apron to wipe it clean.

“Will you come inside?” Eirik asked.

“I've brought Gerda.”

“She's welcome. My mother would like to know about the slippers.”

The other mountain commanders were waiting for them in the guardroom. A board on trestles held cheese, cups of beer and the seals of three kings in a lump of warm wax. Harald had come prepared to bargain hard. He had expected a day of it. The bargain they offered him took a moment to read. All wagons bound for Hrafnborg could go through. He might take his ships' dues, his men and all the grain the city could buy, and go home.

“We'd have to stand aside when the river army comes to the east gate,” Gerda said.

“That is what we ask,” said the eldest of the mountain commanders.

She looked at Harald. “Then for the love of your people take it. My daughter's children were eating yesterday's broth with water in it when I came away.”

He must have grinned. He remembered being conscious of having done so afterward. To come up a mountain with thousands of men expecting a fight and find food and your brother and an end to a bad winter laid on a table for you like supper. He put his arms round Eirik.

“Where's Aron posted? If I've missed his wedding I can at least buy him a drink.”

His brother's back under his hands was stiff. “He's outside. His company leaves with mine for the eastern gate when your last wagon clears the west.”

Harald let go. Through the open door he could see a young man sitting on an overturned bin, mending a fastening under the ear of a helmet. He wore a strip of soft linen wound round one finger where he'd been handling the sharp metal. The foot with which he kept the bin from rocking had a boot on it whose top was punctured with little teeth marks. A toddler could get to just about that height when her father put his boots on in the morning.

Eirik went to the doorway. “Aron, is the child walking yet?”

“Walking! She's halfway to the sea by now. She gets herself under the bed and turns round and shows us her heels. Her mam thought I could do something useful with this helmet while I'm waiting. Did nobody ever tell you to take a turn and mind me while she tried to get something done?”

“I took a turn,” Harald said. “You could howl the roof off in those days.”

“Listen to this one, then.” Out of his pocket Aron brought a piece of soft wood with two slits in it. A breath across the slits made a piercing squawk. His wife had made it to call the little girl out when she couldn't reach her. Twice he had tried it on the other men in the gate, he told them, and both times somebody went looking for a child in distress. The night before last he had almost been home to hear the real one. They'd called him back when the river army's fires showed red on the ridge.

Harald had been looking at the great scarred seals on the document. He had his own hand free and the signet on his finger. A little boy he had wanted to christen for a dead man stood inside a half day's walk of his own house and had had to invent the sound of his child. The paper on the board would let Harald go home to a good supper and leave the boy where he was.

“Does your mother know where you're going?” Harald asked his brother, and instantly wished himself dead for making such a fool's inquiry.

“Where did you think we'd go, Hari? Did you think they'd give us the afternoon off?”

## Chapter Three · Before the Gate Opened

They went down to Stairwatch without signing. Gerda rode beside him in silence till they came to the tent pitched for the coastal kings. She took the mule round twice looking for a place to tie it. Harald went out and took its head.

“If I accept, how long do you think we'll eat in peace?” he asked.

“Long enough for my grandchildren to put some flesh on. That's the part I can do something about.”

“You lay on the wall beside him when he offered to find us timber. What do I tell his mother?”

“Did you ask after that wagoner's family when you took the vote? And if you mean to take the wall money, tell my daughter where she should sleep when the waves come through her kitchen. You make a deal of going to speak to people's mothers when you've a mind to be decent. You've four thousand out there with mothers. Speak to them.”

The mule chewed his glove. It took him several seconds to get possession of his own hand. Gerda watched him do it, and the familiarity of her dear, disagreeable face made his throat ache.

“Stay with me,” he said.

“Of course I'm staying. Somebody will have to read you the figures.”

The men from the harbor wall, the river yards and the ships came up in companies to hear him. He read out the mountain kings' terms loud enough that the back ranks must have heard. A murmur went up when the men at the back found out what he had fetched down the mountain. He had to read the last part twice. Several of the men called for the gate to be opened. One man took the round hat off his head and held it out to show how much grain he reckoned his children needed. A gap opened around him so the king could see it.

Harald turned the offer over on the table. On the back he wrote to the river queen in his own hand. He had brought four thousand people to a gate she was preparing to approach from the other side of a mountain. She deserved to know exactly what price the mountain kings had placed on his friendship. He wrote that he had turned them down and invited her to meet him where both armies could see the bargain they made. He put his seal to those words. He sent the whole paper away over the herdsmen's path, the offer on one side and his answer on the other. When the messenger was out of sight he suddenly felt the cold on the wet backs of his knees. He had come up that mountain with one good answer to children who went to bed hungry. He had just sent it to a stranger on a mule. One of the coastal kings seized his arm. “There's nothing to stop them taking the gate while we're still talking.”

“There will be if they hear what's being said. Did anybody here know Eirik's father?” A few older men stirred. “His son came to my house in autumn and told me the east road had come down. He asked the ports to help pay for its repair. I asked you all what that road had ever cost us. I owe the wagoner who went off it an answer to that.”

He called the company captains and ordered them to bring down the men who had been placed above the western gate. Nobody had opened it. The duke who kept the salt coast to the north walked out on him. For an ugly half hour Harald did not know whether the duke had gone to call up his own people and take the gate. Then on the opposite slope he saw shield after shield turn its bright face toward him as his men came downhill. The northern men came too. Their duke walked at the back of his own company, quite white with rage, doing as he had been ordered.

They waited in the trampled place where the men had stood to hear him. The grass that had been under the companies' cooking pots had gone white for want of light. A corporal went about the empty hearths picking up nails to give to a boy who helped at the forge. Every time the boy let one drop into his sack, Harald heard the small click of iron and looked up at the path again.

The river queen came down on foot, for the track was too narrow for her mount to turn. She had the paper pushed up under her belt. Salt had dried in streaks from her temples to her jaw. At the sight of her, one of the coastal companies still on the road put up a cheer. She looked back at them, visibly counted the number of helmets in the sunlight, and waited till their last man had gone by.

“I had my army on its feet,” she told Harald. “One more night in that place and there would have been a fight about clean water before we ever reached this gate. You sent me the original. How did you know I wouldn't take it to the other kings and read it in the worst possible light?”

“I hoped you would take it to the other kings. They should hear in my handwriting how close I came to signing. For the rest I know one of the men who'd have to fight you tomorrow. I'd as soon eat his flesh as let him find out that way what I thought of him.”

“There's a girl in your camp who came up the path to meet me. You should speak to her father. He's first in line outside your western gate with a load that's been in the wet. Two days on the other side with it and he gets to keep his farm. One more day on this side and my going home with an army is small comfort to him.”

At the gate Harald held out his hand to Gerda. She gave him the split tally, seven cuts on either piece, the king's half and the shipmasters' fitted together. The girl led the queen down the line to a farmer from the western foothills. He had promised his barley to a mill in her valley on the far side of both gates. His daughter had driven all winter with him because the cold had taken the use of three fingers from his right hand.

“The duties from these ships will go to the upkeep of this road,” Harald said. “Both approaches. My ships will pay the same as any man's. Here is half the tally. Gerda will tell you what it's worth. I should like that man to get his barley to his buyer before it spoils. Will you let him through?”

He had spent the winter making fine speeches to his own people about exactly what other people owed them. He could think of nothing more to say to these men while they considered what he had just offered. Eirik came down with his father's helmet on his head. He put his shoulder under the long timber that held the gate.

“Give us a hand, Hari.”

They had done this on the sheepfold a thousand times. Harald knew how to lift so Eirik could get his leg clear. The bar came up. Down at the eastern gate there came the creak of another bar rising. The girl led the horses through and the wagoner went to sleep beside his own grain before the wheels had finished turning.

Aron was waiting with the mended helmet. Eirik traded his father's for the repaired one. “You can take your uncle back down,” he said. “He's been wanting to buy you a drink.”

“Could I go home and see my little girl instead?”

Harald sent for his horse. An hour hadn't passed before the boy was on his way.

## Chapter Four · The Man Who Spoke for Him

Nobody at the waterfront had been up the mountain to see it. What they saw was Gerda coming down the wharf with half the tally and the king beside her to say where the money had gone. Harald took his place on the sea wall to answer them. From there he could see water working up between the floorboards of the net shed with every tide.

A woman asked him whether she would have to move with every easterly wind. A shipmaster who had wanted the work for his daughter's new husband asked what the boy was going to live on. Somebody called Harald a thief. Gerda asked who'd said it, and when the man put his hand up she wrote down his name. She was collecting the names of everyone who had paid so they could all question the new accounts. The man looked at her scrap of paper with his mouth still half open.

“Would you move your own family?” asked the woman from the net shed again.

“Yes,” Harald said. “Would you come up to the old sail loft? I've got room and you can still get down to your work. Gerda, is the roof tight up there?”

“You can go and see. You know where the buckets are.”

It took them all afternoon. He went to the sail loft that evening with Gerda. They set three buckets under the leaks and shifted the king’s sails to the wet end before the families moved in. Gerda's daughter lived at the back of the net shed. Harald went in on his way home. It was Laugardagr, the day everybody washed sheets and the shirts that lay against the skin. Every shirt in that house hung from the ceiling to well above his knees. The little girl between the hanging sheets was in her bare skin on a table, having learned exactly how much her mother could manage at one time. Her brother got her into a dry shift while their mother scoured the floor where the sea had worked through. There was a little thin silver fish lying dead in the ashes. Harald picked it out and found the girl's mother watching him.

“My own mother thinks I should be grateful you've come,” she said. “If the water's any higher when we move, who's going to carry my father up the hill? He used to make rope, and now he can't tie a string at his neck.”

“Would he let me do it?”

“He'd make an objection to anybody doing it. You could bear his objections as well as I can by now.”

Her father was heavy. He wore a leather apron when he got up, even now, and they had left his belt on the bed. His daughter had to show Harald how to make him comfortable against his shoulder. Halfway up the street he objected to the king's carrying a grown man about like a child where people could watch it. Harald's ear was full of his warm breath. The king had spoken at considerable length to four thousand men about the dignity of their neighbors and hadn't the smallest ready answer for this one. He set him down in a doorway facing the harbor. They had a good view there of two boys taking their first skiff out against the tide.

“Can I walk you the last piece when you've had your breath?”

“Give me your shoulder under here. I know how to put a foot in front of me yet.”

It took the two of them till the lamps were burning. He saw the old man into bed. After that, when he heard the wind inside his own chimney at night, he could put a face to the man who would be awake at the sail loft listening to the same thing.

He gave over his own oak from the naval yard for the worst of the gap in the wall. The rest would take longer. Later in the summer, timber with Eirik's household mark on the end grain began coming down the road. Runa brought Harald to see the first piece lowered into place. There was a deep groove in the underside where the sledge had come off a runner coming down the pass. Aron had opened one hand getting the thing raised. Runa fetched two pairs of the knitted wool gloves her crew wore when they drew wet rope. She put the glove for his sore hand on him herself.

“Does that settle it, then?” Harald asked Gerda when the men had come clear.

“Settles where a piece of timber came from. You're still going to have to get up in front of Runa once a year and tell her what you've done with her money.”

“I know. I was asking about the tide.”

The three of them stayed on the quay until they could see the water make its first long pull against the new oak. Nothing came up through the net shed. Runa took a dry chip home in her pocket to show the old man at the sail loft.

Gerda made the journey to Thingvellir beside him in an empty grain wagon. Every king who sent anyone to that summer gathering in the Danelaw had to let the rest of his companions pitch their tents on the same open ground, buyers and farmers and men charged with sheep stealing all together. Harald woke to a voice on the other side of his tent trying to teach a child how to spit a cherry stone. The first stone came over almost at once. He thought of the little wooden whistle in Aron's pocket.

On the first morning Gerda knocked a trestle table together outside the tent and fastened the half tally to it with a bit of cord. Runa was among those who came to find it. She brought other shipmasters, and each could lay a finger on the notch for the cargo he had pledged. There was one who had walked off Harald's quay that spring calling the king a thief. Runa stood by while he worked out what his next cargo should cost him after the advance was counted. It took him three tries. When he had the number right he sat down with paper and a lump of lead to write it out plain enough for his crew. That was the man who would have to announce it when they divided the catch.

The rulers gave the business of the roads two full days. Nobody would agree to a common toll until they knew what happened when the kings quarreled next. A mountain woman who had driven the first meat wagons through asked whether they were to bring their own army every spring. A pilot asked who would judge a claim against Harald if his ships failed to pay. Harald went across to the trestle table. He waited while Gerda untied the tally and walked back beside her so that every ruler present could see who kept it.

“Ask her for my accounts,” he said. “She'll have them here every summer. If any of you has a question for me, you can ask it before the people who paid.”

It went round the circle. The river people agreed to maintain the landings. The mountain kings gave up the right to shut a gate because they had fallen out with another ruler. Each put a name to what he or she owed. By evening a farmer could take a wagon to market across three kingdoms knowing what to bring to pay the toll on the way home.

They came at last to the question of who should call out their armies should a country beyond the passes march on them. Harald expected the river queen to speak. It was Eirik who got up. He had put his father's lopsided helmet under his seat. In the clean summer light he was a man going bald at the temples, in a shirt his mother had probably had to order him to wash. Harald would have known him anywhere by the crooked finger he hooked in his belt.

“I'll take Harald of Hrafnborg,” he said.

“Even after he brought four thousand men up to your gate?” asked the duke of the salt coast.

“That's why I'm speaking. I stood there with a bar down and my boy beside me. Harald could have fed his city and let us go and fight the river people. He had brought a good army and they were angry enough to do what he asked. I saw what he asked. My boy's home with his wife. I'd trust Harald with him again.”

Men had been able to get a laugh out of Harald about that brother of his since he was old enough to walk across a crowded floor without losing his drawers. He found he couldn't get a word out. The river queen stood to give her assent, and one by one as the rulers rose the people at the back took up their king's name. Later they would call him Harald the Unifier. That afternoon, with the heat still on the grass, it was simply his own name coming at him in the accents of a dozen different valleys. Eirik waited for him at the edge of the assembly with two portions of boiled fish in a clean cloth.

“I thought you might want supper,” he said.

“I do,” said Harald. His voice had come back. “Move over and give me half.”
