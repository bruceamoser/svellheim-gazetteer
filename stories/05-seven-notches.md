# Seven Notches

## Chapter One · What the Port Owned

Before he was old enough to command a boat, Harald had kept count of the vessels that came in on the morning tide. He had been wrong on the first day. One ship came in twice. On its second visit it carried six families taken off a burning vessel beyond the headland. His father had made him go down to the quay and give back the second harbor duty himself.

The clerk who had taught him to keep the tally still worked at the harbor of Hrafnborg when Harald became its king. Her name was Gerda. That spring the grain wagons stopped coming over the mountain. The mountain kings had closed their gates until somebody helped them pay for a road every grain buyer on the coast used for free. An army from the river country was marching to force the gates. When Harald led his coastal army up to the mountain town of Stairwatch, Gerda went too, on a mule that disliked him only a little more than its rider did.

“You have taken the people who mend the sea wall,” she told him the night an offer came from the mountain kings. “You have taken the hands who keep the chain across the harbor. Take our money as well and the next gale can come in through the front gate.”

He handed her the offer. The mountain rulers would open the pass for grain bound for his city. In return he had only to let them face the river army on their own.

“Say yes,” she said.

He had expected a lecture about the common good. Gerda had once paid a stranger’s toll from her own purse. He had told that story of her in three kingdoms, and here she sat with both hands wrapped around the paper.

“My daughter has three children,” she said. “They live in your city. I want them fed. Is there a better offer on the back?”

## Chapter Two · The Other Gate

They went up to the western gate before sunrise. The river army lay beyond the crest. Harald could see the tops of its cook fires above the old burial field. A raven went back and forth quite freely between both camps carrying scraps to a nest.

Below them grain wagons stood axle to axle. Harald asked Gerda what money he had. From her saddlebag she took the tally for seven ships due home by summer. Seven deep cuts, one for each duty on its cargo. The shipmasters had pledged that money for timbers to repair the sea wall.

“How bad is the wall?”

“My grandchildren put their heads through it to watch the crabs.” She split the stick lengthways. Fit the halves together and even a child could see if somebody had slipped in another debt.

“Which end do we keep?” he asked.

She gave him a long look. “Oh, Harald.”

Harald sent the mountain kings’ offer to the river camp with his own seal on it. The woman who commanded the grain escort came up on foot. She had brought a cobbler named Ove from the head wagon. At home he made good shoes. The army had been standing here long enough for him to lose the use of three fingers to the cold.

“You can have your share of the harbor money,” Harald told the mountain men. He laid half the tally on the gatepost. “I want Ove here to take his wagon through.”

The man at the gate started taking down the bar. “If it’s bound for your city, we’ve already offered you that.”

“It’s barley for the river,” said Ove.

The gatekeeper let the bar settle back into its brackets. Behind them Harald’s own captains were listening. He could put food in his markets and send those men back safely to their families if he took the offer as it stood. He looked at Gerda. She had both hands in her pockets. She was leaving it to him. He wished very much she wouldn’t.

“You’ll have to take the team through yourself,” he said to the gatekeeper. “Look at his hands.”

The river captain put down her sword belt and went to the off horse’s head. For a moment it looked as though she meant to turn a loaded wagon on that grade. The gatekeeper swore. He heaved on the bar and jerked his head for Harald to take the other end. Then he went down to take the near bridle. Ove walked his barley between them through the gate. Harald called his companies back off the mountain that afternoon.

When he came back Gerda was packing the mule.

“You’ve made me responsible for both ends of a road I’ve never seen,” she said. “I mean to have a look at the other end.”

She rode out with the first grain wagons. It was four days before he saw her again. She had mud on her coat to the shoulder and the mule, apparently reconciled to its duties, had acquired a bag of excellent oats.

“The far retaining wall is coming down,” she said. “Three more wagons like that last one and you’ll be buying a bridge too.”

“Have you told the mountain people?”

“I took them there. What do you suppose kept me?”

## Chapter Three · The King’s Half

At the summer assembly at Thingvellir, Ove sold Harald a pair of boots broad enough for him to curl his toes inside them. He had made them with the help of an apprentice. Harald was wearing them when the kings who had faced one another at the pass took his hand and asked him to lead their common defense. A skald began a song in which Harald stood on a shield with a sword in his hand while the other kings knelt. Gerda laughed so hard at the first performance that the singer had to begin again. By autumn the children of Hrafnborg could sing every word. They called him Harald the Unifier.

Years afterward, three winters into the cold that had closed the southern sea, Harald heard the song through the wall while he sat with Gerda’s daughter over the fuel accounts. The woman set her mother’s old split tally beside his cup. Both halves were there.

“The road men sent the other half back when the last wagonload of timber came down to mend our wall. She kept the pair in the flour crock. May I ask why?”

He turned the two pieces until their rough faces fitted. Seven notches. For a moment there was the mule, alive and malicious in a pool of lamplight, and Gerda asking if there was a better offer on the back.

“Your mother wanted to keep her grandchildren fed,” he said. “I committed the money. She went over the mountain and found out what we had bought.”

“Did you ever pay her for the mule?”

He looked up. She had Gerda’s way of holding in a laugh through her nose. Outside, the singer had raised him onto the shield again.

“No,” he said. “What did she say it was worth?”

The daughter named a sum which would have bought a small ship in the good years. He disputed it. She disputed him back. It was the first satisfactory argument he had had since Gerda died.
