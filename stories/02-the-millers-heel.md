# The Miller’s Heel

## Chapter One · Flour on credit

The first time Vargeld put his hand in mine I thought he wanted the next dance. We were at a wedding. I had been looking at him over somebody’s shoulder for half the evening, and when he finally got to me I was pleased enough to let him see it.

“I hear you know how to save eighty people’s supper,” he said.

The plank across the mouth of the village oven had caught fire. There was soot all over the dough. In the yard the bride’s mother stood with a pan in her apron and a look of such misery that I unbuttoned my good sleeves and gave them to an uncle who happened to be standing there. Vargeld took me to his father’s mill for flour.

He was twenty-three that summer. His father had lost the use of one arm, so the mill was already his to run. I was up from the goblin houses in the mountain with my mother, who had made the bride’s veil. The bride had once worked beside me baking for the quarry gangs. I could feed eighty hungry people. I had no oven of my own and no prospect of one. On the shelf by his scales I saw a blue bowl of fine wheat flour, enough to make the sort of white loaf a bride ought to be able to give her guests.

“That’s my father’s,” he said. “He keeps it for the priest.”

I took my hand away.

“He’s invited the priest. I think that answers.” He tipped it into my apron.

Back in the yard he got the fires going in four of the neighbors’ houses. I divided the flour and put a woman at each table with her hands in the dough. It was a warm night. While we worked, men who had been drinking took the scorched boards off the old oven. Vargeld had a way of going up to a man who had sat firmly down and putting a question to him about something he knew. He asked the groom’s grandfather how they used to lift the old stones. Ten minutes later the old fellow was up on a box bossing the young men, as happy as I ever saw him. Vargeld’s one piece of advice to me was to make the loaves thinner. I suggested that the person who had time to give advice might go and find my sleeves. He went.

The bride danced in her stockings, with a piece of my hot bread in one hand and honey running down the other. Long after dark Vargeld found me behind the last house with the bit of heel I had saved myself.

“I meant to ask you something quite different when I came across the room,” he said.

“I know. I can dance and eat.”

He put my sleeves back on for me first. He fastened every little button properly, although by then there was hardly a tune left. I remember his bent head and the way he wet his thumb to get a stubborn loop over the last button. A woman gets old. Whole years go, and she can still put her thumb on the place on her wrist where a good-looking boy once held it.

I came down for flour the next morning. The bride’s people had been too busy getting married to put by anything for breakfast. I came the day after because there was a little of yesterday’s dough left to start a fresh batch. By the end of the month people were bringing crocks to the mill to collect their dough from me, and Vargeld’s father was asking when he might reasonably expect to see a penny.

We struck a bargain over the scales. Vargeld would mill what I needed on credit. I would bake, pay him on market day, and keep what I made above the price of his flour. When the cart shed came down in a gale, he let me build an oven against the wall that stood. I put my name over it. My mother came down to see it and put her hand to the painted letters as though trying the brow of a child for fever. She had been sewing in other people’s houses all her life. She stayed to see the first loaf come out and broke off the heel for Frigg beside the new hearth. A mouse took it during the night. Mother said the goddess had as much right as anybody to keep a pet.

Every day after that I made bread from Vargeld’s flour. When I had the ague he set the bowl at the end of my bed and I got an arm out to turn the dough. When his first boy was born I burned the bottoms of twenty loaves because he came down to the bakehouse to tell me and we sat on the floor together drinking the brandy intended for his wife. Dalla came down herself two days later and demanded recompense. The three of us made a night of it. She had married a pleasant man. For a great many years it was a pleasure to be their friend.

The miller’s heel was her invention. She caught Vargeld pushing his thumb into the end of a loaf I had set to rise.

“Born with a thumb in the flour and now he has to put it in the bread,” she said. Their small son thought this exceedingly funny. Soon both his little brothers had to have the same mark in theirs. It made a hollow that held butter. Customers began coming in early to ask if I had any of the miller’s heels left, and when Dalla fell ill it was one of the few things she would eat, soaked in broth, sitting up with her feet on her husband’s knee.

After her burial he began coming downstairs in his stocking feet in the hour before dawn. Some mornings I could hear him having a good wash before he came in, so I wouldn’t know he had been crying. He sat where his wife used to sit and watched my hands.

“Is it possible to hear a person after they’ve stopped speaking?” he asked me once.

“Happens to me every day with my mother. I’m halfway through telling an apprentice to wash behind his ears and there she is, large as life.”

“I don’t mean in your head. I hear them through the wall. I got up and went to the boys last night. All three sound asleep, and all the time down here somebody calling my name.”

I drew the bench away from the wall. He stretched out and I put my folded apron under his cheek. I told him whose house the next loaf was going to and who owed me for the last one. When I ran out of bakery business I told him the whole wretched story of my cousin’s marriage to a reed cutter with five geese and a belief that he could sing. By the fourth goose he was asleep. I took the bread out myself that morning so the apprentices wouldn’t see him with his poor swollen face. He got perhaps two hours before the first grain cart came. I was glad to have given him that much.

## Chapter Two · Two loaves on the sill

A long time passed before I understood quite how far a sack of our flour went. I had gone to the low village to buy a secondhand table. A carrier named Jon took me into his house to see it. There were bowls upside down at three places, a little sprig of dried heather stuck through a crack, and under the table their smallest girl was asleep on a bed made up in a packing case. I told him I could find another. He offered me his chairs as well.

“We used to have grain milled up the water,” his wife said. “Since that wheel broke we’ve been going over the ford. They’ve put a toll on the grain going out and another on the flour coming home. What Jon earns between one trip and the next just about pays the toll. That leaves the children to eat his boots.”

I brought Vargeld back with me. He sat down opposite her and made her take him through the reckoning. He asked what Jon wanted for the table and paid him. Next morning when Jon came to deliver it, Vargeld had a load waiting for him. The evening before, I had shown him on the floury counter how the old road through my people’s workings ran under the ford. A fall had blocked it at one end. Clear the stones and Jon could bring a wagon through to our yard again. Would Jon fetch men to clear it? The table could stay where it was until we saw how the business went.

Those men worked a week in cold mud on his word. At the far end there was a young widow whose husband had died under a fall much like it. She came up to the mill to tell Vargeld that her eldest was all she had left to earn. I thought she had come to get the boy out of the workings. She wanted to know when they would see their pay.

“Thursday,” he said.

“Last man he worked for said Thursday. We never did learn which one.”

He took the silver clasp off his cloak. “My wife gave me that. Bring it back when we’ve settled.”

I think that was when I learned what people saw in him. He knew it was a disgrace to go to a house with your hat in your hand to ask for money somebody else ought to have brought to yours. He took on as much of the embarrassment as he could. On Thursday night I saw the boy himself fastening the clasp beneath Vargeld’s chin. Soon we had farmers putting cattle at his disposal, carters hauling for him who had never taken orders from a miller, and men from three villages telling their lords they could take their own grain over the ford if they were so fond of that toll. Jon’s little girl grew too big for the packing case. The table stayed in the family. I ate at it often.

Word of fighting began coming in with the grain. An arrow had killed a ferryman at a lake some distance off. His brothers had gone out in a boat. Then a party of men had gone after the brothers. There were places on the bags I couldn’t read, and when I asked where they were I’d be told, “A good four days from here, Edda,” and I let it rest at that. One harvest we had three marriages among our oven hands. On market days there was a girl in a yellow hood who sang all the way from the fountain to our door. That was where I lived. I could step out under a blue sky and buy my supper off a woman I had known for twenty years. What did I know about a war?

I knew the day the king’s captain came for Vargeld’s two elder boys. The eldest had his mother’s thin wrists. None of the coats in the king’s cart would button tight enough at the cuffs, so he wore his own. His father promised them a loaf apiece when they came back. I baked two good ones each day and left them on the sill. In the evenings Vargeld divided them among whoever was at the door. There was no news for so long that the youngest grew into his eldest brother’s boots before a man who had been with them came to tell us where they lay.

That night Vargeld came down to me in one stocking. He’d been walking the flagstones till the sole of his bare foot was blue. I fetched hot water and sat rubbing his foot dry. He kept saying, “They’re in the little room, Edda. I have only to lift the latch. I can hear them plain as you.” His boys had shared a bed under the eaves for fifteen years.

“Tell me what they say.”

“One’s asking which loaf is his and one says he’s licked them both.” The old joke broke him. He put his head against my shoulder. He was a big man by then, with gray already coming above his ears. It was like trying to hold up a sodden winter coat. We sat till morning that way.

The news took a week to climb the road. Strangers who had never seen the boys brought us provisions. A dozen farmers came together and asked Vargeld to keep their seed grain under the mill where a passing army couldn’t seize it. He had the only wagons large enough to bring it down. One after another the little mills upstream lost their trade to him. He lent money to two whose wheels needed work. At another he paid off a debt to the lord. Both the meal and the men who ground it came to him after that.

A man called Ulfi held out. I was friendly with his wife. She used to sell twice as many little currant buns as I could on the very same stretch of market because she remembered who had a grandmother at home and liked to slip in a soft one. One day she came to ask me for flour.

“Ulfi’s cart is in the way at the landing, they tell me,” she said. “Yesterday he moved upriver. Today the men there say the sacks are spoken for. Edda, has my husband offended yours?”

“He’s never been my husband, for all the good it’s done either of us. Come in and have some tea.”

She stayed on the step. She had her best basket with her. It would hold all the flour her back could carry. “Would you ask him to let me buy again? I’ve paid the three girls who work for me out of my purse.”

At first I thought Vargeld must be ignorant of what was being done in his name. I found him in the old cart shed eating a baked apple out of its skin with a spoon. He looked delighted to see me and held out the other half of the apple. He listened while I told him what was happening to a woman we both knew.

“Ulfi can work,” he said. “He can grind whatever I send him. I offered him that. I can’t promise the upland towns a winter’s delivery and have him taking the best of the crop away to sell for silver.”

“You’ve known his wife since she had pigtails.”

“She may buy enough for the three girls to stay at work till the month’s end. Tell her you spoke for her.”

I thought it a little thing he’d given me. It took me the walk back to the bakery to understand that he could have shut her ovens cold by leaving the apple in his mouth and shaking his head. She thanked me. At the month’s end she took down her sign and came to work for me. I put her at the bench nearest the window where she could see her own people coming to market. I was pleased to be able to give her work. I was pleased with the takings on her currant buns, too.

## Chapter Three · The household share

The yellow-hooded singer stopped coming. One day a customer asked if anyone knew what had become of her. A woman at the back said her village was empty. Another had a brother working leather for a whole company of soldiers. He was stitching the miller’s mark into their coats. They were goblins, she said, and there were orcs in among them. She looked to me to tell her where they were quartered. I knew as much about it as she did. My mother had moved into the rooms above my ovens. I had no occasion now to go farther underground than the old trade road.

That evening I asked Vargeld straight out whether he was raising an army. We were alone in his counting room. I had spent most of the day at my books and I could still see the ghost of a column of figures when I closed my eyes. There ought to have been forty sacks of barley in my accounts that I couldn’t lay hands on.

“More than forty,” he said. “Some came from the other mills. Our people are down in the caves by the old lake. I have to feed them.”

“Our people?”

“Men and women I’ve raised to keep the roads open. A few of the old goblin companies are with us. Orc captains too. There are a good many of us now.”

“Who in the valley agreed to this?”

“Jon did, the year his children were eating grain picked out of his horses’ dung. So did all those men who cleared the ford for us. They asked me to keep the villages fed. How would you have me go about it with two kings taking what they please? Shall I carry a loaf to the captain who had my sons and ask him very nicely to leave yours alone?”

“My son is in a counting house on the coast. Leave him there when you and I quarrel.”

He came round the table and took the book off my knees. “I ought to have told you. If the king east of the mountains heard I had troops, he’d have his own fellows waiting at every one of our yards. The western king would hear it from him. How long before we had both armies at the door? As things stand, you can go to bed without a soldier billeted in your house. When this is done I want to come and sit in your kitchen with nothing worse on my mind than whether I’ve got there before the heel’s gone.”

“I kept you some this morning.”

He smiled. “I rather hoped you had.”

There was a dish of it by the stove. I heard him scraping the last of the butter out of its crock while I lay awake upstairs. By then two villages had lost their roofs in fighting over the grain road. We still had butter. In the morning I entered the forty sacks under the cost of carriage. I had kept an honest set of books for better than twenty years. The figuring came out quite neatly.

The mill wheel stopped one evening for repairs and I woke through lack of the familiar noise. I could hear Vargeld below me. For a moment I supposed he had finally taken sick. I went downstairs in my shift.

Under the mill, behind the meal bins, there was a recess loosely walled with stacked stones. I had supposed it was full of sweepings. A few of the stones had been taken down. I could see the back of his head beyond them and, farther in, a little stool with a bowl on it.

“I have kept both your loaves,” he was saying. “You may tell your brother so. Now let me hear the others.”

I got close enough to see the whole of the room. There wasn’t anybody in it with him. He turned his good ear toward the stone as though an answer had come. Once, very distinctly, I heard him say Jon’s name.

I must have caught my breath. He spun round. “Get out,” he said, with such cold assurance that I had already backed against the bins before I knew I’d moved. Then he saw my bare feet. He put his coat about me and rubbed my arms through the sleeves.

“Who are you talking to?”

“The gods, as a rule. I made myself a little temple. It’s rather a poor thing to have somebody walk in on.” On the shelf there was a toy ox with the horn broken off. I had bought it for his eldest on a market day. Beside it stood a wooden horse that had lost almost all its red paint. “Sometimes I like to say good night to my boys in here. There’s no peace upstairs till after dark.”

“I heard you name Jon.”

“He’s on the lake road tonight. I pray for him to come home with his cart. His wife prays upstairs. Does this seem a worse place to do it?”

“I heard you ask for the others. You listen down here for things you ought to go out into the yard and ask people to their faces. I was there when you told me the walls talked to you. Do you still hear voices?”

“Yes. When I come down here they’re quite clear. I sleep better after.” He felt in one of the bins for a clean sack and spread it under my bare feet. “Edda, your mother eats from our ovens. So do Ulfi’s hired girls. So does every family on the east bank whose men are away. Get out your books. See whether any of them has had less since I began keeping watch for them. If a man needs to shut the door and talk to his dead boys to get up and see to all those people in the morning, I hope an old friend would let him.”

I had seen that same man sitting on my bench with his face swollen from weeping. I understood exactly why he might want a room to himself. I let him put the stones back. We went upstairs, and I made him tea before I went back to bed.

The next spring he sent word for me to bring a loaf to the weighing house. There were a dozen women there and two men, one with a baby and the other trying to fit a little girl’s foot back into a shoe that had belonged to somebody bigger. Most had husbands and sons away at war. Vargeld stood behind my scales.

“It costs us twice as much as last year to bring in the grain,” he told them. “The army has to be fed, and half the households drawing from these stores have nobody in service. There will be a war tax. One child from each house for the work below. The families who pay will draw their flour and bread in full. The rest will draw half.”

I had expected to serve them some of the new barley bread. I still had the loaf against my chest. The baby on its father’s knee was making a wet spot on the clean shirt the man had put on for the meeting.

Jon’s wife, Sella, spoke from the end of the bench. Her man had come home with a cut across his face the week before. We had heard there was fighting along the lake road. I had counted the sacks off his cart before I thought to ask him about it. “How old?”

“Old enough to carry a bowl and wash it. The little ones have soft work. There are stores to look after, boots to mend, warm beds. They’ll be under cover while the armies fight.”

“My girl’s eleven. She goes seasick crossing a puddle. Who’ll look after her?”

Vargeld looked at me. “Edda will get them settled.”

He knew what he had done by bringing me there with bread in my arms. A child took a step my way, and the mother who had hold of him managed to draw him back very gently. I can still see her looking down at his head so that I needn’t look at her face.

After they left I put the loaf on the scales. It was cold enough to eat. It was one of the two with his thumbprint. His youngest son had taken work far off by then. We still made the pair.

“You lost two sons to a man who wanted a road held,” I said. “How can you go in there and take that woman’s girl?”

“I know what it is to eat alone with their places laid. Do you suppose I would send them into that?” He brought a little work jacket out of his cupboard. It was wool, with a good lining and soft patches at the elbows. He let me feel how warm it was. I turned a cuff back to look at the stitching. It had been made by somebody who knew children’s clothes. “I want their fathers and mothers at their trades. Somebody has to see to the men below. When the soldiers come up the children can have their summers back. Yours went away to learn a trade, Edda. It was your hand that packed his box.”

“My boy knew where he was going.”

“Then take them down yourself. Look the families in the eye. Would you sooner those captains came into your yard and picked their own?”

It troubles me that I don’t remember what I said next. I remember going down to Sella’s with the little jacket to see if it would fit her girl. The sleeves were long. I turned them back and she put her hands out for me to admire them.

Her name was Disa. She was the baby who used to sleep in a box under the table Vargeld had bought. She had grown into a spindly child with two cowlicks coming together above her nose and an ambition to get out of the valley and see the sea. She kept a stone somebody had brought her from the shore in a pocket she sewed into each new dress. It was a fine gray stone, with one white stripe all the way round its middle. She carried it up to my mother’s room for her to put her nose to because she thought there must be something of the sea still on it.

On the first morning I took six children down the mill steps. Disa led the way. She had taken charge of a boy who cried, and the best I could do by either of them was follow with his little bundle and tell him that flour on the walls of a cellar shone white exactly like flour upstairs. At the bottom a woman in a soldier’s coat waited by a passage I had never entered. She knew the children’s names. There were lamps in niches along the first stretch and Disa went from one to the next looking for me over her shoulder.

“If we get any letters will you bring them down?” she called.

“Bring you a letter? I shall come and see you myself.”

“Mam’s making me stockings. Will you bring them when you come?”

“I’ll bring them down and we’ll see if she’s allowed for those big feet.”

She went on quite cheerfully after that. I gave the boy his bundle and watched till they passed the last turn. Upstairs a woman had gone out to be sick in the horse trough. Somebody had drawn the first batch of bread for me. The heels were scorched. The rest was as good a bake as we had ever had.

## Chapter Four · The stockings

I sent a letter down to Disa the next week. I wrote that her father’s mare had taken to scratching herself against the bakery door and had rubbed off three letters of my name. Sella tucked in a pair of stockings. The woman at the passage took the parcel, and when I asked to go down with her she said the children had moved on to the south stores. I sent the carter a message to ask after her. He came back with a broken wagon shaft and a load of empty barrels. He had seen nobody but the men who rolled them out.

More children came. I knew them first at the bread counter with their families and a second time by their little parcels. One brought a wooden comb big enough for a horse’s mane. Another had his dinner wrapped in a perfectly good lace cap because it was the only thing in his mother’s pocket when they called for him. I told them I would see to their letters. Some weeks a child on the stair forgot how to breathe, and I sat down beside him and got him laughing about one of the weddings at the mill. I was proud of being able to do this where the woman in the soldier’s coat could only stand and wait.

At midsummer I gave a woman half a sack because she had kept her eldest home. I put an extra scoop in when nobody was looking. She stood with her elbows on the counter adding up the meals that would buy them. Her small son reached for a currant that had fallen in the crack between the boards. She struck the back of his hand. The next instant she had him in her arms, kissing his knuckles and begging his pardon. I would rather she had struck me, and I think she would have liked that better too.

I carried their sack out for them. In the yard, the man from the weighing house had scraped the old figures off the board and put up new ones. A family that kept all its children was to have a quarter share from now on. He wanted to be sure the woman had seen it. He walked after her all the way to the road with his piece of chalk in his hand, calling, “Next time, mistress. Just so you know. Next time.”

One morning I cut a miller’s heel to put by for Disa. I had been doing it since she first came to the bakery alone and stood on tiptoe to slap a copper on the counter. She’d eaten the rim of the hollow first so the butter ran over both her hands. I could scarcely separate in my mind all the mornings I had watched her do it. I stood there with the bread and tried to think of one child from below whom I had seen since. Any child, from any house. One who had come back to have an aching tooth drawn. One who had forgotten the shape of his mother’s face and been given a day to go and look at her. My ovens had burned every day that spring. I had stood where anyone coming up would have to pass me. There had never been one.

Sella had a row of little chalk strokes beside her hearth to mark the days her girl had been gone. She had had to lift the shelf out of its brackets to carry on at the same height. Jon was on the road again. I had come to tell her I could get no answer, and found her on her knees giving the younger boy a haircut. Hair lay in two dark wings on his collar.

“They want a second child for the summer levy,” she said. “Twice the tax and the flour the same. I told Jon we can live on half. He was in the yard a long time hitching the mare after that, and when I went out she was all ready and he was just sitting on the mounting block. Edda. Have you ever seen my girl down there?”

“Only until she went round the corner that first day.”

She folded the scissors. She had barely begun the second side of the boy’s head. “And when I came for flour and you said she had her new stockings?”

“I gave the parcel to a woman who said she would take it.”

“You might have said that. I thought she put them on in front of you. I’ve been picturing her putting them on one at a time.”

She turned the boy round and went on cutting. There was such care in the way she folded his ear down under her thumb to keep it clear of the blades. I set a heel on the table. I had brought it in my pocket, already stale.

“More of them are going down tomorrow,” I told her. “I’ll go down with them and see her. I give you my word.”

“Don’t give me anything else today,” Sella said.

At the door she called after me. “Jon says that along the lake they call him the Hollowking. He wouldn’t say it in our house in front of the boy. He took me outside to tell me, as if his own kitchen wasn’t a safe place to talk anymore. Edda, sometimes you’re the one I’ve wanted to tell about this. I keep forgetting who you work for.”

I kept the bakery working that night. I went round to each girl’s bench and looked with her at the dough she’d set and where she meant to put it in the oven. My mother asked if I was going somewhere. I told her I’d be back by noon. Then I made her the soft loaf she liked and put it under a cloth for morning.

## Chapter Five · The lake overhead

The five children I had the next day all had a brother or sister below already. The youngest was a boy named Ari who had a fancy he’d be in the bunk above his sister. He kept wanting to know how high off the floor the beds were. He had lost an eye to a fever as a baby, and when somebody spoke he turned that whole earnest face to see. His mother had let me hold him while she took a piss on market days. She had touched my sleeve that morning. She was past asking.

At the corner where I usually turned back I went on in the shadow of the last child. The soldier had gone ahead to walk with the oldest. We passed the last lamp and kept going down. Before long I could hear the water finding its way through the roof. One drop fell at a long interval and rang like the edge of a chipped cup. Then there were so many falling at once that my wool was wet to the elbows. At home in the mountain we used to lower jugs into a shaft to keep the milk sweet. There was a smell on them when we hauled them up, and I smelled it there. I thought we must be under the lake. I could almost feel that weight of green water rolling pebbles over my head.

Something was making a noise a long way off. At first I took it for wind across the mouth of an air shaft. It would sink, then swell again, with a second, thinner sound coming in too late to join the first. Ari came up against my skirts. It was so dark that when I lifted my hand before my face I could see no more of it than if I’d left it upstairs in the dough.

“Go on, love,” I whispered. “I’m right here.”

“Is that somebody singing?”

We stopped to listen. Then there came a hitch in the sound, the catch of a breath taken between one cry and the next. There was no mistaking it after that. My arm went round his shoulders. Behind us was darkness, before us a little line of fire-colored light. The woman had gone through already with the older children. Ari turned his face up against my ribs. I could feel him wanting the answer.

“It’s a person,” I said. “Keep hold of me.”

Where the passage widened, the stone had been worn hollow by feet. We came out on a shelf overlooking another shelf, and beyond it another, with a yellow glow in the mouths of great chambers, one opening into the next until the air turned blue with smoke. All the way down the near wall stood a goblin company in the short gray cloaks the shepherds wear in the hills where I was born. I knew a man by his ears. I had danced with his wife at the same wedding where I met the miller. Beyond them ranks of orcs sat in their mail, passing a drinking cup from hand to hand. There were thousands below us. A quarter of my life I’d been putting bread into mouths I never saw. There they were with the crumbs still on their coats.

From where we stood I could see an orc woman darning a sock. She had turned it inside out over a wooden egg. Just past her there was a row of children with their shoes off. One was standing on the toes of his own bare foot, doing a little dance to stop the cold hurting. The ordinary smallness of that movement has stayed with me. I could have crossed the floor and put those shoes back on him.

In the next chamber the rock was black and wet. Bowls of fire stood in the hollows where I would have expected water to gather. In one of them a child’s shadow moved by itself. The little boy it belonged to was stretched out several paces away, held in a dream so cruel that he cried for his mother between every breath. When the shadow caught fire the light came through his thin shift. He woke begging for water. There was the sound I had heard walking under the lake. All round the chamber it was being answered by others. A goblin in a leather cap stood beside a girl, holding out her own mother’s letter. She struggled to sit up for it. By the time she got her hand on the paper her fingers had thickened and bent so far that she could hardly close them. He took the letter back unread. “Tomorrow, then,” he told her. He went on to the next bed. I saw the blankets that had been rolled up to take the blows of their heels. I saw where somebody had drawn a house on the wall beside one bed, complete with a chimney and a washing line. She had only been able to reach far enough to hang one shirt.

At the far end those who could stand were coming into the light. The heat had browned their skin in uneven patches. Some had the heavy brows and ridged jaws of the orcs who guarded them, grown all awry on frames that still had a good deal of child in them. One had to carry his head against his shoulder. A sergeant was fitting them with coats from a heap. The moment a coat would button, its wearer went into the outer cave to sit with the new companies. A small one, a girl I thought, was trying to get both hands to do what the buttons required. From her collar hung a smooth gray stone with one white stripe.

Ari tried to push past me. “That’s Disa. She’ll know where my sister went.”

I caught his sleeve. I had been looking at her hands because her face was so changed I could hardly bring myself to look there. When she heard us I saw those two tufts of hair rise where her eyebrows came together. She did know us. She looked past me at Ari. He rubbed his knuckles across his cheek, the sign the children made at home to mean they had been crying. She made it back, then pointed him away with all the authority she used to get him across a busy yard. The woman who had led us down took her by the shoulders and pointed her to a place in the company. Our flour mark was stitched over the heart of every coat.

The orc with the darning egg had moved her basket to make room for Disa. She buttoned the last two buttons for her. Then she took an army badge out of the basket and began to sew it on. I knew the black thread. It came up to the mill ten spools at a time on the cart with the salt. I had put it in my accounts under household goods. All those little jackets. I had stood mothers in my yard and told them to feel how good the wool was.

The older children I had brought were still by the entrance. I got my arm round Ari and drew him back into the passage. I thought of Sella holding her boy’s ear down to keep it safe from scissors. I would go to her. I would go into every house we supplied, even to the captain at the ford who had taken Vargeld’s sons. I could bring them down to see this. I could name the children. For the first time in months I knew exactly what I meant to say when somebody opened the door.

I turned and ran straight into Vargeld’s chest. I felt a little prick in my belly, no greater than the first touch of a pin when somebody hems an apron on you. He reached round and held me very close. His left eye watered a little in the smoke. It always did when he had flour in it. My hand actually went up to wipe it for him.

“Let the boy go by,” I said.

Ari’s sleeve slipped out of my fingers. I heard the scuff of his shoes on the rock behind me. Something warm was spreading under the waistband of my skirt. I looked down. The blade was buried deep in my belly. What I could see of it was the little crescent between the guard and his hand. I looked back into his face. Until that moment I had still expected him to be horrified with me at what had been done down here.

“Do you remember the first day, Edda? I couldn’t have got one mother to let go of a child. You walked through the door and they put their children in your hands.” His thumb moved on my wrist, over the place where he had fastened my sleeve so many years before. “I needed a woman they loved. You’ve done that work for me. I can carry on without you now.”

He took my weight when my knees went. His coat was soft with flour at the shoulder. I thought of trying to make him remember Dalla, of saying his sons’ names. I had spent so much of my life trying to get this man a little sleep. I wanted what breath I had left for the boy.

“Ari,” I said. “Up the stairs. Even in the dark.”

I heard a footstep. I never knew whose it was.

The light went out at the edges first. For a little while I could still see the seam in Vargeld’s cuff. Then there was a hearth before me and a woman on its low settle, easing her shoes off one at a time. A piece of bread stood beside her, a miller’s heel with the hollow in it. With every batch I had set one there for Frigg, the Hearth-Mother. When my son was little he used to ask how she could possibly find room to keep track of so many people’s children. I’d tell him she knew him by name and that was enough for his purposes. Her fingers went straight to the split in the crust where the heel came clean away. She could have found it blind.

“Will you take it from me today?” I asked.

“I have had my bread,” she said. “There are children who went down expecting you to follow. I have kept their names. I have also kept the hours they lay waiting for you. Those hours are owing, Edda. You will live them, every one. The children may give them up to me now.”

She set her hand beside mine. On her side of the hearth sat a little girl. She was very tired. There were two tufts of hair above the bridge of her nose. Frigg helped her to her feet and took from her a pair of stockings done up in paper. The girl went off barefoot into the warmth. The goddess put the parcel into my hands.

I was lying on a low bed, and the party of new children was coming down. Each time a lamp passed the crack in the doorway a ribbon of light ran across the wall. I tried to lift my head. I was eleven. I could smell Sella’s kitchen in the wool inside the parcel. Everything hurt. In a moment Edda would come through that door and help me put my stockings on. She had laughed about my big feet when she promised. I had been listening for her step for such a long time.

Another light went by. I had baked the next day and the next. I remembered every one of those mornings. Now I had the nights between them too. I drew my mother’s parcel to my chest and listened for the baker on the stairs.
