# A Name in Wool

## Chapter One · A Finger on the Wave

The little boy came up the lighthouse stairs whistling. There were eighty-three steps from the oil shed to the room where the weavers hung their cloth in memory of people lost at sea. By the sixtieth he had to give over. Liv, at work above him, anticipated a knock by about the time she could finish the eye of the gull she was working. He gave a last puff through his teeth outside the door. Then came a small silence in which his mother evidently did something to his hair.

People came there in all weathers. Once their sealskin coats began to steam you could tell if they'd come by boat or on a horse or from a house where somebody had started the day's catch smoking. The air in the lighthouse at Brimstead must have taken up the smell of every household along the Weeping Coast. Frigg kept the names of the dead. The women who looked after her tapestry in the lamproom, with the sea all about them and the light burning overhead, kept those of the drowned. Anybody could come in and run a hand along the cloth and find somebody they would once have gone down to the quay to meet.

Liv was thirty, with a blue mark where she had once pricked a finger deep with the point of a needle stained with woad. She was the woman people went to when a name had pulled loose. She believed herself very good at finding a man in the cloth before his family had quite finished giving her the name of his ship. She knew the mother before the woman had finished pushing a lock of the boy's hair back behind his ear.

“Ragna,” said Liv.

“You remember, then.”

“I remember your asking.”

“This time I've brought him. I think if we go along the wall together we can settle it. He's old enough now to keep his hands clean.”

The boy turned his palms up. Each had a great dint in the center from the nails of his other hand. They had been sailing for two days along the last navigable stretch of a sea that had carried people to the far southern ports when Liv was young. She put down her needle. During the previous summer a woman had come off the island packet and found her the only weaver on duty with an unfinished piece to hang by nightfall. It had been the first summer Ragna ever asked to see the work. Her son had come home from his aunt's with a drawing of the lighthouse. He wanted one of the wave his mother assumed Liv had worked the year his father drowned. For years Ragna had been able to tell him about the tower and what Frigg's women kept there. The drawing seemed a small thing to bring home for a boy who finally wanted to see for himself.

That day there had been somebody sitting in every patch of good light under the windows. One old sailor had brought a piece of pale hair twisted inside a purse his wife had made for him. He had sat in the same place three days running, hoping his fingers would work well enough to put in the last stitch himself. It was rude to offer a hand until somebody asked. Liv kept looking to see if he was ready while Ragna spoke.

“I have to ask a favor. My boy wants the name off the cloth if somebody could take the shape of it on a bit of paper. You had two called Ketil, and it's the one from Eldøy I want. I had the year embroidered on his christening shirt but I've left it at home like a damned fool.”

Liv had got up to fetch the roll, breaking her own thread and having to feel about inside her collar for the needle that had been there all morning. The old sailor had begun again to try to lift his wife's piece of hair. Liv walked down the row of names with her finger, reading them loudly. The ship's master had handed the list to a clerk the day he sailed from Ormsund, and she'd been brought up to regard records from that hand as good enough to bring to court. There was one Ketil.

“He got on at the island,” the woman said. “Listen. I was there.”

“The whole crew is here. I made up the account myself. I have fifteen people in the room waiting for me to come back to work. If you'll let the next lady in when you go down I can get her finished today, too.”

Ragna had got the end of her scarf caught in the latch. Liv had freed it impatiently from inside the door, eager to get back to the old man. In the evening she'd gone to bed pleased because she had helped one stubborn old fellow get an inch of his wife's hair safely put away. Only when she'd come across a little brass hook sticking up in the latch a week later had she remembered the noise of the scarf tearing and the widow going down those stairs without her drawing.

“Which was his boat?” Liv asked the boy. He'd had a share in this journey, too.

“The one Arni had. With two ropes for the bell because one was always breaking.”

They walked that length of the wall together. In a running band of waves on the gray linen backing, between a sea thick with names made in the year of the great easterly storm and a narrower blue worked in brighter times, there was the last voyage south of Arni the shipowner. His boat had sailed from Ormsund and stopped at Eldøy for water, taken on cargo and gone down in a blow off the chalk headlands. The pilot was the only man who got ashore alive. Fourteen names on the roll when the boat left Ormsund, thirteen names in the cloth. Liv had counted them as an apprentice seated by her grandmother. She took the boy's finger and put it on the letters.

The letters under his finger were big enough for a child to follow blind. Liv's grandmother had made them that size because in low winter light a black name on a blue wave was hard to see from several feet away. Sailors who had lost their sight soaking wet on an open deck sometimes came in with a little one to guide a hand up to the right place. A name learned that way went into the hand along with a father's height and the marks on the door at home that told you how much taller a child had to grow to catch up with him.

“Here. Ketil.”

Ragna went to the window for a better light and came back. “That's Blue Ketil. I know his mother's house mark. He stood at my wedding with one of my man's stockings on his head because there'd been more beer than was good for anybody. Sten, love, you can take your hand away. That's your father's friend.”

The boy took his hand away. Ragna faced Liv squarely now. It struck Liv then what it had cost Ragna to bring him. All the way up those steps she must have been afraid she was wrong, and that the woman from the lighthouse with the tidy shirt and the long count was going to take her finger as if she were a child herself and show her where she'd made her mistake.

“Was he on the roll at the first port?” Liv asked.

“I told you last summer. He boarded at Eldøy. His mittens went overboard pushing the bows off the piles. He took mine. There was a white darn across the palm. I could see it clear as day when he waved to the lad who took the stern rope. That's how sure I am. I don't know how to be any surer for you.”

“I'll go to the pilot.”

“I'd have gone myself if you'd given me his direction last time. There used to be money in that boat, and anybody coming about it now gets classed with creditors and people who write songs. They wouldn't tell me.”

She took a piece of bread from a pocket and broke it for the boy. A little of the crust fell along the hem of his coat. He went on looking quite openly at Liv.

“I know his whistle,” he said. “He used to do a little one outside and a long one in the passage. On the good days I'd get all the way into his boots before he could get into the kitchen.”

“Can you do it?”

“Yes. I don't do it for people who didn't hear him.”

“Fair enough,” Liv said. “Give me till the next time the island packet puts in. Would the two of you come up here again then?”

Ragna wiped the crumbs off her son's coat. “He gets seasick coming round the point. We'll stay at my sister's until you've been to see him. I've done coming up all these stairs alone.”

## Chapter Two · The Bird with Bad Feet

The year the roll came in, Liv's grandmother still had all her teeth. She used them to hold a cup by its rim so as to have both hands free for a length of wool. It was a filthy habit forbidden to all the apprentices. Liv loved to see her do it. When she got the cup fairly settled and picked up her needle she could carry on for quite a long time with her eyes alone, if you knew her. Up to tell you to do something about the oil smoking in the lamp. Left if somebody was listening who ought to have gone home by now. Both eyes closed if she was going to sneeze.

Liv was twenty-three that winter. She had come late to the tower. The woman who brought oil up the road in a two-wheeled cart had taken her on at eleven, an age when the two of them together could lean a cask over its rim to steer it through the narrow door. She knew by the weight in one end how high to lift the other. She could get the bung out with one hand clean, which mattered when you had to hold the cart horse afterward. Go to a horse smelling of spilled fish oil and you could get to see the whole of a very long road again on foot. She had thought bringing the light oil enough for any woman to do for Frigg. Then her grandmother began to go late to work because she could only see a dropped stitch in full sun.

“You need another pair of eyes,” Liv had said. “I'm down there twice in the week in any case. I can thread a needle at least while the mare gets her feed and you see where a bit of work has come undone.”

“I could use that,” the old woman had said after pretending quite a long time to be interested in a gull outside the window. “You'll wash twice when you come in. If a girl thinks she can leave her last job at the foot of the stair she should see how far a thumbprint of lamp oil can travel up a length of good white wool.”

It had come as a surprise how the job took hold of her. Her grandmother gave her a strip of the gray linen they used for backing. You could set a thumbnail against one of the threads and count twenty in the space from a finger to a knuckle. The first time Liv shaped the round belly of a little boat she pulled so tight she lifted a ridge through the cloth. The next evening in the tower she was looking for the same boat on her way up the stair. If she did it over and learned to let a little light in under the turns, she would see the rather fat boat with a child's arm hanging out of it that she had meant to make in the first place. It was the child's mother who had told her about that arm dangling over the side trying to catch a jellyfish the day her man took him out. You could work a whole summer to make a woman recognize something so small.

Seven years before Sten came whistling up the stairs, they had worked that night with the lamps lit at midday. The wreck was recent. The harbor got a great wash of wreckage whenever the tide came up through the bay of broken ships off Grátholm. Boots appeared in the wrack with the feet still in them. It was Liv's first bad winter on the coast. Her grandmother had sent her down to get a meal into the woman who sat with the men sorting whatever could be given back to the families. At the door on her way home Liv got a packet from a carter.

She laid the crew roll on the table and set beside it the scraps on which the carter had written down messages from people along the shore. Her grandmother put down her cup. They went through the names together. One pilot alive in the old store below the cliffs. The apothecary was with him. His wife had driven all the way down from the upper coast on a coal wagon, her own clothes still fastened with the straight pins she'd been using to fit a new coat. Thirteen men to enter in the cloth.

“Put the lamp on my other side,” the old woman said.

Liv moved it. The hand that went out in search of the next page went rather wide of its mark. “I can get the oil smoke off the glass if you give over a minute,” Liv said.

“I can't manage clean glass either, child.” Nobody had ever been allowed to mention this. “You take it. If you can read that little wretched close writing of Arni's you have the makings of a weaver.”

“I'll tell you what it says if you'd like to sew.”

They sat with their shoulders touching. She said a name. Her grandmother laid down a few letters and handed the needle to her for the tight work. In the packet there was a scrap of paper with a picture of a bird. The feet on the bird ran the whole length of the body and the head was barely big enough for its beak.

“That's Old Unni’s cockerel the day he got in the bath. Remember when I had to bring him out in the bread basket?” her grandmother said.

The cockerel had belonged to the tower's night watchman. Once a week she had the great privilege of a tub of warm water at the end of her shift. One spring morning a flutter of claws had sent her up out of her bath, leaving a wet bird with all its bones apparently on the outside taking in the room from her place in the tub. There was something of that same ruined dignity in the bird on the paper. Liv brought the scrap so close that the corner of it touched the old woman's nose, and for a moment they could both see the very same thing. They laughed as quietly as they could because of the people downstairs. The old woman turned it over to keep the week's oil reckoning on the back. Letters, initials, names of witnesses, you put all those by. Children's things filled up a working table. That winter it had seemed a happy thing that one could still laugh upon the stairs or in the little pool of lamplight where two heads bent to the same long labor. Her grandmother had been dead and buried on the grassy side of the cliff for six years when Liv went to see the pilot. Till that walk it had been a good evening in her memory, down to the sound of the spoon touching the empty cup.

## Chapter Three · A Man Who Could Draw

The pilot lived at the end of the one road still open past the fishing station. He had a triangular thumb, the tip sliced off on one side so he could hook a little shaving of wood into the rim of a bucket where a whole thumb would hardly fit. His wife sat on a stone with her boots in the sun while he worked. There had to be something wrong with the bucket. It had at least ten little shavings sticking in the same place.

There used to be cod enough on that road to pay a person to come down with a horse and cart. In Skerpla, the month when the farmers sharpened their tools, people had once made quite a day of coming to the fishing station to see which sheds would be putting out new fish this summer. Young men who wanted wives had been known to pick a cart on the strength of whose sister had charge of the money on it. Now the drying frames stood bare on either side of the path with old bits of line hanging from them. One sailor's widow had pegged three small dresses to a frame. They were the only color in the whole long stretch leading up to the pilot's door. Liv saw that the woman with her boots in the sun had put a hand up over her eyes to pick out a visitor from a distance, much as her grandmother used to. For the first time since she set out she thought to consider whether these two people might be having a perfectly satisfactory day that would be the worse for her arriving.

“Are they expecting to see you?” a girl at one of the sheds had asked on the way past.

“No.”

The girl had thought about this. “There's a dog. He makes such a fuss if a person knocks that Mundi comes running and falls over him. If you come up under the pear tree, Helle will see you time enough to get hold of the dog.”

Helle was the woman on the stone. The dog, an old white-faced brute about the size of a child's stool, had seen her well before either of the people and was standing expectantly by the pear tree. She let him smell both hands. Then she explained to the woman who she was. The woman reached a toe out and poked the old man's leg.

“Mundi. Lady to see you. If it's about the love affair you had in the south she can see you've come down in the world.”

“He's deaf?” Liv asked.

“He hears me. Sit over there where he can see your mouth.”

Helle drew one finger down the left side of her own jaw. “Takes him a little longer on one side than the other to say a word out loud. You give him the time for it, will you? You and I are both of us used to earning our living at a good rate of talk. I can see you're in a hurry from here.”

“Have we met before?”

“You brought the last cask of oil to the winter station the day the bay froze over for good. We were the first house that didn't get its share. Long time I wondered why you hadn't gone on one more delivery, till I saw somebody get a lamp going in the tower at the far end of all that dark water. Then I knew where the load had gone.”

“The light keeper sent a boy for me. I didn't know there was anybody beyond the sheds who still wanted it. I'd have come back in the morning. I had a good horse and she'd go wherever I led her.”

Helle gave her a thoughtful look. “You see how long people can go on thinking about the things someone did without either one ever having talked to the other. He's got a good pair of eyes, our Mundi. Go on and use your face where he can see it.”

She sat. Mundi set a hand on the rim of the bucket.

“There's a woman from Eldøy whose husband went down with Arni. She went all the way up the steps at Brimstead with her little boy, and I couldn't show him where his father's name was. The only Ketil we've got there is the man called Blue.”

“There were two,” Mundi said.

She had been preparing for a long afternoon of going to the well with the bucket and trying to find the right moment at which to ask an injured old man to reopen his life for her. Hearing that answer almost immediately was like losing her footing. She sat back hard against the wall.

“Did you know the other one?”

“Sailed with him twice. He came aboard at the island, after the clerk finished his roll and went home. Cost more to carry a man who needed to get down the coast than there was in his purse, so he worked his passage. We'd have paid him in the south for the rest of the trip. He never got there.”

“Was he called something else in the boat?”

“Linnet. He had a chipped tooth, made the damnedest noise with it. We had men from Strandvik aboard who put great stock in keeping the wind sweet with the Sea-King. They wouldn't have a man whistle in the rigging. Pretty soon even those fellows could tell who'd got up to take the watch without turning over in their coats.”

“I hadn't thought to ask her which way he went to sea. A man boards at Eldøy, you'd think I might have asked did he have people there, did somebody see him go. Instead I read a name as though there could only be so many Ketils to a ship. If you hadn't known him by it, what good would the name have been to you?”

“He wouldn't have answered. You could have called along the deck for a Ketil all the way round the headland and the great blue-headed fellow would have answered you every time. Ketil from the island had got so used to that he quite forgot that one of the names was his. He'd sit there on the deck eating whatever fell off the cook's spoon and tell you all about a wooden cradle that had come down from his mother's mother. It had an egg-shaped hole in the back because when she was a child she had wanted to see out. He was always looking for the same sort of yellow wood to patch that hole. Took him two trips to realize he'd been making a fine speech to the fellow who had half a plank of it at home in his shed.”

“Did he get it mended?”

“I got him the board. He came up on the last morning with such a face on him I asked him who'd died. His little lad had got in the habit of standing up when he wanted to see out. The wife said the hole was a help to getting him to lie down and give everybody a night's rest. He said every time he'd been away his son had got up to something else while he was gone. I have children. I know what it's like to come home thinking you've brought just the thing they need.”

“What else was he like?” she said. This question had been available to her when Ragna first came to the lighthouse. She had sent the woman home before it ever occurred to her to ask it.

“Couldn't cut straight cheese. Could repair a rush seat so it kept you up off the floor another year. Liked picking the burnt skin off rice that came up in barrels from the south. The skipper used to save it for him. He was good company in the morning. My wife can tell you what I think of most people's company in the morning.”

Mundi set the bucket down and undid the high collar of his shirt. His lower jaw on the left was altogether smooth, the bone a little hollowed away under the skin. His teeth on that side were gone. “They kept my mouth tied shut while it set. My wife sat up a good many nights feeding me with a tube made out of a quill. I got a piece of paper from her. At first I could barely keep the charcoal between two fingers. The other men had their names on the list. I couldn't come up with Linnet's other name to save my own skin. I thought somebody who knew him would likely know what I meant.”

Liv had a clear view down the road to the sea. The tips of waves broke white against the landfast ice. A man came out of his house with a pail of something to throw on a garden too cold to grow food. Everything went on for some moments precisely as if she hadn't just found out what her hands had done.

“How big were the feet you gave it?” she said.

“Oh, terrible. Only bird I could aim at was the one stitched above the ankle of my wife’s stocking, and if that thing could fly I'll eat the heel.” He was looking with interest at her face, awaiting a recollection he thought would please them both. “Have you still got it?”

“We had it. I showed it to my grandmother. She thought a child had drawn it and she laughed. So did I. She used the back for accounts. Years later, when we ran out of kindling, I burned those accounts in the stove.”

His wife had got her boots back on. She ran her palm along one side, feeling for a place where the seam gaped. Liv waited for one of them to say it was an understandable mistake. The pilot kept looking at the bucket. It seemed he had some defect at last that he couldn't quite get his hands on.

“When I was sitting down there getting some skin back over the hole in my face,” he said, “the one thing I was good for was thinking who I had lost off the boat. Fourteen lost and one ashore. I tried to keep all fourteen straight by putting them where they slept. Only one whose sleeping place I ever forget was Linnet, because the man had a gift for finding somebody crossing to the cold side of a ship and giving him the warm bit he'd just got up from. After I could go outdoors again my wife took me to see that cloth. There was the whole of a ship's company from the door to the window. I saw the blue in it, saw the waves, saw no earthly use in making thirteen families stand up and say to me which one I ought to have saved. I sent Helle in to give them our thank-you and went home.”

“I thought I remembered everybody who came that year,” said Liv.

Helle was still attending to the seam of her boot. “I took an oat cake. A woman got up when I came in and said I'd been given my man back and she wished she'd been given hers. There wasn't a thing to say to that. I put the cake down and got him out before she saw him. A picture might have meant a good deal more to us that winter than it did to a roomful of people who knew nothing about it. But we saw the ship was there. It seemed reasonable to suppose somebody had done the counting.”

Liv had pictured them on this coast within sight of the tower every clear day of those seven years. Helle told her they had gone up the coast to her father's place as soon as Mundi could sit the cart. It was hardly a year since they'd come back to the old house under the pear. Liv thought of the long, firm word she had given Ragna last summer while this man was within a morning's walk of her door.

“I would have brought it myself,” he said, “if I'd known it would take anybody this long to ask.”

“His wife did ask. She came last summer.”

“Then what waited until now?”

“I did. She tried to tell me where he came aboard. I'd the whole ship's company counted up and a roomful of people waiting. I told her I'd finished.” Saying the words to this stranger made it painfully easy to remember her hand already on the door and the woman still getting up from the bench. “I need to tell her that.”

“Yes,” said the old man, and that was all she was going to get.

His wife had gone indoors. She came back with a good square of clean paper and an end of blue chalk. “Give our Ketil some proper feet this time,” she said. “You'll want to put your name on it if you're going to send it.”

“Would you come up and give it to the boy yourself?” Liv asked.

Mundi rubbed the tips of his fingers together. “Do they still haul the oil casks up on a rope? Might they take a pilot up in a sling?”

“I'll get you a chair carried up the tower if you need it. His father will be in your debt.”

“Let his father alone. If the boy wants to hear some things to put his old man in a bad light I suppose I could come along on Laugardagr. Washing day. Helle gets me clean and presents me to the world on my best day in the week.” He picked up the blue chalk. It was plain to her he would sooner have stood naked in the market than tell her how many nights he'd lain awake trying to remember a shipmate's second name.

## Chapter Four · The Other Ketil

Ragna's sister kept a place down by the lower light where fresh milk used to be sold in the days when there were cows enough. Around the door somebody had planted bulbs well before the long winter came. Nobody had disturbed them yet in case it was the weather this year that had kept them so long. Liv stood upon the step with the old man's paper in her pocket. She had stopped on the hill and set aside in Frigg's name a little of her midday bread. She heard the ordinary sound of surf and a man thirty yards away calling on his dog to leave a rotten skate alone.

It was Ragna who came out. She took one look and shut the door behind her. “Has the boy got to hear this?”

“I wanted to say it to you first. I found the pilot. He drew a picture for us at the time because he couldn't speak. There was a bird in your husband's nickname. We missed him, Ragna. I turned you out, last summer. You'd come as far as you could come to have somebody listen to you. I got up and opened the door while you were still speaking. I've wished all the walk here that I could take back having done that to you. I'm sorry.”

Ragna looked past Liv toward the harbor. A woman washing somebody's best dishes had stacked the blue plates on the inside windowsill to let the light play through them. It was a small street like any other. Liv had not wanted an audience for what she had come here to say. At a window upstairs the boy was carefully closing the catch with his mother in plain view below. He had understood.

“They were people who knew me all my life,” Ragna said. “And when I went home the women wanted to know if I had found him, and I said you'd counted them all. Afterwards one of them asked my boy if he was sure his father had gone where his mother thought he went. He asked me about it when I was helping him into bed. He was old enough to think I might have told a lie. He was little enough to cry because he thought I'd tell another to make him feel better.”

“Do you want me to come to the island and tell them whose mistake it was?”

“Yes. Ask them yourself what they remember of him. There'll be some big stories by the time you get there, and all they ever had to do was mend a net beside him. It'll be good for me to hear a few.”

Liv gave her the drawing. The new feet were a little smaller in proportion to the bird. To make up for that, Mundi had given it buttocks like a prosperous chandler's. Ragna took one look and laughed. For all her gratitude to hear it, Liv was ashamed of the ease with which her shoulders went down at the sound.

“Don't you take that as settling everything,” Ragna said. “You can still come to the island.”

“I will. There's somebody else we ought to see before I get the loom set out.”

“Dalla at the oil shed?” Ragna asked.

Liv turned back from the step. “Yes. I meant to ask her for a couple of good lamps. If I stretch the cloth on a frame I can open the wave and add some linen. Why, did you want to see her about something too?”

“She ought to have the chance to say what she wants. Her father was at my table almost as often as my husband. First time Sten tried to shave, he used a spoon and a dipper of the good cream, and it was Blue he sat imitating. He'll have people who remember things he did to them, same as you've come to ask me about mine.”

Dalla was Blue Ketil’s daughter. She kept the key to the oil shed where Liv had rubbed clean her hands before going up to the tower. She had a long new apron folded over her knees, and was putting a patch on it in the place where one end of a cask would come up against her hip. Ragna had walked her to school as a very small girl. Now she had the most alarming way of looking at a person between her father's fair eyebrows, grown into a face mostly her mother's. The space above the names in her father's wave had been worked twice over already. Liv explained that she could lift the whole piece out and open it up to make room for another name. The work would take a month, possibly six weeks as the light went.

“And where would you put him while his piece was out?” the girl asked.

“On a frame in the workroom.”

“My little brother comes by on his way back from feeding the hens. Could you leave the workroom unlocked?”

Liv sat down on an oil case. She could think of any number of places to put iron under a window or a bar outside a door. In the end it was Sten, who had come down without anybody noticing, who said, “Isn't there room in the next wave?”

Dalla went to get the chalk from behind the door and knelt with it on the floor where a spatter of winter oil had gone black with age. She drew out the wave from memory. There was the bird whose wing her brother always felt for before he began to count the letters and there was the piece where the sea dipped so deep they could work a whole boat into the trough. She showed Liv exactly where her little brother put his hand on his way round the tower. He always found his father from the bird, Dalla said. Sometimes he only had time to get in and out before the hens began to go up to roost, and he could stand with their father a minute even on those short evenings.

“Could you get from one of those men to the other in one long step?” Dalla asked. She put the chalk away and rubbed the white mark off her knee. “When the two of them were home you could find them anywhere at the distance one man had to reach to borrow something off the other. I think it would look right to me to see them still at it. We'll go up and see how far it is to the next wave.”

“Yes,” Liv said, looking again at the dip in the wave. “We could put them well within reach of each other.”

“Blue could stay where his little boy can find him, then,” Sten said. “My da wouldn't have gone on the whole voyage without walking up and down a bit.”

On their way back he let Liv hear his father's whistle. There was a gap where a note ought to have been. To a stranger it was the noise a kettle made with the lid badly on. Heard once, there could be no mistaking it. His mother came round the side of the house at the sound, looking for a moment as young as the girl who had walked Blue Ketil's daughter to school.

## Chapter Five · In the Next Wave

It was two days by boat to Eldøy. Ragna's son managed the first day very well on a heel of dry bread and the second very poorly with a bowl of milk that he insisted he was old enough to keep down. They got him ashore at the island and sat on the gritty little strand in bright sunshine while he recovered some sense of what it was to have the earth under his feet. Before they left Brimstead, Ragna had sent a folded note to Mundi’s wife. They had all agreed on the washing day a fortnight hence. It would give Liv time to make the letters, and the boy time to forgive his mother for making him undergo the crossing again.

Somebody recognized Ragna and fetched a cart. Two children leaned over the footboard to see what they had been sent to the beach to collect. The older held the reins rolled up at the ends to fit into her small hands. All the older girl said to Liv was that the basket could go in the back if the eggs were out first. The younger one brought Sten a piece of dried pear which stayed down. In half an hour they had seen a woman doctoring a hen's leg in the front yard, two men taking a hide off a fence and three children sitting on the curb of the well while their mother scrubbed the bucket. Liv had spent the better part of the voyage preparing a speech in which she stood up very straight in front of the whole of Eldøy. When the carter's younger child told her the woman tending the hen had been on the beach with Ragna the morning Ketil last cast off, she had to get out and start talking there in the mud.

While supper was cooking, Ragna opened a chest for her. “This was his. Mundi ought to see it sometime.” It was a little old cradle fitted together with pegs that had gone very smooth and long dark handles at either end for carrying it out into the sunshine or up to a fire in the cold. Liv crouched beside it. There was a space in the far side exactly the shape Mundi had drawn in the air with his hand. Through it she could see Sten's indignant face. He could remember the cradle perfectly well and was at some pains to show them he had never snored.

Ragna sent him out with the girl from the cart to get the eggs in before supper. A neighbor named Svanhild had come over with the spoon she'd used to take the cream off the milk for Sten's christening cake. Liv had heard Ragna greet her as one of the women who used to wait on the beach. When the children were well into the hen yard, Liv took the bowls to the scullery with her.

“Was it you who asked Sten if his mother might have told him a story about his father?” she asked.

“It was my Olaf came up and asked me if Sten's father had left them. So I asked the child. The little man oughtn't to have heard it from somebody who could have crossed a street and asked his mother.”

Liv told her about the roll of names and the old pilot trying to draw a bird with fingers that would hardly hold a stick of charcoal. She told her exactly what she'd said when Ragna first came to see her. One of the children started to cry, wanting to go in and show Sten an animal she had drawn. They took the talk down to the boat shed, where there had always been room for somebody to tell a story after supper. Behind the shed the tide ran deep and fast past the wooden piles. She would have known it had come to the turn by the cold creeping through the seat of her dress and the soft sucking sound under the boards. It was the same water that made such a noise at the foot of the lighthouse stairs.

A great many people came to the shed. Some had a piece of work in their hands and a great many came for the simple pleasure of seeing how much the woman from the tower would mind admitting she had made a fool of herself. Liv minded quite a lot. She was glad to have a bit of smooth spare wood off the side of the shed in her hand while she spoke. A woman told her how the end had come off Ketil's mitten the winter he had been courting Ragna and he had gone to his own wedding with four of his bride's pink stitches on his thumb. Liv asked if anyone else wanted to tell Ragna something about the man. A boy holding his sleeping baby sister on one hip said his father still couldn't get clean the place on his deck where Ketil had once spilled an entire jar of pickled purple cabbage. Sten, who had followed them down with his mother, pleaded to be allowed to go straight round with a lantern and look at it. It was late at night before they finally got the children into their beds. Ragna took the bit of wood out of Liv's hand. There was a deep white patch across her palm where the grain had been pressed against it.

“Anybody else you want to see while we're here?” Ragna asked.

“You tell me. I've the feeling there were things you ought to have been able to say to them and somehow I've come and filled up a whole boat shed myself.”

“I got to sit on a bench with my feet up and listen to people who stopped asking me over to eat because they thought there must be nothing pleasant to say to me at table. When I go home for good I expect I'll be eating with one or two of them again. I asked you to come and as far as the bit between us goes you've been.” She took Liv to get the blanket out of her own chest and showed her which side of the attic dripped on a pillow if the wind got up. “Leave a place for the boy's red thread. That's all I want from the tower now.”

Back in Brimstead, Sten brought the last bit of red wool undone from his father's cap. He had got it free himself although Ragna had told him time and again he could cut it. The end curled up tight, still remembering the turns it had taken round a needle. Liv soaked it in a spare teacup to see what the dye would do. It bled a disreputable pink.

“I won't be mad if it runs,” Sten said.

“Come here and turn the cloth over. See all the things sewn on where nobody can see them? My grandmother put a lock of my grandfather's hair in behind his name. Somebody put a piece of a fishing fly behind a pilot's. Here's a shirt button with a green thread still on it. Do you think we could find a bit of space for your red wool?”

“Will you be able to feel it from the front?”

They tried it first against the inside of his cuff. He held his wrist up to the window while Liv closed a little fold of linen around the red strand, and he rubbed his thumb over it with his eyes shut so looking couldn't help him.

“Would anybody be able to feel it?” he asked.

“Anybody as knows it's in there. Would you like me to show a person where it is, if they come up to find your father?”

“Could you ask me first?” he said, and at once his ears went red. “I won't be here. I mean Mam could tell you which people we'd like to know.”

“Then I will ask her.” When Sten was looking through the waves with his mother for the best place to put the name, Liv went to the back of the frame. Her grandmother's bit of hair was there. It had gone a faded honey color. The little square of cloth she stitched over it had one long side that she must have had to finish when she could scarcely see her work at all. Liv laid two fingers on the stitches. Beyond the window a big swell came and went against the base of the cliffs. She stayed for the whole sound of it, as she had stayed beside her grandmother in the evenings when they needed the light of one lamp between them. Then she went to fetch the new red wool.

Inside a square of linen the piece of cap made a little raised tick under the letters Sten and Ragna had chosen, *Ketil of Eldøy*. Liv had worked them in good madder red that had been washed until the water came away clear. Sten could feel it. Ragna put the flat of her hand on the letters and closed her eyes. The boy drew the last thread tight. After some consultation he had the little bird sewn beside the name, feet and all.

It took four men, a house door and a length of good rope to get Mundi all the way up to a room plenty of people in their prime came into panting. Halfway up they heard the alarmed noises he made on the first turn, where the slit in the wall gave a man a good long view of the surf at the bottom of the cliffs. They heard him laugh after that. Ragna went down to spell one of the carriers. Liv kept a hand on the open door. She thought of her grandmother's cup, and the little patch of warm lamplight, and how willingly she would have sat late again and done all the woman's close work for her with her own good eyes.

Sten stood in the doorway and gave the little whistle his father used to give when he came home. The old man, half lifted and half walking on his wife's arm, stopped and answered him. The gap in the tune fell in exactly the same place.

“What's it mean?” the boy asked when Mundi had got onto a bench and been allowed one swallow of beer.

“At your house it might have meant he was home for his tea. The first time I ever heard it we were a long way south and there was a pig in the captain's bed.”

“Was he a big pig?”

“Fine spotted gilt. Lady pig. She'd come aboard to be delivered to somebody's brother. We'd lost two days' sailing to weather and there was a letter for the brother to say the pig was due to have a family. A wicker crate is drafty when you're little and new in the world. Your da looked into the captain's berth while the captain was at supper and he said to me, 'Mundi, look at that good clean straw ticking, with a fella away eating off silver when he could be laying in it.' Then he whistled the way you did just now. The pig got up and followed him in like a fellow officer.”

“Did he get in trouble?”

“Well, the captain came down about an hour later. He lifted a corner of the blanket. There she lay with five beautiful little spotted pigs laid out beside her and your da sitting on the edge of the bed pulling his stockings back on.”

“Why'd he take them off?”

“He'd put one runt in each stocking while the old sow was getting settled. So he tells the captain the total is seven. He holds the two little runts up, a head in each hand, quite square with the captain's face. And the skipper says, 'Linnet, heaven knows I've had occasion to put many things down against your wages, but how in all the green world am I to charge a pig for a berth?'”

“What did Da say?”

“Your father reached in his pocket and came up with a worn silver bit. He put it on the pillow. 'One fare for the mother,' he told him. 'The little ones is mine. Mine go free.'”
