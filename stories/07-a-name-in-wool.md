# A Name in Wool

## Chapter One · Red Thread

The cupboard in the lighthouse at Brimstead had a drawer for every shade of sea a weaver could think of and one drawer marked red. Liv kept her lunch in the red drawer. Her grandmother had kept a comb there. The old woman used to say anybody who put clean red wool in with the blue deserved the purple sea he got.

The tapestry ran round the wall above their heads. Into it the weavers worked the names of drowned sailors, in honor of Frigg who remembered them. Liv had spent her apprenticeship unpicking mistakes. She knew what it cost to put the wrong name into a well-made sea.

The trouble began when an island woman came in with a little boy and asked to see where his father’s name was.

“Ketil,” the woman said. “On the south run. He sailed with Arni.”

Liv took them along the wall. There he was in the curls of a breaking wave. The little boy put his finger on the letters. His mother frowned.

“But that’s Blue Ketil,” she said. “I knew him. He came to our wedding.”

Liv went to the drawer for the ship’s roll. Fourteen names. The pilot had come back, and thirteen names ran along the wave. As a girl she had counted them herself.

“My husband went aboard at Eldøy,” the woman said. “He borrowed my mittens because he lost his over the side bringing the boat in. I had to walk home with my hands in my armpits. You may depend on it I remember which boat.”

“Would the others have known him by another name?”

“Blue’s sister wondered that. She says you would have made sure.”

“It must be my mistake,” Liv said. The boy withdrew his finger. She had been on her first watch the winter the list was made, fetching tea for a grandmother who still did everything right as far as the town knew. Everybody in Brimstead had somebody up on that wall. Imagine them beginning to wonder whose name they were visiting. Liv locked up the roll, went home for walking shoes and told the other weavers she would miss the morning lighting. The pilot lived halfway up the Weeping Coast.

## Chapter Two · The Pilot’s Teeth

The pilot had lost several teeth and most of his hearing. Liv found him outside his house, fitting a new handle to a bucket. Three times she explained her errand. The third time he walked off with the bucket. She followed him to the well.

“Draw,” he said.

She did. When the bucket came up it held a little less than half of what had gone in. Water ran down the wood and soaked her boots.

“Needs a hoop,” Liv said.

“Good. Then you can see a thing in front of your face. That’s a start.” He sat on the low coping. “The last one who came had his whole story in his pocket before he got here. Wanted me to tell him the captain was frightened.”

“Was he?”

“Of course he was. But he did his work.”

She put her notebook down by the bucket. For some time they worked the loose hoop back into place. When the bucket held, she handed the pilot his hammer and said, “One of the families is missing a name.”

The pilot looked up at her. “Did he have a boy?”

“Yes.”

“We called him Linnet. He had a way of whistling round a chipped tooth. Used to do it when he hauled. Drove Blue Ketil half out of his mind. One time Blue got a bucket over his head for saying something about it.”

“Fourteen names on the roll,” she said.

“Aye. Made at Ormsund. We took him aboard at the island. He wanted the fare, and we could use a hand with the sail. We’d have put him on the roll when we paid him off.”

She could almost feel the flat table under her elbows at the lighthouse, the rattle of a spoon when she set her grandmother’s cup down. There had been a scrap in the packet with a linnet drawn on it. She remembered the bird because it had legs as thick as a hen’s. When she asked what it was doing there her grandmother said one of the children must have drawn it and turned it over to reckon the lamp oil on the back.

“Did you draw him a bird?”

The old man looked up at the hill above the well. “I had a broken jaw when I came in from that voyage. My wife had to feed me by the spoon. All the others I could find by name on the paper. What else could I have put for him?”

“It got there,” Liv said. It cost her something to look at him. “You sent it. We had it in our hands and we used it for scrap.”

He struck the handle into the bucket once more, though it already sat tight. “Have you still got it?”

“I threw the old oil accounts into the stove myself.”

He took the pencil from her open notebook. The second bird had the same ridiculous legs. This time he put his initials underneath.

The pilot whistled three notes, the last one high and a little long, to carry a man through the hard pull when the net came clear of the water. Through his poor old teeth it was clear and sweet. Liv thought of the woman at the tower, how she had stood beside a strange man’s name and let her boy touch it before she said a word.

“Would you come down and tell her boy how you drew that?”

“If you send for me in a cart. I’m damned if I walk it.”

## Chapter Three · The Empty Space

The boy came alone to see the weaving. He brought a length of red wool and explained that it had come out of his father’s old cap. His mother said red bled in wet weather and he was to ask first.

“Red does bleed,” Liv said. “We could wash a little and see.”

They made two cups of tea while the strand soaked in a third cup. A pink cloud came off it. The boy turned the cup around so that Liv wouldn’t see. She had been an apprentice herself. She turned it back.

“My grandmother and I missed your father,” she told him. “We had a picture somebody drew of him. We didn’t know what it meant and we never asked. I thought I ought to take the old part out and do it over. What do you think?”

He studied the tapestry. “Would you have to take Blue Ketil out?”

“For a while.”

“His little girl comes here too.” He pointed to a space in the next wave. “There’s room there.”

Liv dried the old red wool and folded it inside a square of unbleached linen. She sewed the linen to the back of the tapestry behind the place the boy had chosen. In front she worked his father’s name in colorfast thread. She left one end loose and showed him how to draw it snug with a shuttle.

At the door they found his mother helping an old man down from a cart. The pilot had worn his good coat. He looked at Liv as though he might still turn round and ask to be taken home. She put the square of paper with the linnet on it into his hand, and the boy leaned over to look.

“Did my father have feet like that?”

“You had better come inside where your mother can’t hear what I’m going to tell you about his feet.”

The boy took him by the sleeve. “He’s in the next wave,” he said, and went ahead to show the way. As he worked the latch he whistled through his teeth, an impatient little run of notes. The pilot caught Liv’s eye. They both knew the tune.
