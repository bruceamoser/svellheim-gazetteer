# A Name in Wool

## Chapter One · A Finger on the Wave

The little boy came up the lighthouse stairs whistling. There were eighty-three steps from the oil shed to the room where the tapestry hung. By the sixtieth he had to give over. Liv, at work above him, anticipated a knock by about the time she could finish the eye of the gull she was working. He gave a last puff through his teeth outside the door. Then came a small silence in which his mother evidently did something to his hair.

People came there in all weathers. Once their sealskin coats began to steam you could tell if they'd come by boat or on a horse or from a house where somebody had started the day's catch smoking. The air in the lighthouse at Brimstead must have taken up the smell of every household along the Weeping Coast. Frigg kept the names of the dead. The women who looked after her tapestry in the lamproom, with the sea all about them and the light burning overhead, kept those of the drowned. Anybody could come in and run a hand along the cloth and find somebody they would once have gone down to the quay to meet.

Liv was thirty, with a blue mark where she sometimes pushed her needle into her sleeve and missed. She was the woman people went to when a name had pulled loose. She believed herself very good at finding a man in the cloth before his family had quite finished giving her the name of his ship. She knew the mother before the woman had finished pushing a lock of the boy's hair back behind his ear.

“Ragna,” said Liv.

“You remember, then.”

“I remember your asking.”

“This time I've brought him. I think if we go along the wall together we can settle it. He's old enough now to keep his hands clean.”

The boy turned his palms up. Each had a great dint in the center from the nails of his other hand. They had been sailing for two days along the last navigable stretch of a sea that had carried people to the far southern ports when Liv was young. She put down her needle. During the previous summer a woman had come off the island packet and caught her alone with an unfinished piece to hang by nightfall. Liv remembered the impatience with which she'd got up to fetch the roll for her. She had counted the names twice, loudly enough for Ragna to hear her from the door.

“Which was his boat?” Liv asked the boy. He'd had a share in this journey, too.

“The one Arni had. With two ropes for the bell because one was always breaking.”

They walked that length of the wall together. In a running band of waves on the gray linen backing, between a sea thick with names made in the year of the great easterly storm and a narrower blue worked in brighter times, there was the last voyage south of Arni the shipowner. His boat had sailed from Ormsund and stopped at Eldøy for water, taken on cargo and gone down in a blow off the chalk headlands. The pilot was the only man who got ashore alive. Fourteen names on the roll when the boat left Ormsund, thirteen names in the cloth. Liv had counted them as an apprentice seated by her grandmother. She took the boy's finger and put it on the letters.

“Here. Ketil.”

Ragna went to the window for a better light and came back. “That's Blue Ketil. I know his mother's house mark. He stood at my wedding with one of my man's stockings on his head because there'd been more beer than was good for anybody. Sten, love, you can take your hand away. That's your father's friend.”

The boy took his hand away. Ragna faced Liv squarely now. She had depended on being right about that wave. All the walk up those steps she must have been afraid she was wrong, and that the woman from the lighthouse with the tidy shirt and the long count was going to take her finger as if she were a child herself and show her where she'd made her mistake.

“Was he on the roll at the first port?” Liv asked.

“I told you last summer. He boarded at Eldøy. His mittens went overboard pushing the bows off the piles. He took mine. When I close my eyes I can see the darn across his palm waving to the lad who took the stern rope. That's how sure I am. I don't know how to be any surer for you.”

“I'll go to the pilot.”

“I'd have gone myself if you'd given me his direction last time. There used to be money in that boat, and anybody coming about it now gets classed with creditors and people who write songs. They wouldn't tell me.”

She took a piece of bread from a pocket and broke it for the boy. A little of the crust fell along the hem of his coat. He went on looking quite openly at Liv.

“I know his whistle,” he said. “He used to do a little one outside and a long one in the passage. On the good days I'd get all the way into his boots before he could get into the kitchen.”

“Can you do it?”

“Yes. I don't do it for people who didn't hear him.”

“Fair enough,” Liv said. “Give me till the next time the island packet puts in. Would the two of you come up here again then?”

Ragna wiped the crumbs off her son's coat. “He gets seasick coming round the point. We'll stay at my sister's until you've been to see him. I've done coming up all these stairs alone.”

## Chapter Two · The Bird with Bad Feet

The year the roll came in, Liv's grandmother still had all her teeth. She used them to hold a cup by its rim so as to have both hands free for a length of wool. It was a filthy habit forbidden to all the apprentices. Liv loved to see her do it. When she got the cup fairly settled and picked up her needle she could carry on for quite a long time with her eyes alone, if you knew her. Up to tell you to do something about the oil smoking in the lamp. Left if somebody was listening who ought to have gone home by now. Both eyes closed if she was going to sneeze.

They had worked that night with the lamps lit at midday. The wreck was recent. The harbor got a great wash of wreckage whenever the tide came up through the bay of broken ships off Grátholm. Boots appeared in the wrack with the feet still in them. It was Liv's first bad winter on the coast. Her grandmother had sent her down to get a meal into the woman who sat with the men sorting whatever could be given back to the families. At the door on her way home Liv got a packet from a carter.

The roll and the few notes people had sent by word of mouth she laid on the table. Her grandmother put down her cup. They went through the names together. One pilot alive in the old store below the cliffs. The apothecary was with him. His wife had driven all the way down from the upper coast on a coal wagon, her own clothes still fastened with the straight pins she'd been using to fit a new coat. Thirteen men to enter in the cloth.

“Put the lamp on my other side,” the old woman said.

Liv moved it. The hand that went out in search of the next page went rather wide of its mark. “I can get the oil smoke off the glass if you give over a minute,” Liv said.

“I can't manage clean glass either, child.” Nobody had ever been allowed to mention this. “You take it. If you can read that little wretched close writing of Arni's you have the makings of a weaver.”

“I'll tell you what it says if you'd like to sew.”

They sat with their shoulders touching. She said a name. Her grandmother laid down a few letters and handed the needle to her for the tight work. In the packet there was a scrap of paper with a picture of a bird. The feet on the bird ran the whole length of the body and the head was barely big enough for its beak.

“Somebody drew our reeve in the bath,” her grandmother said.

They laughed as quietly as they could because of the people downstairs. The old woman turned it over to keep the week's oil reckoning on the back. Letters, initials, names of witnesses, you put all those by. Children's things filled up a working table. That winter it had seemed a happy thing that one could still laugh upon the stairs or in the little square circle of lamplight where two heads bent to the same long labor. Her grandmother had been dead and buried on the grassy side of the cliff for six years when Liv went to see the pilot. Till that walk it had been a good evening in her memory, down to the sound of the spoon touching the empty cup.

## Chapter Three · A Man Who Could Draw

The pilot lived at the end of the one road still open past the fishing station. He had a triangular thumb, the tip sliced off on one side so he could hook a little shaving of wood into the rim of a bucket where a whole thumb would hardly fit. His wife sat on a stone with her boots in the sun while he worked. There had to be something wrong with the bucket. It had at least ten little shavings sticking in the same place.

Liv explained to the woman who she was. The woman reached a toe out and poked the old man's leg.

“Mundi. Lady to see you. If it's about the love affair you had in the south she can see you've come down in the world.”

“He's deaf?” Liv asked.

“He hears me. Sit over there where he can see your mouth.”

She sat. Mundi set a hand on the rim of the bucket.

“There's a woman from Eldøy whose husband went down with Arni. She went all the way up the steps at Brimstead with her little boy, and I couldn't show him where his father's name was. The only Ketil we've got there is the man called Blue.”

“There were two,” Mundi said.

She had been preparing for a long afternoon of going to the well with the bucket and trying to find the right moment at which to ask an injured old man to reopen his life for her. Hearing that answer almost immediately was like losing her footing. She sat back hard against the wall.

“Did you know the other one?”

“Sailed with him twice. He came aboard at the island, after the clerk finished his roll and went home. Cost more to carry a man who needed to get down the coast than there was in his purse, so he worked his passage. We'd have paid him in the south for the rest of the trip. He never got there.”

“Was he called something else in the boat?”

“Linnet. He had a chipped tooth, made the damnedest noise with it. We had men from Strandvik aboard who put great stock in keeping the wind sweet with the Sea-King. They wouldn't have a man whistle in the rigging. Pretty soon even those fellows could tell who'd got up to take the watch without turning over in their coats.”

“What was he like?” she said. This question had been available to her when Ragna first came to the lighthouse. She had sent the woman home before it ever occurred to her to ask it.

“Couldn't cut straight cheese. Could repair a rush seat so it kept you up off the floor another year. Liked picking the burnt skin off rice that came up in barrels from the south. The skipper used to save it for him. He was good company in the morning. My wife can tell you what I think of most people's company in the morning.”

Mundi set the bucket down and undid the high collar of his shirt. His lower jaw on the left was altogether smooth, the bone a little hollowed away under the skin. His teeth on that side were gone. “They kept my mouth tied shut while it set. My wife sat up a good many nights feeding me with a tube made out of a quill. I got a piece of paper from her. At first I could barely keep the charcoal between two fingers. The other men had their names on the list. I couldn't come up with Linnet's other name to save my own skin. I thought somebody who knew him would likely know what I meant.”

Liv had a clear view down the road to the sea. The tips of waves broke white against the landfast ice. A man came out of his house with a pail of something to throw on a garden too cold to grow food. Everything went on for some moments precisely as if she hadn't just found out what her hands had done.

“How big were the feet you gave it?” she said.

“Oh, terrible. Only bird I could aim at was the one on my wife's stocking clock, and if that thing could fly I'll eat the heel.” He was looking with interest at her face, awaiting a recollection he thought would please them both. “Have you still got it?”

“We had it. I showed it to my grandmother. She thought a child had drawn it and she laughed. So did I. She used the back for accounts. Years later, when we ran out of kindling, I burned those accounts in the stove.”

His wife had got her boots back on. She ran her palm along one side, feeling for a place where the seam gaped. Liv waited for one of them to say it was an understandable mistake. The pilot kept looking at the bucket. It seemed he had some defect at last that he couldn't quite get his hands on.

“I would have brought it myself,” he said, “if I'd known it would take anybody this long to ask.”

“His wife did ask. She came last summer.”

“Then what waited until now?”

“I did. She tried to tell me where he came aboard. I'd the whole ship's company counted up and a roomful of people waiting. I told her I'd finished.” Saying the words to this stranger made it painfully easy to remember her hand already on the door and the woman still getting up from the bench. “I need to tell her that.”

“Yes,” said the old man, and that was all she was going to get.

His wife had gone indoors. She came back with a good square of clean paper and an end of blue chalk. “Give our Ketil some proper feet this time,” she said. “You'll want to put your name on it if you're going to send it.”

“Would you come up and give it to the boy yourself?” Liv asked.

Mundi rubbed the tips of his fingers together. “Have they put in a lift on the stairs since I was there?”

“I'll get you a chair carried up the tower if you need it. His father will be in your debt.”

“Let his father alone. If the boy wants to hear some things to put his old man in a bad light I suppose I could come along on Saturday.” He picked up the blue chalk. It was plain to her he would sooner have stood naked in the market than tell her how many nights he'd lain awake trying to remember a shipmate's second name.

## Chapter Four · The Other Ketil

Ragna's sister kept a place down by the lower light where fresh milk used to be sold in the days when there were cows enough. Around the door somebody had planted bulbs well before the long winter came. Nobody had disturbed them yet in case it was the weather this year that had kept them so long. Liv stood upon the step with the old man's paper in her pocket. She had stopped on the hill and set aside in Frigg's name a little of her midday bread. She heard the ordinary sound of surf and a man thirty yards away calling on his dog to leave a rotten skate alone.

It was Ragna who came out. She took one look and shut the door behind her. “Has the boy got to hear this?”

“I wanted to say it to you first. I found the pilot. He drew a picture for us at the time because he couldn't speak. There was a bird in your husband's nickname. We missed him, Ragna. I turned you out, last summer. You'd come as far as you could come to have somebody listen to you. I got up and opened the door while you were still speaking. I've wished all the walk here that I could take back having done that to you. I'm sorry.”

Ragna looked past Liv toward the harbor. A woman washing somebody's best dishes had stacked the blue plates on the inside windowsill to let the light play through them. It was a small street like any other. Liv had not wanted an audience for what she had come here to say. At a window upstairs the boy was carefully closing the catch with his mother in plain view below. He had understood.

“They were people who knew me all my life,” Ragna said. “And when I went home the women wanted to know if I had found him, and I said you'd counted them all. Afterwards one of them asked my boy if he was sure his father had gone where his mother thought he went. He asked me about it when I was helping him into bed. He was old enough to think I might have told a lie. He was little enough to cry because he thought I'd tell another to make him feel better.”

“Do you want me to come to the island and tell them whose mistake it was?”

“Yes. Ask them yourself what they remember of him. There'll be some big stories by the time you get there, and all they ever had to do was mend a net beside him. It'll be good for me to hear a few.”

Liv gave her the drawing. The new feet were a little smaller in proportion to the bird. To make up for that, Mundi had given it buttocks like a prosperous chandler's. Ragna took one look and laughed. For all her gratitude to hear it, Liv was ashamed of the ease with which her shoulders went down at the sound.

“Don't you take that as settling everything,” Ragna said. “You can still come to the island.”

“I will. There's somebody else we ought to see before I get the loom set out.”

Blue Ketil's daughter lived in the town. Ragna had walked her to school as a very small girl. Now she worked in the oil shed and had the most alarming way of looking at a person between her father's fair eyebrows, grown into a face mostly her mother's. The space above the names in her father's wave had been worked twice over already. Liv explained that she could lift the whole piece out and open it up to make room for another name. The work would take a month, possibly six weeks as the light went.

“And where would you put him while his piece was out?” the girl asked.

“On a frame in the workroom.”

“My little brother comes by on his way back from feeding the hens. Could you leave the workroom unlocked?”

Liv sat down on an oil case. She could think of any number of places to put iron under a window or a bar outside a door. In the end it was Sten, who had come down without anybody noticing, who said, “Isn't there room in the next wave?”

“There is,” said Liv slowly.

“Blue could stay where his little boy can find him, then. My da wouldn't have gone on the whole voyage without walking up and down a bit.”

On their way back he let Liv hear his father's whistle. There was a gap where a note ought to have been. To a stranger it was the noise a kettle made with the lid badly on. Heard once, there could be no mistaking it. His mother came round the side of the house at the sound, looking for a moment as young as the girl who had walked Blue Ketil's daughter to school.

## Chapter Five · In the Next Wave

Sten brought the last bit of red wool undone from his father's cap. He had got it free himself although Ragna had told him time and again he could cut it. The end curled up tight, still remembering the turns it had taken round a needle. Liv soaked it in a spare teacup to see what the dye would do. It bled a disreputable pink.

“I won't be mad if it runs,” Sten said.

“Come here and turn the cloth over. See all the things sewn on where nobody can see them? My grandmother put a lock of my grandfather's hair in behind his name. Somebody put a piece of a fishing fly behind a pilot's. Here's a shirt button with a green thread still on it. Do you think we could find a bit of space for your red wool?”

“Will you be able to feel it from the front?”

They tried. Inside a square of linen the piece of cap made a little raised tick under the name Liv had worked in good madder red that had been washed until the water came away clear. Sten could feel it. Ragna put the flat of her hand on the letters and closed her eyes. The boy drew the last thread tight. After some consultation he had the little bird sewn beside the name, feet and all.

It took four men, a house door and a length of good rope to get Mundi all the way up to a room plenty of people in their prime came into panting. Halfway up they heard the alarmed noises he made on the first turn, where the slit in the wall gave a man a good long view of the surf at the bottom of the cliffs. They heard him laugh after that. Ragna went down to spell one of the carriers. Liv kept a hand on the open door. She thought of her grandmother's cup, and the little patch of warm lamplight, and how willingly she would have sat late again and done all the woman's close work for her with her own good eyes.

Sten whistled his father in at the door. The old man, half lifted and half walking on his wife's arm, stopped and answered him. The gap in the tune fell in exactly the same place.

“What's it mean?” the boy asked when Mundi had got onto a bench and been allowed one swallow of beer.

“On your boat it might have meant he was home for his tea. The first time I ever heard it we were a long way south and there was a pig in the captain's bed.”

“Was he a big pig?”

“Fine spotted gilt. Lady pig. She'd come aboard to be delivered to somebody's brother. We'd lost two days' sailing to weather and there was a letter for the brother to say the pig was due to have a family. A wicker crate is drafty when you're little and new in the world. Your da looked into the captain's berth while the captain was at supper and he said to me, 'Mundi, look at that good clean straw ticking, with a fella away eating off silver when he could be laying in it.' Then he whistled the way you did just now. The pig got up and followed him in like a fellow officer.”

“Did he get in trouble?”

“Well, the captain came down about an hour later. He lifted a corner of the blanket. There she lay with five beautiful little spotted pigs laid out beside her and your da sitting on the edge of the bed pulling his stockings back on.”

“Why'd he take them off?”

“He'd put one runt in each stocking while the old sow was getting settled. So he tells the captain the total is seven. He holds the two little runts up, a head in each hand, quite square with the captain's face. And the skipper says, 'Ketil, heaven knows I've had occasion to put many things down against your wages, but how in all the green world am I to charge a pig for a berth?'”

“What did Da say?”

“Your father reached in his pocket and came up with a worn silver bit. He put it on the pillow. 'One fare for the mother,' he told him. 'The little ones is mine. Mine go free.'”
