# Foreword

A baker on the quay at Hrafnborg used to know how the barley harvest had gone by the price of a sack. Grain came from valleys on the far side of the mountains. Ships carried the surplus south, and a family with a few good harvests behind it might own a piece of silver worked in a port none of them had ever seen. Harald, the king who helped open the roads to that trade, still rules in Hrafnborg. From the same quay he can see the ice closing the harbor.

The change began on the longest night of the year. Across Svellheim the moon turned red and disappeared. For three days people struck flint against steel and watched the sparks die in dry tinder. Even the fire that rises from bare rock above Hrafnborg went cold. Then a spark caught again. People cooked what they had left and waited for the thaw. The earth has stayed frozen through three planting seasons. This is Fimbulwinter. Priests, farmers and the poets who keep the old histories in song offer different reasons for it. They agree on the night it began.

Uphill from the harbor a traveler can lay wet clothes on a warm shelf of rock. Fire wells up through a cleft there. The first people to build houses on the shore could see its light from their boats. The people call the place a flame spring and its fire an Ember. An Ember Keeper cares for the spring and keeps a place by it open to strangers. Out along the roads, Ember Tenders travel between shelters where smaller Embers burn. A dry bench there can mean a night’s rest for someone still a day’s walk from home.

Most written histories begin when Yggdrasil, the World Tree, put down roots. For the time before that, families still tell their own accounts of where they came from. This book follows their peoples through two wars and the rebuilding that brought them into shared towns. When the history reaches Harald’s reign and the onset of winter, the atlas takes up the country as it stands today, from the icebound ports to the passes where his grain wagons once crossed.
